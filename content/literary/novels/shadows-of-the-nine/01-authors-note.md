eyebrow: Shadows of the Nine
description: Author's Note for Shadows of the Nine, a mythological fantasy novel.
---

I did not grow up thinking of stories as myths. I grew up thinking of them as my company.

Long before I understood what many words even meant, I knew the shape of these tales the way you know a familiar room in the dark — by feel, by the sound of a father's or a grandmother's voice slowing down at the parts they loved best, by the particular hush that fell over a room when a story reached the part everyone already knew was coming and waited for anyway. I did not read or listen to them so much as I was handed them, the way you're handed something fragile and told to be careful, and only later understood how much care had already been spent keeping them whole long enough to reach me.

I am writing this because I am afraid of a quieter kind of forgetting — not the dramatic kind, where a story is banned or burned, but the ordinary kind, where a heritage simply stops being anyone's first choice. Where a nine-year-old today has a hundred other worlds arriving faster, better lit, more insistently marketed, and the old stories wait patiently on a shelf that gets walked past a little more often each year. I don't think our mythology lost to a competition. I think it just needs a door built for the generation standing outside to walk right into it now.

So this is my door. Not a lecture on heritage, not a sermon on faith — just a story, the kind I fell in love with as a child and have never quite stopped falling in love with ever since. If a reader closes this book and goes looking, on their own, for the older stories these characters echo — the itihasa, the Puranas, the epics that raised me — then this book would have done more than entertain. It would have done what my childhood folklores did to me: it would have handed something forward.

That is the only ambition I have ever really had for this particular series. Everything else — the plot, the politics — is just the shape I built to carry it in.
