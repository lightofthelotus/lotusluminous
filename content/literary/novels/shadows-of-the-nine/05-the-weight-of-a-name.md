## I

Charudatta had a particular way of raising a subject he had already decided the outcome of, which involved never quite raising it directly.

"The southern scholar has been given generous latitude," he said, three mornings after the treasury, addressing Anantaraja in the unhurried tone of a man discussing the weather rather than a man laying groundwork. "The archive, unsupervised hours, and — if the stewards' gossip is to be trusted — a great deal of the princess's own time besides." He did not look at Vaanathi as he said it, which she understood, with the particular cold clarity court had taught her, to be entirely deliberate. A man who wished to needle you looked away as he did it, so that you could not object to a glance that had never technically landed on you.

"The princess oversees the work at my instruction," Anantaraja said, without heat, without hurry, in the manner of a man closing a door so gently the hinges made no sound at all. "If you have a concern about the archive's cataloguing, Minister, I would hear it plainly, rather than hear it dressed as concern for my daughter's time."

"I have no concern about ledgers, Your Grace." Charudatta inclined his head, the very picture of a man conceding gracefully, which fooled precisely no one in the room. "Only a minister's ordinary caution, that a stranger newly arrived on the same night as a death should not find himself quite so thoroughly woven into the household's confidence quite so quickly. Court talks, Your Grace. It talked before I was old enough to understand what it was saying, and it will talk long after I am dust. I would rather it talk about embankments than about the princess."

It was, Vaanathi thought, watching her father's face for the small flicker of expression he permitted himself in private counsel and never in open court, a remarkably efficient piece of cruelty — worded so carefully that no single sentence of it could be called an accusation, and yet the whole of it landed as one anyway, the way a net closes without any single knot doing the strangling.

"Then let them talk about embankments," Anantaraja said. "I did not ask your opinion of the princess's virtue, Charudatta. I asked whether the ledgers were being properly kept. Are they?"

"I have not inspected them personally, Your Grace."

"Then you have nothing further to report." Her father's tone did not rise. It did not need to. "You are dismissed."

Charudatta bowed — a fraction too shallow to be true deference, a fraction too deep to be called insolence, the precise calibration of a man who had spent thirty years learning exactly how far a bow could be tilted before it became punishable — and withdrew, and Vaanathi did not fail to notice that on his way out he permitted himself, at last, one single glance in her direction. Not hostile. Something closer to appraisal. The look of a man filing information away for later use, the same way she herself had learned to file things.

"He is not wrong," she said, once the doors had closed, "that court is talking."

"Court is always talking," her father said. "The question that concerns me is never whether they talk. It is only ever whether they are correct." He studied her for a moment with the same grave attention he had given her three days prior, in the shade at the courtyard's edge. "Are they correct, Vaanathi?"

She thought of a hand not quite touching hers over a high shelf. She thought of *I have buried a great many friends*, offered without being asked, in a voice that had cost something to use.

"They are correct that I spend a great deal of time in that archive," she said, which was true, and answered nothing, and she watched her father recognise the answer for exactly the evasion it was, and choose, with the same deliberate mercy he had shown her all her life, not to press it.

"Then finish the work quickly," was all he said. "For your own sake, if not for Charudatta's peace of mind."

***

She found Nila in the western gallery an hour later, and did not need to say a word before Nila set down the manuscript she'd been pretending to read and gave her the particular assessing look reserved for occasions requiring immediate and undivided attention.

"Charudatta," Nila said. It was not a question.

"Charudatta," Vaanathi agreed, dropping into the seat across from her with rather less grace than the gallery's cushions were designed to accommodate. "Delivered with such exquisite care that I could not object to a single word of it, and yet I have spent the better part of an hour wanting to throw something at his retreating back regardless."

"What did he actually say?"

"Nothing, if you ask him to repeat it. Everything, if you were in the room." Vaanathi pressed the heels of her hands briefly against her eyes. "He as good as told my father I have compromised myself with a stranger, in language so careful that my father could not even properly reprimand him for saying it."

"And has he said anything," Nila asked, with the particular gentleness that meant she already suspected the answer and wanted the courtesy of hearing it confirmed rather than assumed, "that isn't, in some narrow and infuriating way, true?"

Vaanathi did not answer that immediately, which she supposed was answer enough.

"The court will always find something to say about a princess who spends her afternoons anywhere a minister has not personally approved," she said instead. "I refuse to organise my life around what Charudatta finds convenient to whisper."

"That was not what I asked."

"I know precisely what you asked." Vaanathi rose, restless, and crossed to the gallery's tall window, looking out over gardens gone violet with the evening. "I do not have an answer for you yet, Nila. I am not certain I want one before I am ready to hear it myself."

Nila let that sit a moment, watching her with the patient, unhurried attention that had carried them both through worse evenings than this one. "Then I will not press," she said, "except to say this — whatever it is you are not yet ready to hear, I would rather you heard it from yourself, in your own time, than have Charudatta hand it to you dressed as scandal."

It was, Vaanathi thought, exactly the kind of true and unhelpful thing Nila had always had a gift for saying at precisely the moment it could least be avoided.

## II

Iruli found the letters before Sancharan did, which she considered, on the whole, an entirely unsurprising outcome, given that she had spent the better part of two nights doing the actual work while he sat cross-legged on an archive floor being charmed by a princess's laugh.

She did not put it to him quite that way. She was fond of him, in the manner one is fond of something one has watched make the same mistake across several centuries and has long since stopped expecting to learn otherwise, but fondness did not require gentleness, and gentleness, in her experience, rarely improved him.

She had begun, two nights prior, with nothing more than an old habit of thoroughness — a suspicion, born of long practice, that a man as careful as Smaran would not have kept everything he knew in a room the whole palace had access to. She had searched the obvious places first, as one always did, and found nothing: his official chambers held only what an honest scholar's chambers ought to hold, tidy and unremarkable and entirely without secrets. It was the servants' gossip, overheard in passing near the scribes' hall, that gave her the true thread — an old woman complaining that the narrow room above the hall had stood locked and unused for years, ever since the archive-keeper had asked for its key and never once been seen using it for anything a steward could account for.

*Smaran kept a second set of quarters,* she told Sancharan, on the third night, having led him without explanation through the palace's servant passages to that same narrow, disused room above the scribes' hall that no roster accounted for and no steward had cause to visit. *Not his official chambers. This one. He never slept here. He worked here, alone, at hours no honest scholar keeps.*

The room held a single low writing table, a shuttered window, and, beneath a loose floor plank that gave without much persuasion, a small bundle of palm-leaf sheets bound in cord gone soft with handling — not part of any official archive, not catalogued, not written in the careful formal hand Smaran had used for thirty years of royal record. This hand was smaller, faster, urgent in a way that thirty years of dynastic ledgers had never once been urgent.

Sancharan read by the thin light of a single shielded lamp, and felt, with each leaf, the particular cold settle that he associated — across every name he had ever worn, in every court he had ever stood in — with watching a private fear confirm itself.

*The mark returns,* Smaran had written, in ink gone brown with age less than a year old, *though the court would have it that I imagine things. I do not imagine the treasury bell. I do not imagine the seam in the genealogical volume that no honest binding produces on its own. Something old is being searched for in this house, and I do not believe the search began with me, only that it will very likely end with me, if I am not careful about who I ask.*

Further on, in a hand grown hastier, less controlled: *I have found reference, in the border correspondence no one reads but border lords and the occasional dull scholar, to an accord older than this dynasty — nine anchors, the old text calls them, though it will not say anchors of what, only that each was entrusted to keep something in balance that mortal hands were never meant to hold outright. I do not know which anchor this mark belongs to. I fear I am closer to finding out than is good for me.*

The last leaf was shorter than the rest, the hand shakier still, as though written in haste, or in the particular fear that comes only once a man has stopped believing he is imagining anything at all.

*If anything happens to me before I have found the answer, look to the old sky-tower beyond the northern wall — the one the court calls ruined and the astronomers stopped climbing two generations ago. I do not believe it was ever truly abandoned. I believe it was only ever left to look that way.*

Sancharan sat a long while in the shuttered dark, the bundle of leaves loose in his hands, and did not, for once, need Iruli to tell him what he was thinking, because she was thinking it too, in the particular silent unison two old companions sometimes fell into without needing the words.

*He knew,* Sancharan said, finally. *He knew, and he told no one, and it killed him for the knowing.*

*He told someone,* Iruli said, quietly, for her. *He simply did not live to say it aloud. Now it falls to you to finish what he started asking.*

## III

Vaanathi did not weep when he showed her the letters. She had expected, watching her own hands hold Smaran's last hurried handwriting, that she might — had braced for it the way she braced for most things now, quietly, privately, so that no one else would have to manage the sight of it — but what came instead was something harder and more useful, a kind of clean, cold anger that settled into her chest and stayed there.

"He was frightened," she said, "and he told no one. Not my father. Not me." Her voice did not shake, though she felt, distantly, that it had earned the right to. "I sat in that archive a hundred afternoons of my childhood and he never once let me see that he was afraid of anything."

"He was protecting you," Sancharan said, "the only way he knew how to. By making certain you never had reason to be afraid of the same thing."

"That is a poor kind of protection, if it ends with him dead and me finding out from a stranger's hidden room."

"It is the kind most people choose anyway," Sancharan said, quietly, "when they have decided the fear is theirs to carry and no one else's." Something in the way he said it made her look at him properly — not the careful, guarded scholar's face he wore for the rest of the court, but something rawer beneath it, briefly unguarded, gone again almost as soon as she'd caught it. "I have known a great many people who made that same choice. It rarely ends well for them. It is, nonetheless, remarkably difficult to argue a person out of, once they have decided the weight is theirs alone to bear."

"You say that," Vaanathi said slowly, "like a man with some experience in the matter."

"I say most things like that," Sancharan said, and did not deny it, and did not confirm it either, and she let the silence sit between them rather than press it, because she had learned, over these strange few days, that his silences carried more honesty than most men's speeches, if she was patient enough to let them finish.

She read the letters a second time, more slowly, and found herself lingering on a line she had passed over too quickly the first time — *I do not believe the search began with me, only that it will very likely end with me, if I am not careful about who I ask* — and something in the phrasing snagged at her.

"He suspected someone," she said. "Not a stranger. Someone he knew well enough to be careful of asking."

Sancharan went very still. "What makes you say so?"

"Because that is not the sentence of a man wary of the world in general. That is the sentence of a man wary of a particular room, and particular faces in it." She looked up at him. "Smaran spent thirty years in this palace. If he feared asking the wrong question of the wrong person, it was someone close enough to the crown to matter. A minister. A steward. Someone with standing enough that even a frightened old archivist would think twice before naming him aloud, even to paper."

"That is a narrower list than you might hope," Sancharan said slowly, "and a more dangerous one to go asking after directly."

"I am aware." Vaanathi set the letters down with more care than her anger wanted to allow. "Which is precisely why I do not intend to ask directly. Not yet."

She looked back down at the last leaf, at *the old sky-tower beyond the northern wall*, and felt something in her chest tighten with a purpose that was, for the first time in a long week, cleaner than grief.

"Then we go there," she said. "Tonight, if it can be managed quietly. My father will not sanction an expedition to a ruin on the strength of a dead man's hidden notes, and Charudatta would turn it into a scandal before we'd cleared the palace gate."

"Your father asked you to tell him what you found."

"My father asked me to tell *him*," Vaanathi said, "and only him. He did not specify when." She met Sancharan's eyes, and found, not for the first time, that she did not entirely mind what she found there. "I intend to have something worth telling him, scholar, before I tell him anything at all."

Something that might almost have been admiration crossed Sancharan's face again, the same quiet flicker she had caught once before over a genealogical ledger, and this time he did not trouble himself to hide it.

"Then I imagine," he said, "that I had better come with you."

## IV

They left separately and met at the old stable gate an hour past the changing of the night watch, which Vaanathi had timed with the same precision she brought to court, and Sancharan had matched without needing to be told the schedule, which she noted, and did not yet know what to make of.

The northern wall's shadow fell long and cool under a sky just beginning to prickle with early stars, and they walked the disused path in a silence that was not, for once, the wary silence of two people bracing against each other. Iruli flew ahead in low, easy circles, more scout than companion tonight, her dark shape cutting against the darker shape of the wall.

"You did not ask why I was so precisely on time," Sancharan observed, after a while, "which I confess surprises me. You strike me as a woman who notices everything and comments on most of it."

"I assumed you had reasons of your own for punctuality, and that they were none of my concern."

"A generous assumption."

"An efficient one," Vaanathi said. "I find I have very little patience left this week for questions I do not yet need the answers to. There are enough of those already waiting for me at the top of that tower."

"Charudatta thinks I am compromising you," she said, into the quiet, because it had been sitting in her chest since morning and she found she wanted it said aloud rather than carried silently, the way Smaran had carried his fear silently, to no good end. "Or that you are compromising me. He was careful not to say which."

"And what do you think?"

"I think," she said, "that a week ago I would have agreed with him without needing to be asked twice." She kept her eyes on the path ahead, which made the next part easier to say. "I do not agree with him now, and I find I am considerably more troubled by that than I am by anything Charudatta actually said."

Sancharan was quiet for several paces. When he spoke, his voice had lost the careful lightness he generally wore like armour. "For what it is worth — and I am aware it may not be worth a great deal, given how little of myself I have actually given you to judge — I have not lied to you about anything that matters. I have withheld. There is a difference, though I understand it is a difference that asks a great deal of your trust to credit."

"You withheld the mark's meaning for three days."

"I withheld it because I did not yet know it, and because a guess dressed as certainty would have served you worse than silence." He glanced at her, and in the thin starlight his expression was, for once, entirely readable. "I will tell you plainly what I can, when I can. That is the only promise I am presently able to make you, and I make it knowing it is not enough."

"It is more than Charudatta thinks I deserve," Vaanathi said, "and rather more, I confess, than I expected from a man who greeted me by insulting a dead friend."

"I have been trying," Sancharan said, with the ghost of something that was almost a smile, "to improve upon that first impression ever since."

"You have made some progress."

"Only some?"

"I have not yet decided how much," Vaanathi said, "which I would ask you to take, scholar, as considerably more generous than it sounds."

He did smile then — briefly, unguarded, gone almost as soon as it arrived, but she had seen it, and something in her chest did something complicated and unwelcome and entirely against her better judgement, and she found, walking beside him toward a ruin neither of them yet understood, that she did not particularly want to argue herself out of it tonight.

## V

The sky-tower stood where the northern wall's shadow gave way to open scrubland, taller than Vaanathi had expected from the court's dismissive talk of it — three storeys of pale stone, its upper platform open to the sky, the stair within worn smooth by generations of feet that the palace's official memory had entirely forgotten climbing it. Common creeper ivies had claimed the lower windows. The great door hung on one hinge, and had, Vaanathi noted, been opened recently enough that the drag-mark through the dust told its own small honest story before either of them had said a word.

"Someone has been here," she said.

"Someone has been here often," Sancharan corrected, crouching at the threshold, studying the marks with the same careful attention he had given the treasury bell. "This is not the trail of a single visit. Whoever comes here comes and goes with some regularity, and takes some care about the coming and going." He looked up at her. "Smaran believed this tower was not truly abandoned. I think he was right, and I think whoever kept it from being abandoned would very much prefer that fact remain unknown."

They climbed together, Iruli gliding ahead through the tower's broken upper windows, calling back small warnings — a loose stone here, a rotted stair-tread there — in the low private register only Sancharan could properly understand, though Vaanathi found she had begun, without quite noticing when, to read the crow's moods well enough regardless.

The lower storeys held little beyond dust and the husks of old instruments too broken or too heavy to have been worth stealing — a cracked water-clock, its basin long dry; a set of graduated rings, corroded past any use, still hanging from a beam by rusted chain. Vaanathi paused at each in turn, unable to help herself, tracing the old astronomers' careful markings with something between reverence and grief. Whoever had once climbed this stair to chart the turning sky had done so, she suspected, with the same patient devotion Smaran had brought to his ledgers — a whole life given over to watching something larger than themselves, in a room the rest of the world had long since stopped believing mattered.

"They knew something," she said quietly, as they climbed past the second landing. "Whoever worked here. Not superstition. Something they measured, and wrote down, and were believed, once, for knowing."

"Most true things begin that way," Sancharan said. "Measured, and written down, and believed. It is only afterward, once the measuring stops and only the fear remains, that they curdle into omens."

The tower's upper chamber had once been an observatory, that much was still legible in the bones of the place — a stone table at its centre scored with old sighting-lines, half-effaced by weather; niches in the wall that must once have held instruments long since removed or stolen. Vaanathi trailed her fingers along the sighting-table's worn grooves, trying to imagine the astronomers who had once stood here charting a sky that had, in some sense she was only beginning to understand, been charting them back. And at the chamber's far side, cut into the stone itself where the old astronomers' sighting-table met the wall, half-hidden beneath a fall of creeping vine that someone had clearly, repeatedly, cleared away and let grow back as camouflage—

A narrow ring. Broken, at one point along its circumference, by a thin dark wedge.

Larger here than either of the others. Deliberate, unhurried, cut by a hand that had known exactly what it was making and had not needed to hide the making of it, the way a door's wax seal or a bell's collar had hidden theirs. This one had been carved to be found by someone who already knew to look, and to be overlooked by everyone else.

Vaanathi stood before it a long moment, lamp raised, feeling the same cold settle along her spine that had found her twice now already this week, deepened this time by something that was not quite fear and not quite excitement and sat somewhere uncomfortably between the two. Iruli landed on the sighting-table's edge and went still, wings folded, watching the mark with an attention that carried none of her usual restlessness.

"Three marks," Vaanathi said quietly. "A door. A bell. Now a tower no one admits still stands. Whatever this is, scholar, it is not confined to one dead man's grief, and it is not confined to my father's house."

"No," Sancharan agreed, studying the carved ring with an expression she could not entirely read — something between recognition and dread, old enough that she suspected, not for the first time, that whatever he was not telling her went back rather further than a single lifetime could easily account for. "I do not believe it is confined to very much at all."

He reached out, almost without meaning to, and let his fingers rest just beside the carved mark — not touching it, the way he had not touched her hand over the high shelf, the same careful, deliberate distance — and Vaanathi, watching him, understood with sudden and unwelcome clarity that whatever this mark meant to him, it was not merely scholarship. It was something closer to memory. Something closer to grief.

"Sancharan," she said, quietly, using his name for the first time without the armour of *scholar* wrapped around it. "What is this to you?"

He did not answer at once. Below them, through the tower's broken window, Iruli went very still on her perch, watching him with an attention that was, for once, entirely without mischief.

"A very old question," Sancharan said finally, "that I had hoped, when I came to this city, I would not be asked to answer quite so soon."

It was not an answer. Vaanathi understood, standing in the ruined observatory with a stranger's fingers hovering beside a mark older than the throne her family sat, that it was the truest thing he had said to her yet.

They stood a while longer in the observatory's broken quiet, the mark between them like a third presence neither of them was quite ready to name, and when they finally descended the worn stair and started back toward the palace's sleeping walls, Vaanathi found she did not ask him again. Not because she had stopped wanting the answer, but because some part of her — the same part that had long since learned exactly what a closed mouth could say — understood that this was not a truth to be taken by force. It was a door, and doors of this kind, she was beginning to suspect, only ever opened from the inside.

## VI

Viyamban climbed the sky-tower's stair three nights later, alone, as he had climbed it more times than he had troubled himself to count across the five decades he had been doing so, and knew before he had reached the second landing that something in his tower had changed.

It was nothing so obvious as a footprint, though there was one of those as well, half-erased in the dust near the threshold. It was the vine. He had trained it himself, patiently, season after season, to grow precisely thick enough across the sighting-chamber's far wall to hide what lay beneath it from any eye that did not already know to look — and vines, like most living things a man tended with sufficient care, learned to grow the way they were guided. This one had been disturbed. Pulled aside and allowed to fall back, not quite in its old pattern. A careless hand undoing a careful man's work in a single evening.

He stood before the mark a long while, lamp in hand, feeling something colder than the night settle into his chest.

*Someone has been here.*

He had known, of course, that it would come to this eventually — had known it since the day the archivist first began asking careful, oblique questions about border correspondence three centuries old, questions that circled the truth the way a hawk circles before it commits to the strike. He had hoped for more time regardless. Hope, he had learned across a lifetime of watching his family's name erased a little further from the record with each passing generation, was a luxury the patient could rarely afford and never quite managed to stop indulging in anyway.

He thought of his father, old and diminished and still climbing this same stair on failing knees long after the court had stripped the office from him — still charting a sky the throne had officially decided was no longer worth the funding to observe, because a man who has spent his whole life reading the heavens does not simply stop reading them because the men below have grown too proud to listen. He thought of the ledger his grandfather's grandfather had once kept, before it was smoothed out of the dynasty's memory entirely, that recorded a name Viyamban's own family had once borne with honour, in the years before that honour was quietly, bureaucratically erased by men who found an inconvenient older claim easier to forget than to answer.

*The nine do not forget,* his father had told him, the last winter of his life, voice gone thin as old cloth. *Time keeps its own accounts, whatever the throne chooses to write in its own. Ours is not a lineage of vengeance, my son. Ours is a lineage of patience. Only remember: patience that outlives its purpose is merely another kind of forgetting. Do not let our name die twice.*

Viyamban had buried his father believing he understood those words. It had taken him another twenty years, and the slow accumulation of every small humiliation a dismissed office collects, to understand that patience, unused, was no virtue at all — only a longer, quieter kind of surrender, and sometimes, he was only now beginning to admit to himself, a kind of torture.

The archivist had come too close, and so the archivist had stopped coming anywhere at all. That had bought him time. It had not, he now understood, bought him enough.

He knelt before the mark and drew from beneath his robe the small bundle he had carried up this stair a hundred times before and never yet had cause to use in full: an old palm-leaf, brittle with age, covered in a hand three centuries older than his own, listing in patient, meticulous detail the location of eight seals his ancestors had once been sworn to help protect — eight, not nine, because the ninth, the one whose ring this chamber's wall had worn into stone since before the dynasty existed, had never needed listing. It had never left this tower. Not truly. Only its meaning had been buried, the way his family's name had been buried, beneath centuries of a throne's convenient forgetting.

*Two visitors,* he thought, studying the disturbed vine, the half-erased print, with the same patient precision he had once used to chart the slow drift of stars. *A scholar who asks too many careful questions, and a princess who has inherited her father's stubbornness without yet inheriting his caution.*

He had not intended to move so soon. He had intended — as he had intended for so many careful, patient years — to wait for the correct hour, the correct alignment, the moment his father's old teachings named as the only safe threshold for what came next. But a threshold delayed too long, he was beginning to understand with a clarity that felt almost like relief, was simply another kind of erasure, and he had buried enough of those already.

He would need to know how much the scholar had already guessed. He would need to know whether the princess had told her father, or whether — as he suspected, given how carefully the two of them had climbed his stair without so much as a single guard in attendance — she was still keeping this secret entirely her own, the same foolish, admirable way her father had once kept secrets at her age, before a throne taught him better.

And he would need, before the month turned, to visit the archive one final time — not for the ledger, whose secrets he had already taken, but for the under-steward who had helped break down Smaran's door, and who had stood close enough to the writing table that night to have seen, however briefly, whatever the old man had been reading in his final hour.

Viyamban drew the vine back into its careful, deliberate pattern, extinguished his lamp, and began the long climb down, already composing, in the unhurried way of a man who had spent a lifetime learning patience and was only now beginning to learn its limits, precisely what an old, dismissible, harmless retired astronomer might say to a frightened young steward to make certain he never spoke of that night to anyone again.
