## I

The reference had been sitting in Smaran's own hand for three bunches of manuscripts before either of them thought to chase it properly — a single line, buried in a margin annotation beside a border treaty neither of them had judged worth a second look, mentioning a tank the old maps called the Amudha Kulam and the border lords, more practically, called nothing at all, because no one had drawn water from it in living memory.

"He circled it," Vaanathi said, crouched over the leaf by lamplight, tracing the small, decisive mark Smaran had made beside the name. "Twice. He did that when something troubled him enough to want to return to it and never quite found the hour."

"Then we will find the hour for him," Sancharan said, and there was something steadier in his voice than she was used to hearing when the subject was Smaran — not less grief, she thought, but a grief that had found somewhere useful to stand.

They left before the palacehold properly woke, on the thin pretext of a border survey Vaanathi had invented with rather more ease than she was entirely comfortable admitting to herself. Nila had not asked questions when she saw the riding clothes laid out; she had only looked at Vaanathi a long moment, the particular searching look she used when she suspected a thing but had decided, for once, not to make her friend say it aloud before she was ready.

The Amudha Kulam sat half a day's ride beyond the northern wall, further out than the sky-tower, in scrubland the empire's maps marked only as *unclaimed*. The ride passed easily enough, the early light gentling the scrubland's harsher edges into something almost soft, and Vaanathi found herself, more than once, aware of Sancharan's eyes on her back rather than the road ahead — the particular unhurried attention of a man who trusted his horse to know its own way and no longer troubled himself to pretend otherwise. She turned once, catching him at it, and watched, with some satisfaction, the colour rise faintly along his throat before he looked deliberately back to the path.

"You are quiet today," he observed, at the last low rise before the light began its slow slide toward evening.

"I am thinking," Vaanathi said, "about how strange it is that a man who tells me almost nothing has become the person I trust most readily with the things that frighten me."

Sancharan did not answer that immediately, and when he did, his voice had gone low in a way that made her chest tighten pleasantly, unhelpfully, entirely against the sensible schedule she had meant to keep her feelings on this week. "I have not decided," he said, "whether that is a compliment or an indictment. I find I would like it to be the former rather badly."

What they found, cresting the last rise before the light failed entirely, was a stepwell — a vast square throat cut into the earth, tier upon tier of pale stone descending in a slow spiral toward a floor of black water so still it held the first stars without a single tremor.

"No one built this to be forgotten," Vaanathi said, looking down into it. "Look at the stonework. This was not some border village's water tank. This was made by people who understood exactly what they were making."

"Yes," Sancharan agreed, and did not elaborate, and she had grown accustomed enough to that particular silence, these past days, to let it stand without pressing.

Iruli went ahead of them down the first tier, wings folded tight against the narrow stone, calling back the ordinary small warnings — a loose step here, a crumbling ledge there — with nothing in her voice to suggest this descent was different from any other they had made together. Vaanathi followed two steps behind Sancharan, one hand trailing the cool stone wall, counting tiers the way she had once counted the sky-tower's landings, and did not yet understand that she was walking toward the last ordinary hour she would have for some while.

***

They found the mark on the ninth tier down — a narrow ring broken by a thin dark wedge, cut into a single flat threshold stone set crosswise in the stair, worn nearly smooth by centuries of water that no longer reached this high.

Sancharan crouched over it, tracing the old carving with two fingers, his attention narrowed the way it narrowed whenever the marks demanded something of him that he was not yet ready to say aloud.

"This one is different," he said slowly. "The others were dormant. Old, but settled — a door long since closed and forgotten. This one is not settled."

"What does that mean?"

"I am not entirely certain," he said, which she was learning to recognise as the particular honesty of a man who genuinely did not know, rather than the practised evasion he wore for questions he understood perfectly well and simply would not answer. "Something has disturbed this place recently. Recently enough that whatever guards it has not yet gone back to sleep."

Vaanathi stepped past him to look more closely at the stone — and it was only a step, an ordinary step of the kind she had taken a thousand times on a thousand staircases, her weight coming down at the threshold's very edge where the old carved ring met the tier's lip.

The world did not so much strike her as *remember* her — a cold that had nothing to do with the evening air, arriving all at once in every part of her body simultaneously, as though something ancient and precise had been waiting a very long time to recognise exactly this shape of trespass and had, at last, been given the chance. She heard Sancharan's voice say her name from very far away, though she knew, distantly, that he was standing less than an arm's length from her. She heard Iruli's cry, sharp and wordless, cutting through the deepening dark.

And then the black water below them was rising up to meet her, or she was falling down to meet it, and she could no longer tell which, or whether either mattered any longer at all.

## II

Sancharan caught her before the fourth tier, which was not fast enough, and he knew it was not fast enough in the same instant his arms closed around her, because whatever had taken hold of her was not the kind of harm that stopped simply because the falling had.

Her skin had gone cold in a way no living body cooled on its own. Her pulse, beneath his hand at her throat, came slow and growing slower, an unhurried, terrible arithmetic he had watched conducted in exactly this fashion more times across more centuries than he allowed himself to count on ordinary days. Not poison. Not wound. Something older and colder than either — an old warding-law reacting to a mortal trespass the way law reacts to anything: without malice, without mercy, entirely without interest in the particular person currently dying beneath its judgment.

*She has perhaps an hour,* Iruli said, landing hard on the stair beside him, her voice stripped of every ordinary edge of wit it usually carried. *Perhaps less. This is old work, Sancharan. Older than the tower. I do not know a mortal physician alive who would recognise it, let alone treat it.*

"Then I will not take her to a mortal physician."

*You are not thinking clearly.*

"I am thinking with perfect clarity," he said, and the calm in his own voice frightened him rather more than the cold spreading through the girl in his arms. "There is no time left to be anything else."

He had not opened that particular door in longer than he cared to remember — had told himself, across the ordinary business of an ordinary week in an ordinary court, that he had no cause to, that the fortress beyond it, the one he still privately called Anaadi, in a tongue no court in this age still spoke, belonged to a version of himself that a scholar's borrowed name had no need to touch. He touched it now. His lips shaped words older than any language Anantapura's scribes had ever set to a leaf, low and unhurried, while two fingers traced a single mark into the air before him — a narrow ring, broken by a thin dark wedge, the same shape that had haunted a dead man's ledger and a dead bell's collar, drawn now not as a warning left behind but as a key turned forward. The air at the stepwell's floor tore itself open along the seam his fingers had traced, a seam that had no business existing in any mortal geometry, and beyond it there was no black water and no ancient stone.

There was only the vast, patient dark of a place that had never once, in all its existence, needed to hurry.

He carried her through before he allowed himself to consider, even once, what it would mean that she had seen him do it.

## III

Vaanathi woke to a sky that was wrong.

She understood this before she understood anything else, before the cold in her limbs had finished retreating, before she had even fully remembered there ought to be a *before* to return to. The sky above her was not black in the way night skies were black. It held colour the way deep water held colour, moving slowly, vast folds of something between violet and a colour she had no name for at all, and scattered through it, not scattered the way stars were scattered but arranged, deliberate, patient, hung a multitude of lights too still and too knowing to be anything so ordinary as stars.

She was lying on a floor of stone that was not stone — cool, seamless, faintly luminous beneath her, as though it remembered light rather than reflected it. Around her rose towers she could not make her eyes hold steady, architecture that seemed to continue existing in directions her mind kept sliding away from rather than resolving into shape, the way a word repeated too many times stops meaning anything at all. There was no wind here, and yet something moved, some vast unhurried current she felt rather than heard, the way one feels the tide through a boat's hull long before any wave arrives to explain it.

Her vision was entirely clear. That was the detail that frightened her most, once the fear arrived properly — this was not fever, not delirium, not some dream her body had built to soften an unbearable hour. She was looking, with perfect clarity, at a place that could not exist, and it existed anyway, patiently, around her, indifferent to whether she believed in it.

"Do not move yet," Sancharan's voice said, close behind her, low and careful in a register she had never once heard him use. "You are safe. I promise you that much, whatever else I cannot yet promise."

"Where—" Her own voice came out thin, unfamiliar to her own ears. "Sancharan, where *are* we?"

He was silent, and in that silence she watched something move across his face that she had glimpsed before, in fragments, over the course of a strange week — the tower, the treasury, the alcove — but never once so plainly as now: not evasion exactly, but a man standing at the very edge of a truth he had spent an unbearably long time keeping to himself, deciding, in real time, whether tonight was finally the night he set it down.

"You should rest," he said instead, and she understood, with a clarity that had nothing to do with the strange light around her, that this was his answer. That it would remain his answer. That whatever this place was, whatever *he* was, she was not going to be told tonight, no matter how plainly she had already seen it with her own clear, unclouded eyes.

She tried, in the quiet that followed, to fit words to what she was seeing, and found every word she reached for arriving too small, the way a child's cupped hands arrive too small for water. It was beautiful. It was also, she understood distantly, watching him kneel beside her with an old, careful tenderness she had not yet earned an explanation for, the single most dangerous place she had ever been in her life, and some small, reckless part of her found she did not mind that nearly as much as she suspected she ought to.

***

It was the light that moved first — three small shapes detaching themselves from the dark beyond the towers, drifting toward them with a slow, deliberate patience that reminded her, absurdly, of birds of prey circling something already too weak to run.

Sancharan was on his feet before she had finished registering they were there, placing himself between her and the approaching shapes with a speed that belonged to nothing she had ever seen a human body do. "Stay behind me," he said, and there was command in it now, old and absolute, nothing at all like the careful, deferential court scholar who had spent a week letting her lead.

The shapes resolved as they neared — pale rings of light, each easily as wide as a man was tall, turning slowly end over end, and where they turned the air itself seemed to bend faintly around their edges, the way water bends around something dense enough to matter. They did not look at her. She understood, somehow, watching them, that they did not *look* at all, not with anything so simple as eyes — and yet she felt, with absolute certainty, that she had been noticed, weighed, and found to be a question none of them yet knew how to answer.

*Threat,* one of them said, or did not say, the word arriving in her mind without ever troubling the air to carry it. *Unbonded. Unmarked. Unaccounted.*

Vaanathi felt the word land in her chest like something physical, a judgment rendered without cruelty and without appeal, and understood, watching the great rings draw slowly closer, that whatever these things were, they had not come to be reasoned with. They had come to correct an error, the way a hand corrects a page gone crooked, without malice and without the smallest capacity to be argued out of the correcting.

"She is under my protection," Sancharan said, and his voice, when he spoke to them, had lost every trace of Sancharan in it entirely — something older spoke through that shape now, something that made the great rings slow their turning, though they did not yet withdraw. "She came to no harm by her own choosing. I will answer for her presence here myself."

*She should not see this place.*

"She already has," he said. "That is done, and cannot be undone by any of us standing in this hall arguing about it. What remains within my power is whether she leaves it remembering kindness or remembering fear, and I would very much prefer, tonight, that it be the former."

*You have never brought one before.*

"No," Sancharan agreed, and something in the admission cost him visibly, a weight passing over his face even as his voice held perfectly steady. "I have not. I do not intend to make a habit of it. I ask, this once, that you trust the judgment behind the choosing, even if you cannot yet trust the choosing itself."

The rings turned a while longer, weighing something Vaanathi could not follow, close enough now that she could feel, faintly, a pressure against her skin like the moment before a struck bell finds its note — and then, one by one, they drew back into the vast dark beyond the towers, folding themselves away as though they had never been more than a trick of strange light to begin with.

Sancharan did not move for a long moment after they had gone. When he finally turned back to her, whatever had spoken through him a moment ago had receded again behind his own tired, familiar face, and she saw, with a small shock that cut through even the larger one still lodged in her chest, that his hands were not quite steady.

"They will not trouble you again," he said. "I have seen to that. No one here will know you were ever within these walls, if I have any say in the matter — and I intend to have a great deal of say in the matter."

"You are hiding me," Vaanathi said slowly. "From your own — " She did not have the word. She was not certain, tonight, that any word she currently possessed would fit. "From whatever those were."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He looked at her a long moment, and something in his face, in that strange folding light, was more unguarded than she had ever seen it in the ordinary world above. "Because you are the first thing I have brought into this place in longer than I can comfortably measure," he said, "and I find, rather to my own surprise, that I am not prepared to lose you to it."

## IV

He tended her through what should have been the worst of the night, though *night* was a word that meant very little in a place where the strange sky never once completed whatever slow turning it was always in the middle of.

Whatever this place was, it did not move through time the way the mortal world moved through it — she understood that much without being told, watching the careful, unhurried way he worked, as though the desperate hour that had brought them here had already stretched, impossibly, into something roomier than any hour had a right to be. He did not chant over her, did not perform anything she could have named a ritual. He simply knelt beside her with his hand resting lightly over the place the old warding-cold had settled deepest, patient in a way that made her chest ache to watch, and let whatever he was do, slowly, whatever it was that mortal medicine could never have done in time.

"You are angry with me," he said, at some point in that long, roomless while, not looking up from his work.

"I am frightened," Vaanathi said, honestly, because she found she no longer had the strength tonight for anything less than honest. "I do not think those are the same thing, though I understand why you might mistake one for the other."

"What frightens you?"

She considered the question with more care than she had given anything in days. "Not this place," she said finally. "Not even you, though I suspect I ought to be considerably more frightened of you than I currently manage to be." She met his eyes, and did not look away, though it cost her something to hold there. "I am frightened of how little I seem to mind not knowing. I have spent my whole life needing to know things, Sancharan. Needing to be the one who noticed the missing page, the wrong seal, the detail no one else caught. And here I am, in a place that should not exist, being tended by a man who is very plainly not entirely what he has told me he is, and the truth is I am too tired, and too grateful, and — " She stopped herself before the last word could finish arriving, though she suspected, from the way his hand went briefly still over her heart, that he had heard it anyway.

"I will tell you," he said quietly. "Not tonight. I am not yet brave enough for tonight, and I would rather give you truth I can stand behind than truth I offer only because I am too exhausted to keep guarding it. But I will tell you. I want you to know that much, even if I cannot yet give you the rest."

"That is not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It is a promise instead. I have found, of late, that I am considerably better at those than I ever expected to be."

## V

She slept, eventually, in the strange roomless way the fortress seemed to accommodate, and when she woke the cold was gone from her entirely, and Sancharan was watching her with the particular exhausted stillness of a man who had not slept at all.

"Anaadikottai," he said, before she could ask again, offering the word the way one offers a single coin from a much larger fortune, testing whether it would be enough. "That is the nearest name I have for this place, in a tongue that still exists anywhere mortals speak it. I will not pretend it explains very much."

"It is beautiful," Vaanathi said, and found, examining the admission, that she meant it entirely. "I did not expect that. I do not know what I expected. Not this."

"Few do." Something almost like a smile touched his mouth, tired and real. "It is time I returned you to a world that will have noticed your absence rather more than either of us would like to explain."

"Wait." She caught his sleeve before he could rise, surprising them both with the suddenness of it. "Before we go back — before I have to be a princess again, and you have to be a scholar, and we both have to pretend this night fits into the shape of an ordinary week — tell me one true thing. Not everything. One thing."

He considered her a long moment, weighing something she could not name behind his tired eyes. "The rings," he said finally, quietly, "answer to me because they are, in some sense I do not know how to make smaller for you, a part of me. I do not fully understand it myself, some days. I have had a very long time to stop asking."

It was not very much. It was, she understood, turning the words over with the same care she gave everything now, considerably more than he had ever offered her before, and she found she was grateful for it in a way that surprised her with its intensity.

"Thank you," she said simply.

"Do not thank me yet," Sancharan said. "I suspect, before this is finished, you will have considerably more cause to be angry with me than grateful."

"I will decide that for myself, when the time comes. I generally do."

That, finally, earned her the smile properly — brief, tired, entirely unguarded — and something in her chest turned over at the sight of it in a way she no longer had the energy, tonight, to argue herself out of.

***

The passage back felt shorter than the passage in, though she could not have said whether that was true or only felt true, the way the whole of this night had already begun to feel less like memory and more like something she had read once, long ago, in a book she could not entirely trust. When the strange sky gave way at last to the ordinary dark above the Amudha Kulam, dawn already greying faintly at its edge, she found her own legs beneath her again, her own cold, ordinary breath in her chest, Iruli a dark, relieved shape circling once overhead before settling on Sancharan's shoulder with unusual gentleness.

*You are both fools,* Iruli said, though there was no real bite in it tonight. *I want that said plainly, before either of you decides this was somehow a reasonable evening.*

"Noted," Sancharan said, "and disregarded, as always."

*As always,* Iruli agreed, with the air of a creature long since resigned to the futility of her own good advice.

"You will ask me again," Sancharan said, turning back to Vaanathi, "what that place was. What I am."

"Yes."

"And I will not answer. Not today."

"I know." Vaanathi studied him a long moment in the greying light — the same careful, guarded face she had first met across a startled mare in a courtyard that now felt impossibly distant, the same face that had, not an hour past, spoken with a voice that belonged to something far older than any scholar. "But I find, Sancharan, that I am no longer willing to simply stop asking. You have given me a door and no key. I intend to go on trying every lock I can find, until one of them finally yields."

"I would expect nothing less of you," he said, and there was no fear in it, only something that looked, in the pale coming light, almost like relief — the particular relief of a man who had spent a very long time being feared for what he was, and had just been told, plainly, that he would instead simply be *pursued*.

They began the long ride back to Anantapura as the sun cleared the scrubland's low rise, and if neither of them spoke a great deal on the road home, the silence between them had changed its shape again, the way it kept changing, chapter upon chapter, into something that no longer resembled distance at all.
