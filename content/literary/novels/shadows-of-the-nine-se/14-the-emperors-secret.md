## I

Nobody slept that night. By dawn the palace had been searched twice, the pantry sealed, and the dead man laid out in the guardroom under a sheet. Nobody knew his name.

Vaanathi sat on the edge of her bed with her sandals still on. She had washed her face. She had eaten a little. She had told the story three times, and each time it had come out shorter. A man had attacked her. Sancharan had stopped him. The man had swallowed something and died. It was all true, and it was not the truth. She did not like how smoothly it came.

At the first grey light, a servant knocked.

"The emperor asks for you, Highness. And for the scholar. He says to come as you are."

She came as she was.

Her father was waiting at the back of the throne hall. The hall was empty at that hour. The great doors stood shut. The long bars of light had not yet begun to fall through the windows, and the gold throne on its dais was only a heavy shape in the shadows. Anantaraja stood behind it, in a plain white wrap, with a lamp in one hand.

Sancharan was already there. He stood a little way off with his arms at his sides. Iruli sat on his shoulder, wet-looking and tired.

"Close the door," said the emperor.

Vaanathi had never been in this part of the hall. A curtain of heavy brown cloth hung behind the dais. He drew it aside. Behind it, set into the stone floor, was a square iron trap with a ring. He put down the lamp, took hold of the ring, and heaved. It rose without a sound. Cold air came up from below, dry and old, smelling of dust and dead lamp oil.

"Mind the steps," he said. "They are narrow."

"Where do they go?"

"Down."

"That is what you told me yesterday."

"I did." His voice was rough with tiredness. "I was not lying."

She remembered. *Do not go down further than you can climb.* She went after him.

The stair was a short one. It turned twice and ended in a round room, no wider than the royal bedchamber, with a ceiling so low that Sancharan had to stoop. There was no window. Nine small niches ran round the wall at the height of her shoulder. Each held a clay lamp, cold and black with old smoke. In the middle of the floor stood a bronze table, green with age.

A single plate lay on the table. It was the size of a shield. It was covered in close-set letters, and at the top of it, cut deep, was the wheel.

She stopped.

"You have seen it," said her father.

"Four times," said Vaanathi. "In four places. Each time there is a piece missing."

"Look closer."

She bent over the plate. The wheel was cut in the bronze with great skill. The circle, the hub, the nine spokes. At the bottom there was a wedge-shaped gap. But the edges of the gap were not like the rest of the carving. They were ragged. She ran her fingertip along them. They were rough and raw, like the broken end of a bone.

"This was not cut," she said. "It was broken."

"Yes."

"When?"

"I will tell you." He drew a long breath. "I will tell you all of it. I have wanted to for twenty years. I was told I could not, until the water rose."

## II

"My father died when I was twenty-two," said Anantaraja. "He had a fever. It went to his chest. He did not know he was dying until the last day. On that day he called me to his bed and said, *There is a room under the throne. You will find the key in the third niche. I have not read what is in it. I was afraid to.*"

He laid his hand on the bronze.

"I came down here a week after the burning. I read the plate. And then I put it back, and I did not come down again for twenty-six years."

"Why?"

"Because it told me what was asked of me, and I did not want it." He glanced at her. "It told me that I was not the king. I was the keeper. There is a difference."

Sancharan had said nothing. He stood against the wall, in the shadow between two niches. His face was calm. But Vaanathi saw that his hand was on the stone, flat, and that his knuckles were pale.

"Read it," said her father. "Aloud. It is not long."

She read. The letters were old, a stiff square script she half remembered from the oldest lessons. She read slowly, one line at a time.

*In the first year of the hill, I, Anantha, chief of this place, was shown a thing that no mortal had seen.*

*Nine came to me. They stood in the rain under the thorn tree. They said that the world was held. They said it had been held since before there were chiefs, and that the holding was a burden, and the burden was shared among nine.*

*They said that something waits at the end of every world. They said it has no hatred. It does not wish us ill. It is only the going-out of things. They said that all the other worlds have gone to it, in their time, and that ours has been kept from it by the work of the nine, who stand between.*

Her voice had begun to shake. She steadied it.

*They said they could not keep the work alone. A keeper cannot tend the stone that holds him. They asked for a mortal hand.*

*I gave them mine. I gave them my house.*

*The bell is given to the house of Anantha. The stones are given to the house of Anantha. The peace is given to the house of Anantha. If the house keeps faith, the nine will keep the world. If the house breaks faith, the nine will not be able to.*

*This is the Accord. It is written in bronze so that it cannot be forgotten.*

She read to the end. At the foot of the plate there were nine small signs, arranged in a ring. They were too worn to make out. Beneath them was a single line in a different hand, much smaller and much more recent.

*Speak the name Sancharan at the foot of the throne, if the water moves against its course.*

She looked up.

"You said it," she said.

"Eight nights ago. I stood here with the lamp and spoke it aloud. I did not know if anything would happen." A faint, dry smile touched her father's face. "And three nights later, a tall man came to the river gate."

"I came on foot," said Sancharan.

"You did." The emperor met his eyes at last. "I did not know what to call you. The plate says only the name. I did not know if you would be a man or a storm."

"I am sorry to be neither."

"I am not." Her father drew a breath. "I am only very glad you came."

Vaanathi's mind was moving fast. It went to the stepwell, to the nine landings, to the door standing in the open air. It went to the violet sky and the nine seats. *Nine came to me. They stood in the rain.*

"Father. The ward at the stepwell. The door. The three sentinels. They said, *the line of Anantha has crossed the threshold.* They said no one of our house had come since the Accord was sealed."

"No one has."

"But you said we were given the stones."

"We were given the keeping of them. We were never given leave to enter." He closed his eyes briefly. "I think the founder understood a thing that his sons did not. A keeper who can touch what he keeps will, in time, begin to use it. So the door was shut to us from the first day. It opens now, because..."

"Because I bled on the handle."

"Because you are what you are." He looked at her. "Did it hurt?"

"A little."

"I am sorry." The words came out cracked. "I should have told you. I should have told you years ago."

## III

They sat on the floor in the end. There was nowhere else. Her father took the place nearest the table. Vaanathi sat across from him, hugging her knees. Sancharan remained standing, in the dark between two niches, and said nothing at all.

"There is more," said Anantaraja. "There is the thing I least wish to say."

"Tell me."

"The founder did not only receive the stones. He took them." He rubbed his face with both hands. "There was a family who kept them before us. They were astronomers. They had tended the nine places since before the hill had a name. They watched the sky and cleaned the marks and kept the count. When the founder came to power, he wished to be the sole keeper. He did not want rivals. And he feared what they knew."

Vaanathi felt the floor tilt.

"The House of the Tower," she said.

"Yes."

"He drove them out."

"He did more than that. He erased them. He had their names cut from every record. He burned their books. He gave out that the keeping had always been ours. A generation later, no one remembered otherwise." Her father's voice was flat and weary. "Only the last of them was left. A boy. The founder was ashamed. He gave the child the old tower, and a stipend from the crown, to be paid in perpetuity. No one was told why. It was passed from steward to steward, an entry in a ledger that nobody questioned."

"Viyamban's family."

"I did not know his name. I knew there was a warden. I never went to see him." He lowered his hands. "I was a coward."

"You were a king."

"There was no difference, that year."

The lamp guttered on the floor beside them. Vaanathi looked at the plate, at the ragged wedge.

"The leaf in the genealogy," she said. "The one that was torn out. Smaran's own page."

"The first leaf. Before the names begin." Her father nodded slowly. "It lists those the founder displaced. It is not long. Someone wished it gone."

"Who?"

"I do not know." He turned his hands palm up. "I thought it was Charudatta. I still think it may be. His house has served the throne as ministers of the seal for ten generations. He knows the bell exists. He knows there is a secret. I do not think he knows all of it. But he is a man who would burn a great deal to keep one thing hidden."

"You suspect him."

"I suspect everyone who is alive. It is the habit of a long reign." He gave a short breath that was almost a laugh. "I have no proof. If I accuse a minister of ten generations in the middle of a flood, the court will split. I will lose the granary roads. People will starve. So I wait."

"That is a very poor reason."

"It is the best one I have." He did not smile. "I told you I was a coward."

Vaanathi did not answer. She was thinking of Smaran, who had sat on the floor of the archive and let a child read ledgers. She was thinking of the red cloth, and the dates, and *I am still counting.* She was thinking of an old man in a stone room, wiping a single tear from his white beard.

"The wedge," she said. "How did it break?"

"On a single night, about two hundred years ago." Her father spoke quietly. "My great-great-grandfather wrote it down. He was a careful man. He said that on that night every wheel in the kingdom lost its wedge. Carved stone, cast bronze, painted wood. The masons found the gaps in the morning. They said no chisel had touched them. This plate was whole when he was a boy. By morning it looked as you see it."

She turned.

Sancharan had not moved. He stood very straight in the dark, his face lifted a little, like a man listening to a distant sound.

"You know," she said.

"I know the night."

"What happened?"

"Not here." His voice was low. "Not in front of the plate. I will tell you. I promised. But not here."

Anantaraja looked from one to the other. Something sharpened in his face. He did not ask.

## IV

"The bell," said the emperor.

He had gone to the third niche and taken something down from behind the cold lamp. It was a small iron key, black with age. He turned it over once in his hand and gave it to Vaanathi.

"That is for the treasury vault. The inner one. I have not used it in twenty years. I want you to have it."

"Why?"

"Because the bell is yours. It was always going to be." He closed her fingers around the key. "It was cast in the founder's time. It has one purpose. I do not know how it works, and I have never wished to find out. But I know what it does."

"Tell me."

"When one of our blood holds it, and speaks a lie that would put the throne in danger, or any of the nine stones, it sounds. One low note." His mouth tightened. "I held it once. I was twenty-six. A merchant lord from the coast had asked me to promise something, and I knew I could not keep it. I said I could. The bell rang."

"What happened?"

"Nothing. The merchant lord was deaf in one ear." A thin, rueful smile. "But I heard it. I have heard it every night for thirty years."

"That is why you never held it again."

"That is why I have never told a lie to the throne since."

She thought of the cold vault. She thought of the small green bell in its niche. *My father rules justly. The river is rising. I am not afraid.* She had said all three, with her hand on the bowl, and nothing had happened. She had told no lie that mattered.

"Smaran wrote *ask the bell*," she said.

"Did he?"

"He saw the nine wheels on the rim. He knew something was there. I think he meant for me to find out what."

"He was a very clever man." The emperor looked at the floor. "I should have trusted him with all of it. I could not. I had been told to tell nobody until the water rose. And I was afraid for him."

"And for me."

"For you most." He lifted his head. His eyes were bright and tired and unguarded. It was the look she had waited her whole life to see. "You asked why I never let you speak in court. I said I would tell you. This is why. The plate says the heir will be sought. Not for love. For use. I did not know by whom. I kept you where you could be seen and not heard, so that nobody would think you mattered."

"You made me a decoration."

"I made you safe. I made you a very annoyed decoration." The corner of his mouth moved. "I see now it was not enough."

She wanted to be angry. She searched for it and found it was already gone. What was left felt larger and quieter. She crossed the small room and sat beside him, hip to hip, and he put one broad hand on the top of her head the way he had in the garden.

"I would rather have known," she said.

"So would I," said the emperor. "In your place."

## V

Sancharan moved at last. He came out of the shadow and stood before the bronze table, and he did not look at either of them. He looked at the plate.

"May I?" he said.

"It is yours as much as mine," said Anantaraja.

Sancharan put out his hand. He did not touch the ragged edge of the wedge. He laid two fingers on the bronze above it, very lightly, and a small tremor passed through the metal. It made a faint high sound, like a struck wine cup.

"I knew about the family," he said.

The room went still.

"I knew the founder had driven them out. I knew it that same year. I was standing under the thorn tree when it was agreed. I heard the first king say that no other house should keep the stones." His voice was quite level. "I said nothing."

"Why not?" asked Vaanathi.

"Because it was not my place to weigh a king's mercy."

"You are the one who weighs."

"I weigh what a thing costs. I do not decide what it ought to have cost." He took his fingers from the plate. "It is the rule I live by. It has served me for a very long time. It has also, I think, allowed one very large wrong to stand for three hundred years."

"You judged it?"

"I did not judge it. That is the fault." He turned. His face in the lamplight was grey and grave. "I stood by. I told myself that it was not my business. A tall man who stands by is a very useful accomplice."

Her father stood slowly. For a moment the two of them faced one another, the broad stocky king in his plain white wrap, and the lean dark stranger with the crow on his shoulder.

"You are the Walker," said Anantaraja.

"I am."

"You are the one the plate means by the name."

"I am one of the nine. I am called other things as well." Sancharan inclined his head, gently. "I have told your daughter. I thought you would prefer that I did not wait."

"I would have waited." The emperor's voice was dry. "I am not sure I wished to hear it said."

"I am sorry."

"Do not be." He looked at Vaanathi, and then at the god, and she saw him take in something that was not quite surprise. It was a father's quick, unwilling arithmetic. "She did not tremble."

"She laughed," said Sancharan.

"Did she."

"At something I said. I have not been able to forget it."

Anantaraja was silent for a moment. Then he said, very evenly, "If you harm my daughter, I will not be able to punish you."

"I know."

"If you break her heart, I will find a way."

"I will not touch her."

"That is not what I said," said the emperor.

Vaanathi put both hands over her face. Iruli made a sound very like a snort.

"One more matter," said her father, and his voice changed. He had turned away to hide it. "It is why I waited until now to bring you down. I have had a letter."

"From whom?"

"From the court of Samudrapuram. From the young prince, Veeran. He writes with great courtesy." He took a leaf from his waist cloth and held it, without opening it. "He asks for your hand. He proposes to come here within the month. He asks that the betrothal vow be spoken at the sky-tower, on the dark of the moon, as the old custom of his country requires."

"What custom?"

"I have never heard of one." The emperor's grip tightened on the leaf. "I have asked three scholars. There is none. And Vaanathi, the sky-tower is not known beyond these walls. It is not on any map. I did not know its name myself until a week ago."

"Then how does a prince from across the sea know where to ask for it?"

"That," said Anantaraja, "is precisely what I would like to find out."
