## I

The sky-tower stood at the far end of the east wall, where the palace gave way to scrub and rubble. Nobody went there. A track ran up to it, but weeds had eaten the track. A hundred paces off, it looked like a broken tooth.

Close up, it was larger than Vaanathi remembered. She had seen it from her window all her life. She had never stood at its foot. The base was a block of pale stone as wide as a house. Above that, the walls climbed straight up, cracked and grey, hung with creeper from top to bottom. The leaves were dark and glossy. They did not move in the morning wind.

"The vines are too neat," said Sancharan.

"Neat?"

"A wild creeper grows in tangles. This one grows in lines." He stepped up to the wall and lifted a trailing stem. It hung straight down, like a plumb line. "Someone has been training it."

"For what?"

"To hide something. Or to hold it up." He let the stem fall. "I cannot tell yet."

There was a door at the base, narrow and low, made of dark wood bound with iron. It stood slightly open. Vaanathi knocked. The sound went in and came back thin.

"Hello?"

Nothing answered.

"Perhaps he sleeps."

"Perhaps." Sancharan put out a hand and pushed. The door swung inward without a sound. The hinges had been oiled.

Inside was a stairwell. It was a plain round shaft of stone with a spiral stair winding up the wall, and it was lit from above by a pale grey shine. A smell hung in the air. It was dry, and a little sweet, like old paper and cold ash. It was not an unpleasant smell. But it made her think of a place where time was kept in jars.

They climbed.

She counted steps to steady herself. At fifty she gave up. The stair turned and turned. Narrow slits in the wall let in thin blades of light, and each blade showed the same bare stone and the same worn grooves. She went up with one hand on the wall. Sancharan came behind her, a step lower, and she could tell he was watching her feet.

At the sixtieth step she stopped to rest. He stopped too, one stair below. Their heads were level.

It was very quiet. Dust turned slowly in the light between them.

"You keep a distance," she said.

"I do."

"Always?"

"I would prefer it." He did not look away from her. He did not look anywhere else, either. "It is not a dislike."

"I did not think it was."

She had not meant to say that. It sounded like something else once it was out. She turned quickly and went on climbing, and heard him let out a breath behind her. It was the kind a man lets out when he has been holding it for some time.

The stairs ended at a trapdoor. She pushed it up and climbed through into the light.

## II

The chamber at the top was round, with a ceiling lost in shadow. Pale stone walls rose on every side, thick with the same dark creeper, which here hung loose and straight from the roof in long curtains. A single arched window faced east. Through it the whole sky was visible, going from grey to a clear soft gold.

In the middle of the floor stood a low stone table. Its top was scored with lines. They were faint and old, and they crossed each other in a pattern like the veins of a leaf. She saw at once that it was not a table at all. It was a sighting-board. Somebody had used it, over many years, to mark where things moved in the sky.

A man sat beside it.

He was old. That was her first thought. He was very old and very thin, and he sat so still that she had not noticed him. His skin was deep brown and deeply lined. His brows were heavy and black. His hair fell to his shoulders in a grey cloud, and his short beard was white. He wore a plain antariyam the colour of weathered wood, and his feet were bare on the stone.

He looked up. His eyes were large and mild. They did not look surprised.

"Ah," he said. "Smaran's girl."

She had meant to say something dignified. She forgot it. "You know me?"

"I have watched you from this window for twenty years." His voice was soft and dry. It sounded like a page being turned. "You were small. You ran in the courtyard. I used to think, there goes the next one."

"The next what?"

"The next of the house." He gave a thin smile. "Forgive me. I do not get many visitors. I say what comes."

He began to rise. It took a long time. His knees cracked. Sancharan stepped forward as though to help, then stopped, and Vaanathi saw the old man note it with a single sharp flick of the eyes.

"You are Viyamban," she said.

"I am. Uncle Viyamban, to those who knew me when." He steadied himself on the stone table. "And you have come with news. I know it. I saw you at the burning ground. I could not come down." His voice thickened. "I have not come down in nine years. It is a long thing to explain."

"You were his friend."

"I was his friend for sixty years." He put one hand to his chest. For a moment he seemed to fold, and Vaanathi was across the floor before she knew it, one hand under his elbow. His arm was thin as a stick. "Child. Child, I am well. Only old. Only..."

He did not finish. A single tear ran down the side of his nose and into his white beard. He did not wipe it. He let it fall.

She helped him to the floor mat beside the table and he sat, breathing hard. When she looked up, Sancharan was standing very still, watching the old man with an expression she could not read.

"I am sorry for your loss," said Sancharan quietly.

"Thank you, sir. And you are?"

"A guest of the emperor. I am called Sancharan."

"Sancharan." The old man said it carefully, as if trying a lock. "It is a name I have seen in old writing. In the margins, mostly. Not the sort of name one hears." His eyes rested on the tall man a moment too long. "You walk like someone I have read about."

"Many people are read about," said Sancharan. "Few of them are accurate."

The old man laughed. It was a small dry sound, and it ended in a cough.

"Sit, sit. There is water. There is fruit, though it is not fresh. I eat very little." He gestured at a low wooden table against the inner wall. On it stood a clay lamp, a stack of narrow palm leaves, and a basket of withered plums. "Forgive the state of things. A man who lives alone becomes a hermit."

"You had a visitor four days ago," said Vaanathi.

She watched his face. It did not alter.

"Smaran came to see me," he said. "He climbed all those stairs. He was seventy-one and he climbed all those stairs, and he arrived grey in the face and grinning. He had a leaf in his hand." He shook his head slowly. "He asked what the mark meant. I told him it was the sign of my house. A keeping-sign. That was all I told him. I wish now I had said more."

"Why did you not?"

"Because it is a long story, and an old one, and I was afraid." He lifted his eyes to hers. They were wet. "I have been afraid of that story all my life. My father was afraid of it. And his father. We are not brave people, we of the Tower. We are people who keep things."

"Keep what?"

"The sky," said the old man simply. "That is what we were told. That the House of the Tower keeps the sky for the throne."

"What does that mean?"

"I have no idea." He spread his thin hands. "I have never known. We tend the stones and we clean the marks and we keep the count. We do it each dark moon, as our fathers did. We were given a charge, and the reason was lost along the way."

"Which stones?"

He looked at her for a very long moment.

"Look at the wall," he said.

## III

She turned.

The far wall was the only part of the chamber that was not covered by hanging vine. It was veiled instead. A thick growth of creeper had been trained across it, laid flat in a lattice, so that it made a green screen from floor to roof.

Vaanathi crossed the room and drew the lattice aside.

It was carved into the stone. It was no relief or plaque, only a flat shape cut into the surface and worn smooth by the centuries. A circle, a small hub, and nine straight spokes running out to the rim. At the bottom of the circle, where the sixth hour would be on a water-clock, a wedge was missing. It was cut out cleanly through the rim and through the two nearest spokes and into the very edge of the hub.

It was perfectly plain. It was perfectly silent. She stood in front of it and felt every hair on her arms rise.

"It is the mark of my house," said Viyamban behind her. "We carved it here before the tower was built. The tower was built to hold it."

"It was carved before the tower?"

"The wall was an older wall. The tower grew around it." He had come up beside her without her hearing, leaning on a stick. "I have often wondered who cut the first one. And why they left it unfinished."

"Unfinished?"

"The wheel is not meant to have a wedge gone. I think it was whole once. I think something took the piece." He touched the stone, gently, at the edge of the gap. "My father believed the wheel was a picture of the sky. Nine houses, nine spokes. A great round machine of nine parts, turning in balance. He said one part had slipped. He did not say which."

"And you?"

"I am an old man with a sighting-table." He smiled. "I believe what I can measure."

Vaanathi was looking at the gap. It seemed to her to be deeper than it should have been. She had the sudden unpleasant sense that if she put her finger in it, the finger would not come out.

She took a step back.

Sancharan had not moved. He stood by the sighting-table, with the dawn behind him, and she saw that his face was quite empty. It was the face of someone who has been struck and is waiting to feel it.

"Sir?" said Viyamban. "Is anything wrong?"

"No." Sancharan looked at the wall for a moment more. Then he walked slowly across the room and stopped in front of the carving. He did not touch it. His hand rose. It hovered a finger's width from the stone and stayed there, and a muscle in his jaw moved.

"It is very old," he said.

"The oldest thing in the palace, I think."

"Older than the palace." His voice was level. "Yes."

"How old?"

"I could not say." He lowered his hand and turned away. "I am only a scholar."

The old man's mild eyes went from his face to the wall and back again. Something moved in them, quick as a fish under ice. Then it was gone, and he was only a tired old man again, leaning on a stick.

## IV

They stayed for the better part of an hour.

He gave them water in clay cups. It was cool and tasted faintly of the stone. He told them about the tower. His great-grandfather had been the first of the House to live in it, he said, though the line went back further. They were astronomers. They had watched the sky since there had been a city to watch it from. Before the Anantha kings, perhaps. He did not know.

"We were here before the throne," he said. "That is what my father said. When I was young I thought it a boast. Now I think it was a warning."

He did not say what kind.

"You said the stones," said Vaanathi. "You said you tend them. Where are they?"

"Nine of them, they say. One in the tower here. The others I have never seen. My father thought they were scattered, one in each place where the sky comes closest to the earth." He waved a thin hand. "I tend only mine. At each dark moon I climb to the roof and clean the face of it. That is all. Nobody has told me to stop."

"When is the next dark moon?"

"In twelve days." He glanced at the sighting-table, and a little of the dreaminess went out of his face. "The sky is thin that night. Without a moon, you can see how many holes there are in it. You should come."

"To your tower?"

"To the roof. If your guest allows it." He glanced at Sancharan, and away. "Smaran was meant to come, this time. He said he would. I would like to think someone would take his place. It would please him, wherever he is. He always said the sky was better with company."

"I will think about it," said Vaanathi.

"That is all anyone does, in the end." He smiled at her. It was a sweet smile, a little crooked. "Think about it, and then go on as before."

Vaanathi asked a hundred questions. He answered most of them, mildly, with long pauses. He had no children. His wife had died long ago. Smaran had been the only friend who climbed the stairs. He had a sister, once, in the south. She too was dead.

She was watching his hands as he talked. They were long and thin and spotted with age. They shook a little when he lifted the cup. One finger was crooked.

On the low table against the inner wall, beside the clay lamp, stood a small stoppered vial. It was dark glass, no taller than her thumb. She had seen it as soon as she came in. She had pretended not to.

"What is that?"

"That?" He followed her gaze. "My heart medicine. A tincture. Bitter root, mostly. It is the oldest thing my family knows how to do." He gave a rueful smile. "Smaran used it too. I made it for both of us. We were old men with failing hearts, and neither of us would admit it."

She crossed to the table. She did not touch the vial. She bent and sniffed, once, quickly.

It was bitter. It was faint and sharp, and it was the same. It was the exact smell she had breathed in at Smaran's cold fingers two days before.

Her heart gave a heavy knock.

"It has a strong smell," she said.

"All the good ones do." Viyamban's voice was gentle. "Smaran's hands must have smelled of it. He took a drop each night. It would have stayed on his skin."

It was a reasonable answer. It was an entirely reasonable answer. She told herself so.

"May I take a little?" she asked. "To show a healer."

"Of course." He picked up the vial with a trembling hand and held it out. "Take it all. I can make more. Show it to anyone you like. It is only a medicine."

She took the vial. It was cool and light. She slid it into the fold of her waist cloth with the three leaves.

When they came to the trapdoor, she looked back. The old man stood in the middle of the round room in the full gold of the dawn. He had put one hand on the carved wall. His lips were moving. She could not hear what he said.

"He prays," said Vaanathi.

"Perhaps," said Sancharan.

They went down. The stairs were long and the light thin. She counted the steps, and at forty-one she heard him speak behind her.

"He did not leave the tower in nine years."

"He said so."

"His feet were bare." Sancharan's voice was very quiet. "A man who sits above stone for nine years has grey soles and dry heels. His were scuffed. And there was a fleck of red clay between two toes."

"Red clay?"

"There is none on this wall. The nearest I know of is at the western stepwell, where they dug down through the old red rock."

She stopped on the stair. She did not turn.

"He is a frail old man who has been kind to us," she said.

"He is."

"He wept for his friend."

"He did."

"Then it means nothing."

"Very likely." Sancharan came down a step and stopped. The stairwell was narrow. For a moment she felt his breath on her hair. "I am only telling you what I saw. You must decide what to do with it."

She went on down in silence. The red clay bothered her more than she wanted it to. She tried to picture the old man walking out through the weeds at night, and could not make the picture hold. The door at the bottom was open and the morning lay beyond it, flat and bright. She stepped through. Behind her the tower rose against the sky, and the creeper hung straight down its walls like a hundred green threads, perfectly still.

She had a small bitter vial in her waistband, and a ledger entry, and three letters, and a mark on a wall. And one more thing she had not noticed until she was halfway across the weeds.

Her hand was shaking. She put it in a fold of her cloth so that he would not see.
