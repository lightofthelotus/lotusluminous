## I

Word of her arrival ran ahead of her through the city like fire through dry thatch.

By the time she reached the west gate there was a crowd. Nobody had called it. They simply came out of doorways and side lanes and stood in the road, and the crowd grew as she walked. The lamp-sellers stopped. The women put down their baskets. A man with a bundle of firewood took off his head cloth and held it in front of him in both hands.

Nobody cheered. That frightened her more than a cheer would have. They stood in silence on both sides of the road and watched her pass, and their faces were thin and worn and strange. She saw an old man leaning on a stick who could not have been more than forty. She saw a girl with white at her temples. She saw a woman holding a baby who looked up at Vaanathi with eyes that were far too wise.

"They are all older," she said, very quietly.

"They are," said Sancharan, at her shoulder. "A little. The farther from the palace, the less. But everywhere."

"Eleven nights."

"Yes."

"It was one night to me."

"I know."

She had a sick, floating sense of having mislaid something. She pressed her hand against her ribs, where the cold of the stone still lingered, and walked on.

A boy ran alongside her for a time, a skinny child of nine or ten. He was looking up at her with great round eyes. At last he said, "Is it true you went to the sky?"

"Who told you that?"

"The washerwoman. She said you went up with the scholar and the crow, and the stars gave you back." He paused. "Did they?"

"They did."

"Was it very far?"

"It was a good deal farther than it looked," said Vaanathi.

At the palace gate the guards met her with drawn spears. Then they saw her face, and the spears went up, and a captain whom she had known since she was twelve fell on one knee in the dust. He did not say a word. He only put his forehead down against her sandal.

"Please," she said, "get up."

"We lit the lamps, Highness," said the captain, in a muffled voice. "Every night. I said that we should. His Grace did not say no."

"Thank you."

"The scholar..." The captain did not look at Sancharan. "There are some in the city who say he took you."

"I was not taken," said Vaanathi. "I went. If anyone is to be blamed, it should be me."

She did not say it loudly. But the guards heard, and the words moved off through the gate like a ripple, and behind her the first quiet murmur rose from the crowd.

## II

The court was assembled by noon. It had not been summoned. It had simply gathered, as water gathers in a hollow.

She had expected the throne hall to be full. She had not expected it to be so quiet. Every minister was there, every general, every grey-haired clerk and anxious steward. They stood in their ranks in the long bars of light. Nobody spoke. It was the silence of a room where everyone is waiting to be told that something is not true.

Her father sat on the gold throne.

He was bent. He had a rug across his knees, though the day was warm. His hands lay on the carved arms, thin and spotted, and his white beard rested on his chest. He looked as though he had been carved from candle wax and left too near a lamp. But he held his head up. When she came down the long hall alone, he looked at her. It was the first time in her life that he had looked at her in court.

He did not say anything. He only inclined his head, by a hair's breadth.

She did not go to her place.

She had sat in it for twenty years. She had sat on the left of the throne, one step below, close enough to be seen and far enough to be ignored. She walked past it. She walked to the foot of the dais, to the broad flat stretch of floor where petitioners stood, and she stopped there, in the middle of the hall, with the whole court behind her and the throne above.

"Your Grace," she said.

"Daughter."

"I have been away."

"You have been missed."

A faint dry movement went through the ranks of the ministers, like wind in a field. Somebody coughed.

"My lord," said a smooth voice. "If I may."

Minister Charudatta stepped forward from the front rank. He had not changed. That was the first thing she saw. He was the only man in the hall who had not been touched. His forest green was crisp, and his moustache was trim, and his dark hair was streaked only at the temples as it had always been. He bowed to the throne with exactly the right depth.

"I rejoice at the princess's return," he said. "All of us do. But I must raise a matter that cannot wait. For eleven nights this house has been in grief. The people have lit lamps at crossroads. And the palace has been struck by a sickness the like of which no physician has seen. His Grace himself..." He allowed his voice to fall. "I would not say aught that might wound. I say only that these afflictions began in the week the foreign scholar came."

"They did," said Vaanathi.

The minister paused. He had expected a different answer.

"And the princess vanished on the same night as the scholar," he went on, more slowly. "And has now returned with him. I ask only for an account. I think that the court is owed it. It is a plain question. Where has the princess been?"

"I will answer it."

"I am sure Your Highness will."

"I will answer it as the heir of this house. And as the one who was sent." She turned. She faced the whole hall. Her voice rang up into the roof, thin and clear, and she heard it and was astonished that it did not shake. "Minister, you were quite right. These afflictions are not a sickness. They are not the work of the scholar. They have a cause. I have been to see it. I have been to the place where it lives."

Nobody breathed.

"I will not tell you where. I will not tell you what. It is not mine to tell, and it is not yet safe." She drew a breath. "I will tell you that the aging is real. It is spreading. It touches every living thing in this city, and it will touch everything in the land if it is not stopped. I went to try to stop it. I have slowed it. I have not ended it."

"With what authority?" cried a voice at the back.

"With the authority of the one who sits above me." She turned and looked up at the throne. "Father. I speak on your word. Is that so?"

A long, silent moment.

Anantaraja rose.

It took him an enormous effort. He put both hands on the arms of the throne. He pushed himself upright by inches, and the rug slid from his knees, and the whole hall watched him do it. He stood. He was stooped and white and shaking. But he stood like a man at the end of a long road who will not be carried across the last step.

"The princess speaks for me," he said. His voice was thin, but it carried. "She has done so since the day she was born. I did not tell you. I did not know how." He drew a breath. "She was sent. She did what none of you could. If you doubt it, look at the one who stands before you in the ordinary dust. Not one of you has ever done as much."

He sat down.

The hall was dead silent.

Then, from the back, slowly, a single pair of hands began to clap. It was the old treasurer, Bhargava. He clapped three times, quite clearly, in the hush. And then another man joined, and another. A sound like soft rain began to move through the hall. It rose. It swelled. It was not a cheer. It was something stranger, a kind of low incredulous relief. It was the noise of people being allowed to hope.

Charudatta stood where he was. He did not clap. His smile had not changed. But his eyes had gone for a moment very still, and she thought she saw in them a thing that was not anger. It looked like grief. It was gone before she could name it.

"I rejoice," he said, and bowed. "Beyond measure."

## III

She found Ratnan outside the throne hall afterwards, standing in a corner with his arms crammed full of ledger bundles. He had gone thin. There were shadows under his eyes. He looked at her as though she were a ghost, and then dropped a bundle and burst into tears.

"I am sorry," he said. "I am so sorry. I have been trying to be brave. I have been telling everyone you would come back, and nobody believed me, and I did not believe me either."

"Ratnan. Breathe."

"I have been breathing for eleven days. It is exhausting." He scrubbed his face with a sleeve. "The lady Nila said you were safe. She said she was certain. She sat in the archive all night and did my accounts. I do not know how she knew."

"She is good at knowing."

"She is the only reason I did not run mad." He gathered up the dropped bundle. "I have things to tell you. I have been keeping a list. I thought you might want it. It is a little long."

"Is it about Sundar?"

"No. I mean, yes, a little. He has not come back. But it is about the minister." He glanced up and down the corridor. His voice dropped. "I followed him. In the eleven days. I am sorry. I know it was not asked. But he is so very clever. And I thought, if he is the one, he will make a mistake, and I might see it."

"Did you?"

"No. That is the strange part." Ratnan looked wretched. "He did not leave the palace. Not once. Not for a night. On the night of the last dark moon, the one before the flood, he was in the temple on the hill all evening, in front of two hundred people, giving out alms. I asked afterwards. I asked the priests, and the porter, and the man who sells flowers. He was there from dusk until dawn. He did not go to any stone."

"The dark moon was the red hill."

"Yes, Highness. And he was nowhere near it."

"Then it is not Charudatta."

"It is not Charudatta who scrapes the stones." Ratnan wrung the leather straps of the bundle. "He may be something else. He is something. But he is not that."

Vaanathi leaned against the wall. She felt oddly deflated. She had built a whole structure in her mind, and it had stood on a single beam, and the beam had gone.

"Thank you," she said. "That was well done."

"There is one more thing." He swallowed. "It is small. It may not matter."

"Say it."

"It is about the archive. About Smaran." His eyes had begun to shine. "I was thinking, in the long nights, about all the things he did that I thought were habit. He had so many. He always rose before dawn. He always sharpened his brush three times. And every morning when he came to the archive, he stopped at the door. He touched the threshold with two fingers. Just there, the slab. As though to say good morning."

Vaanathi stood very still.

"A keeper begins and ends at the threshold," she said.

"Highness?"

"It is a saying. He told me when I was six." She heard her own voice, strange and far away. "He said that every record in a house is only as safe as the door it passes through. He said the keeper's first duty and his last is to touch the stone."

"I thought it was only a quirk."

"So did I."

She did not wait for him to finish. She took the bundle from his arms and set it on the floor. She was walking before she knew it, fast, down the corridor, past the guardsmen and the dusty arches and the closed shutters of the long hall. Ratnan was running behind her, his sandals slapping.

"Highness! Where?"

"The archive."

## IV

The reading chamber was quiet. The shelves stood in their rows. Someone had taken away the writing table, and the stain of the old lamp lay on the floor like a coin. A sunbeam fell in at the narrow window, slow and thick with dust.

She went to the door.

It was a plain doorway, dark with age, two paces wide. The threshold was a single slab of grey granite, worn into a shallow dip. She had stepped over it a thousand times. She had never looked at it.

She knelt. She laid two fingers on the slab, as Smaran had, as he had every morning for thirty years.

It was cold. It was solid. It made no sound.

"There is a seam," said Sancharan.

She had not heard him come in. He stood at the end of the room with Iruli on his shoulder, in the shadow of the shelves. He had followed them without a sound. He did not come closer.

"Where?"

"At the left. A hair's breadth. You will not see it by looking." He tilted his head. "He pressed there, I think. Here. Where the stone dips lowest."

She put her thumb on the dip, at the left. She pressed. For a moment, nothing.

Then there was a small, dry click. The whole slab shifted, a bare finger's width, as though it had been waiting. It rocked. Underneath it, a narrow dark hollow opened in the floor.

Ratnan gave a faint cry.

She slid the stone aside. It moved easily on a rail of brass. In the hollow beneath, wrapped in a square of oiled cloth, lay a small clay box.

It was the size of her two hands. She lifted it out. The cloth was soft with age. She unwrapped it, very slowly, as though it might break. The lid was sealed with wax. She cracked it with her thumbnail.

Inside lay a sheaf of palm leaves, tied with a cord. A small folded cloth. And beside them, in a twist of old cloth, two small objects.

She took them out. She turned them over in her palm.

One was a cowrie shell. It was small and white, rubbed smooth by years of handling. The other was a hard dried twist of tamarind, black with age. She did not need to ask what they were. She had left them on the high beam when she was six years old, in a game she had half forgotten.

She did not know that she was crying until Ratnan, standing quiet by the door, held out a corner of his waist cloth. She took it. She pressed it to her eyes. Nobody spoke.

"He kept them," she said, at last.

"Yes."

"All these years."

"I think he kept everything," said Sancharan, very gently. "I think it was the kind of man he was."

She put the shell and the tamarind carefully into her sash. She untied the cord on the leaves.

It was a long letter. It was in Smaran's own hand, small and neat. The ink was fresh. The last line had been written, she thought, on the last night of his life.

![Smaran's Last Letter](13-03-dead-mans-floor.jpg)

*My dear girl,*

*I write this at the threshold, with the lamp lit, and I am almost sure that I am being watched. I will tell you what I have found. It is not all. I think it is enough.*

*First. The stones. There are nine, as the wheel says. I have seen two. The first, at the stepwell, I have visited for three years. It is scraped, once each dark moon, with a fine tool that cuts as no iron cuts. The scrapings are grey. They do not burn. I have put a pinch of them in the cloth below. Do not touch it with bare skin.*

*Second. There is a pattern. The stepwell was scraped every dark moon for three years. Two moons ago it stopped. One moon ago the second stone began. I believe there are three whose cutting counts, and that the third is the tower. I believe that the hand which scrapes is preparing a final act, and that the act is to be done at the dark moon, at the tower, and that it requires one thing that it does not have.*

*Third. It requires the heir.*

*I do not know why. I have worked it out as far as I can. A stone is a door. A door is opened by blood. I think the scrapers have cut the others open, and want only the key to turn the last. I think you are the key. I think the one who cuts has been waiting, patiently, for three hundred years, for a woman of your line to be old enough to say yes.*

She stopped. She read the sentence again. A cold finger went down her spine.

*Fourth. I have not told the emperor. I do not trust the minister's house. I may be wrong. But I would rather be a coward than the cause of a death.*

*Fifth. The warden of the tower.*

She read this part very slowly.

*I have loved Viyamban sixty years. I climbed his stair four nights ago and told him everything. I did not mean to. He asked so gently, and I was so glad to speak. I have been thinking since, and I do not like what I have been thinking. His feet were scuffed and red. I know that clay. It is from the stepwell. He told me he had not left his tower in nine years.*

*I believe he is the one who scrapes.*

*I pray that I am wrong. I think I am not. I think it is why he has been so kind.*

*If I am dead when you read this, you will know that I was right, and that I forgave him. Please do not hate him for me. He is a man in very great pain. I think that he has been in it a long time, and I never once asked.*

*I think I shall sleep tonight. I am not afraid. Be well, my dear. Be kind to Ratnan. Be better than I was.*

*Your old friend,*
*Smaran*

The room was very quiet.

She held the letter. A small cold wind had come into the room, though no window was open. It stirred the dust in the sunbeam. It lifted the corner of the leaf, and it died.

"He was wrong," said Vaanathi.

"About what?"

"Viyamban does not scrape the stones." She looked up at Sancharan. "He cleans them. He told me so himself. He tends the one at the tower. The red clay is from the stepwell because he goes down there to tend it, in secret. He is not the one who cuts."

"Then who?"

"The man who has the means. The man who wants me at the tower." She stood up. The letter shook in her hand. "The man who wrote to my father."

## V

They heard the horns at dawn.

She was already awake. She had sat up all night in the small garden with the letter in her lap, and Sancharan had sat across from her, and neither of them had said very much. The stars faded one by one. A thin cool breath of air came down from the east, smelling of the sea.

It came from the harbour. It was a long, low, sonorous note, like a conch, and then another, and then a third.

"The tide has turned," said Ratnan, from the path. He had been sleeping on the bench. He sat up, rubbing his eyes. "The sea has been slack for eleven days. The ships could not enter. They have been standing off the coast. Now it has come in." He blinked. "Those are the Samudrapuram horns."

"Already?"

"They are at the harbour mouth." He swallowed. "Highness. The prince is here."
