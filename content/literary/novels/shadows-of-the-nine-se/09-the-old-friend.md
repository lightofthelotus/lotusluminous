## I

The palace physician kept his rooms behind the kitchens, where the smell of steam and cumin covered the smell of medicine. He was a round, quick man named Kesavan, with a bald head and a habit of talking to his jars. Rows of them filled the shelves. Each one had a tiny knot of coloured thread tied round its neck, in a code only he knew.

"A visitor," he said, and jumped. "Highness. I was just telling the dried ginger that it has been very rude."

"I am sorry to interrupt."

"Not at all. It never listens." He wiped his hands on his waist cloth. "What can I do?"

She took the vial from her waist and set it on his table. She had not meant to be so quick about it. Her hands wanted to be rid of the thing.

Kesavan bent over it. He did not pick it up. He took out the stopper with two fingertips, wafted the smell towards his nose, and went still. Then he tipped one drop onto the back of his wrist and touched it with the tip of his tongue.

"Ah," he said.

"Is it dangerous?"

"Everything is dangerous, Highness. It depends on how much." He spat quietly into a bowl. "This is bitter root. Kuttam, in the southern name. It steadies a weak heart if you give a drop at night. It is as old as medicine. Half the grandmothers in the city make it."

"And if you took more?"

"A spoonful would slow the heart." He frowned at the vial. "Two would stop it. But the stuff is so foul that nobody could swallow that much without knowing. You would taste it in your teeth for a week."

"Unless it were hidden in something."

"In what?"

"I do not know. Honey water. Palm wine. Something sweet."

Kesavan looked at her for a time. His round face was no longer comical.

"Highness. Is this about the keeper?"

"Yes."

"I examined him last winter. He was sound. His heart was strong. He complained of his knees, and of the stairs, and of young clerks who could not spell." He tapped the table with one finger. "I have been told he died of age. I would like to hear someone say how."

"So would I."

"Then I will tell you something and I will deny it afterwards." He lowered his voice. "I saw the body before they covered it. His lips were not blue. His nails were not blue. A weak heart gives you that when it stops. His were pink, like a sleeper's."

"What does that mean?"

"It means that whatever stopped his heart did not feel like dying." Kesavan put the stopper back in the vial and pushed it across the table to her. "Take this away. Do not give it to anyone. I will say it is a common tincture and that I smelled nothing odd. And I will keep my eyes open."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me. Go and find out." He turned to his shelf and addressed the ginger. "You see? That is how a person behaves."

## II

"It is a common tincture," Vaanathi said. "It is exactly what he told us. It means nothing."

"Yes."

"You keep saying yes."

"I am agreeing with you."

"You are agreeing in a way that means no."

They were walking in the lower court. A thin warm rain had begun, and the paving stones had turned dark. Iruli was sitting on a gutter spout above them with her feathers puffed. She looked like a very old woman in a very bad mood.

Sancharan walked at her left, a full pace away, with his hands behind his back. He had not touched her since the riverbank. She had begun to notice the exact width of the space he left between them.

"I liked him," she said. "I did. He was gentle. He cried when he spoke of Smaran. He has lived alone in a stone room for nine years. Nobody who weeps like that could..."

"Could what?"

"Could do a thing like that."

"I have seen men weep for their victims," said Sancharan. "I have also seen men weep for their friends. The tears are the same. That is the difficulty with tears."

"You have seen a great deal."

"I have."

"For a scholar of thirty."

He did not answer.

"You do not look thirty," she said. "You look very tired. And you stand as if you are holding up a wall. And that morning in the tower, when you looked at the carved wheel..." She stopped in the rain. Water ran off the edge of her cloth. "Who are you?"

He stopped too. A drop of rain ran down the side of his face, and she saw that he did not blink it away.

"I am Sancharan," he said.

"That is a name. It is not an answer."

"It is the only one I can give you tonight."

"Why?"

"Because the true one would not help you," he said. "It would give you something heavy to carry, and you have enough already."

She looked at him for a long moment. His face was calm. But there was something under the calm that she had not seen in anyone before. It was not a lie, and it was not a secret. It was closer to a long patience, worn thin.

"Very well," she said. "But I will ask again."

"I would expect nothing less."

"And one day you will tell me."

"One day," said Sancharan, "you may wish I had not."

Above them, Iruli shook out her wings with a rattle like dry leaves and sent a spray of rain down onto both their heads. It broke the moment very nicely. Vaanathi was almost grateful.

## III

The old colonnade ran along the north side of the inner court. At night it was a long dim tunnel of arches, each opening black and square, each pillar plain and square and weathered smooth. The right-hand wall was covered in carved panels, scenes of kings and elephants and harvests, so worn by hands and weather that they looked like melting wax.

About halfway down, between two panels, there was an alcove.

It was narrow and deep. A slim pillar stood at its mouth, grey with age, like a thin man keeping watch. Above the arch a small stone ledge jutted out. Iruli was already on it, folded into a black knot, one eye glinting.

"In here," said Sancharan.

"There is barely room."

"There is room for two who stand still."

She went in first. He followed. The alcove was no wider than a grain bin. When he stepped in behind her, his shoulder was a hand's breadth from hers, and the air between them seemed to thicken.

![Vaanathi and Sancharan at the Alcove](04-02-vaanathi-alcove.jpg)

It was almost midnight. She had a plan, and the plan was this. At the burning ground that morning she had noticed Ratnan standing apart from the crowd, white-faced, with a look she knew. It was the look of someone who has said something he regrets. Later, in the archive, when she spoke aloud of the sky-tower, he had gone very still.

Then the minister had known about it, the next morning, before she told him.

She had said nothing to Sancharan. She did not need to. He had simply said, in the dark of the afternoon, "Where does the under-steward go at night?" And she had said, "To the colonnade. He walks there when he cannot sleep." And he had said, "Then we will wait."

So they stood.

The minutes went by. A lamp guttered in the court. Somewhere a watchman coughed. She became aware of small things. The roughness of the stone against her back. The slow pulse in her own throat. The smell of him, which was not any smell she knew. It was like rain on iron, like a deep cold cellar, like the quiet at the bottom of a well.

She turned her head very slightly. He was standing with his back flat to the alcove wall and his arms crossed tight across his chest. His eyes were shut. His jaw was set. He looked like a man being very careful about something.

She almost laughed.

"What are you doing?"

"Standing still."

"You are pressing yourself into the wall."

"There is very little wall."

"Is it so terrible?" She meant to be light. It came out softer. "I do not bite."

His eyes opened. In the dark, they looked black.

"It is not you," he said. It was barely a breath. "It is what I am. Where I come near a thing, it feels my weight. A leaf will wither. A flame will lean. I am accustomed to it, and I forget that people are not."

"I am not a leaf."

"No." His voice dropped lower. "You are a good deal more breakable, and a good deal more bright. And I have been very careful not to ruin you."

She stopped breathing.

It was the closest he had ever come to saying it, and the farthest he had ever stood from it. The words hung in the narrow dark. Neither of them moved. Above her head, a very small sound came from the ledge, like the tick of a crow's claw on stone. It was the only thing that told her the world had not stopped.

"I do not think I am as fragile as you imagine," she said.

"I do not imagine it. I know it." He closed his eyes again. "And for the sake of the three of us, I would be grateful if you stopped looking at me like that."

"Like what?"

"As if I were something that could be kept."

Footsteps sounded in the colonnade.

## IV

Ratnan came along the arches like a man walking through a dream. He held a small oil lamp low, so it lit only his feet. His shoulders were hunched. He stopped by the third pillar from the end and stood there a long time, listening.

Vaanathi held her breath. Sancharan did not move at all.

A second figure came out of the dark. It was a short, soft-bodied man in a dull brown wrap. His face was round and forgettable. He carried a leather bag in one hand and he moved very quietly, with a small stoop.

"Sundar," said Ratnan.

"The minister sends his regards." The voice was oily and pleasant. "He wishes to know how the day went."

"It went... as you would expect."

"The princess went to the physician."

"She went to the tower. First. Then to the physician."

"We know about the tower." The round man smiled. "She took something from there."

"A vial. A medicine. I do not know."

"And she had the ledgers out. For an hour."

"She did. She read the whole bundle." Ratnan's voice shook. "Please. I have told you what I know. You said it was only to protect her. You said the minister feared for her safety."

"And so he does."

"Then why do you need to know what she reads?"

"A guardian must know the dangers, my friend." Sundar set down the leather bag. It gave a small chink. "He knows that you act out of care. He has not forgotten your mother. The fees for her treatment have been settled for the next three months. He asked me to tell you."

Ratnan's face worked. For a long moment he said nothing.

"Thank you," he said, in a very small voice.

"Of course. And what did she say about the keeper's papers?"

"Nothing." He swallowed. "She said nothing."

It was a lie. Even Vaanathi could hear it. Sundar's smile did not change. He picked up the bag and put it into Ratnan's hands.

"Think about it tonight," he said. "Think about what is best for her. We will meet again in two days."

He drifted off into the dark, and the sound of his sandals faded.

Ratnan stood where he was. He stayed there until the lamp flame had steadied. The lamp in his hand trembled and sent small shaking shadows up the carved wall. Then he lowered himself slowly onto the stone floor, put the leather bag beside him, and covered his face with both hands.

His shoulders began to shake.

Vaanathi stepped out of the alcove.

## V

He heard her and looked up. His face was wet. In the lamplight his eyes were huge.

"Highness."

"Ratnan."

"I did not... I never meant..." He scrambled to his knees, then flat to the ground, his forehead against the stone. "Please, Highness. I will go. I will leave the palace. Please do not tell the emperor. My mother."

"Get up."

"I cannot."

"You can. You are not a criminal. You are a frightened man. Get up, and sit, and tell me the whole of it."

He got up. He sat on the edge of the nearest pillar base, his hands clasped between his knees. He told her. It came out in a halting rush. Nine years ago, when he was a boy of nineteen, the minister had put in a word for him at the archive. A year later, his mother had become ill. The healers had been costly. The minister had been generous. Once or twice a month, he had asked for small things. Who came to the archive. What Smaran had said. They had seemed harmless.

"He told me it was for the throne's sake," Ratnan said. "He told me Smaran was old and he worried. He said a good servant tells his master what he sees."

"And after Smaran died?"

"After, he asked about you." The young man's voice broke. "I thought... I thought he meant to protect you. I thought you were in danger. I said you were reading the ledgers. I said you asked about the tower. I swear, Highness, I thought it was all to help."

Vaanathi looked at him for a long time. His face had no cunning in it. It was only afraid.

Smaran's letter came back to her. *Trust him with small things only. The big things frighten him, and a frightened man is a poor guardian.*

"Do you know how Smaran died?" she asked.

"No." He shook his head fast. "No. I swear it. I found the bolt dropped. I thought it was age. I wanted to think it was age."

She believed him. It surprised her to find that she did.

"Then I will ask something of you," she said. "A small thing."

"Anything."

"You will go on meeting Sundar. You will tell him what I tell you to tell. Nothing more and nothing less."

His mouth fell open. "You want me to... to lie to the minister?"

"I want you to repeat what I say, and be afraid in the way you are naturally afraid. You do that very well." She heard the dry tone in her own voice and nearly smiled. "He will see nothing odd. He sees only what he expects."

She thought quickly. "Next time, tell him this. Tell him I am grieving and cannot be reasoned with. Tell him I sit in my rooms with the shutters closed and talk to nobody but my maid. Tell him the scholar is a dull old man who bores me, and that I am sorry I agreed to guide him."

"He is not a dull old man," said Ratnan, before he could stop himself. He glanced at Sancharan and went scarlet.

"I am afraid I am," said Sancharan gravely. "You may tell him I snore."

Ratnan made a small choked noise that was almost a laugh. It seemed to surprise him more than anyone.

Ratnan stared at her. Slowly, a small crooked relief crept over his face.

"I can do that," he said. "I think I can do that."

"Good."

"Highness..." He hesitated. "There is one more thing. I did not tell Sundar. I do not know why."

"Tell me."

"Smaran used to go out in the evenings. Alone. Through the west gate." He rubbed his face with a sleeve. "He said it was for his knees. He said the walk helped. But he came back with red mud on his sandals, thick red mud. I scrubbed them myself. Once a month, near the dark of the moon, for the last year."

The words fell like a stone into still water.

She did not look round. She did not need to. She heard Sancharan step out of the alcove behind her, very quietly, and stop.

"Which way did he walk?" the tall man asked.

"Down the west road, sir. Past the old stepwell. I thought he went to feed the fish."

"What fish?" said Vaanathi.

"There are none," said Ratnan helplessly. "That is what I kept thinking. There are no fish in that well. There never have been."

Overhead, on the ledge, Iruli unfolded herself from her knot and turned her face towards the west.

Later, when Ratnan had gone to bed with his lamp and his leather bag, the two of them stood alone at the end of the colonnade. The rain had stopped. The stones gave off a cool, wet smell.

"I am sorry about what I said in there," Vaanathi said. "About biting."

"You said nothing wrong."

"I only meant..."

"I know what you meant." He was looking at the dark arches, not at her. "It was the kindest thing anyone has said to me in some time. That is why I would rather not discuss it."

She waited for him to say more. He did not. After a moment he turned and set off along the colonnade. She followed, a full pace behind, and saw that he kept his hands tightly clasped at his back the whole way.

Neither of them said the word aloud. But both of them were already thinking of the road that ran down past the west gate, and of a well with no fish in it, waiting in the dark.
