## I

Her father received her at dusk, in his private room above the council hall. It was a small, bare room. It held a low bed, a table, a lamp, and a window that looked out over the river. He was standing at the window when she came in, with his back to her. He did not turn.

"Tell me," said Anantaraja.

She told him. She did not tell him everything. She told him that the scholar had asked to see the river, and that the river was moving wrongly. She told him about the tower, and the old astronomer, and the carved wheel. She did not mention the vial or the letters. She did not mention Ratnan. She was surprised by how easily the omissions came.

He listened without moving. When she finished, he asked only one question.

"The wall in the tower. What did the carving look like?"

"A wheel, Father. A circle with spokes. One piece cut away at the bottom."

His shoulders did not stir. But the silence in the room changed. It grew heavier, and she felt it settle on her like a cloth.

"I see," he said at last.

"Do you know what it means?"

"I know a great many things that do not help me." He turned. His face, in the last of the light, looked older than she remembered. "Vaanathi. You asked me once why I never let you speak in court."

"I did not ask. I only wondered."

"You wondered very loudly." The corner of his mouth moved. It was not quite a smile. "I will tell you the reason one day. Not today. Today I will give you this."

He took something from the table and held it out. It was a small bronze disc, no wider than her palm, stamped with the sunburst and the crown. A cord ran through a hole at the top.

"Leave from any gate, at any hour. The guards will not stop you." He closed her fingers over it with his own. His hand was warm and hard. "I ask only one thing. If you go down into any place, do not go down further than you can climb."

"Where would I go down?"

"I do not know. It was only advice."

"It was very specific advice."

"I am a very specific man." He released her hand and turned back to the window. "Go. Take the scholar. Come back."

She stood a moment, with the disc cold in her fist. The lamp on the table threw her shadow long against the wall, and it looked, for a moment, like the shadow of someone much older. She wanted to ask what he knew. She wanted it so badly that her teeth ached. But his back was turned, and his shoulders were straight, and she understood that the door had been opened as far as it would go tonight.

"Yes, Father," she said, and left.

## II

They left by the west gate an hour after midnight.

The gate guard was a stout young man named Pazhani, who knew the princess by sight and the scholar by reputation. He raised his eyebrows when he saw them. He raised them higher when he saw the crow.

"The emperor's leave," said Vaanathi, and held up the small bronze token her father had given her that afternoon.

"Highness, it is very late."

"I am aware."

"And the road is not safe."

"We will not go far."

He looked at the token, and at her, and at the tall man standing behind her with his hands at his sides. Then he sighed. It was the sigh of a man who had been told to let things happen and was doing so against his instinct.

"There is a lantern on the hook," he said. "Take it. And mind the ditch at the third bend. It floods."

"Thank you, Pazhani."

"Come back," he said, rather helplessly. "That is all I ask."

The road beyond the gate was dark. It ran downhill between hedges of thorn and a few stunted neem trees. The night was warm and very still. No wind moved. The stars were out, and there were many of them, but they were far apart and cold, as stars are when there is no moon.

It was the dark of the moon in all but name. Eleven days off, by the old astronomer's count. But the sky was already thin.

They walked without talking. Sancharan carried the lantern. Iruli flew ahead and came back and flew ahead again, a small piece of night cut loose. Vaanathi counted the bends. At the third, she stepped over the ditch. The water in it was moving the wrong way, uphill, in a slow dark slide, and she decided not to look.

"You saw it," said Sancharan.

"I decided not to."

"That is a sensible decision."

"I make them when I can."

The stepwell stood at the bottom of the slope. She had passed it on festival days, with crowds and drums and women selling jasmine. At night it was something else. It was a long square hole in the ground, lined with stone. Around its mouth ran a low wall, black and wet. Beyond the wall, there was nothing at all. The dark went straight down.

"It is deeper than I remembered," she said.

"It always is."

"Have you been here?"

"No." He raised the lantern. The flame lay flat, bent towards the water below. "But I know what it is. And I know what it holds."

## III

The steps began at the south side. They went down in broad flights, nine levels in all, each ending in a stone landing with a pillared gallery on three sides. The pillars were thick and square, carved with the faded shapes of animals. On every landing, the air was a little colder.

She went down first. He followed one landing behind, as he had in the tower. Iruli rode on his shoulder, silent, and her yellow eye caught the light.

At the second landing, the sounds of the night went out. There were no frogs. There were no insects. Only the soft scrape of sandals on stone.

At the fourth, the air became damp. A faint mineral smell rose from below, like wet iron. The steps were worn in the middle and slick with a green film. She went carefully, her hand on the wall.

At the fifth, her lantern burned lower. The flame shrank to a thumb's length and held. She raised it. The light fell on the wall beside her and she saw something she had missed.

Scratches. They were small and neat, in a column, cut into the stone at the height of a man's hip. Nine in a row. Beneath them, nine more. And beneath those, nine more. She counted three columns and stopped.

"Smaran," she said.

"Yes."

"He was counting." She ran a finger over the nearest mark. It was shallow and clean, a few months old at most. "Every dark moon. He came down and made a mark."

"Of what?"

"I do not know." She turned. "Do you?"

"I think so. Not yet." He was standing on the step above, one hand on the wall, and his face had gone strangely stiff. He was breathing slowly and carefully, as someone does when carrying something fragile up a flight of stairs. "Go on. I will follow."

At the sixth landing, the lantern would not stay lit. It guttered and shrank and went out. She stood in the dark. A moment later, a faint blue-grey glow rose from below, from the ninth tier far beneath her feet. It was just enough to show the shapes of the pillars.

She looked back.

Sancharan had stopped at the top of the sixth flight. He stood with one foot on the step below him and the other on the landing above. His face was grey in the strange light. Sweat stood on his forehead. His left hand was flat against the air in front of him, as if there were a wall there.

"Sancharan?"

"I cannot go lower."

"What do you mean?"

"There is a ward." His voice was level, but it cost him. "It is old. It was made by people like me, to keep out people like me. I can feel it, here." He pressed his palm against nothing. "It is very well made."

"Can you break it?"

"I could." He paused. "It would be noticed."

"By whom?"

"By everyone it was made to keep out." He drew a breath. "Go back, Vaanathi."

It was the first time he had said her name. It stopped her where she stood. She had been called Highness and Princess a thousand times. It had never sounded like that, quiet and plain, as if it were a stone he had carried a long way and was setting down.

"Why can I pass?" she asked.

"Because it was not made to stop you."

"Who was it made for?"

"For your house." He closed his eyes briefly. "Your ancestors made a promise at this place. The ward knows the blood that made it. It will let you through. It will not let me."

"What promise?"

"I do not know the whole of it. I was not here when it was made." That was not quite true. She heard it in the pause. She let it go. "Go back, Vaanathi. Please."

"You said please."

"I have been known to."

"Smaran came here every month." She was surprised how steady her voice was. "He came alone. He made a mark on the wall. He wrote three letters and did not finish the fourth. If I turn round, I will never know what he was counting."

"And if you go on, you may not come back."

"Will I be stopped?"

"I do not know."

"Will the stairs fall down?"

"I do not think so."

"Then I will see what is at the bottom." She stepped back up one stair, close enough to see the lines in his face. "You are afraid."

"Yes."

"For me?"

"Yes." He said it without any attempt to soften it. "And for a reason I cannot tell you. If you go and I cannot follow, it is the one thing I have not made provision for."

For a moment she thought he would put out his hand. His fingers flexed at his side. Then he closed them, slowly, and let his arm fall.

"Take Iruli," he said.

The crow had already left his shoulder. She came down through the dark in a long glide and landed on Vaanathi's with a small hard grip. She did not seem pleased about it. She did not seem unwilling either. She put her head on one side and fixed her yellow eye on the stairs ahead.

"She will not speak," said Sancharan. "But she will not let you fall."

"That is a great deal to ask of a crow."

"She has asked it of herself."

Vaanathi turned and went down.

## IV

The seventh landing was empty. The eighth was empty, too, except for a thin skin of water on the floor that did not ripple when she walked through it. By the time she reached the head of the last flight, the glow from below was bright enough to read by.

It was a blue-white light, cold and steady, and it came from the stone.

The ninth landing was a square platform at the bottom of the well. On three sides, the walls rose straight and bare into the dark. On the fourth side lay a pool of black still water. It was perfectly flat. It reflected nothing.

At the edge of the pool stood a stone.

It was grey and rough, about as tall as her waist. It had been squared off by hand in some earlier age, and its top was worn smooth. In its face, cut deep, was the wheel. She knew it before she saw it. The circle, the hub, nine spokes, and a wedge gone at the bottom.

The light was coming out of the wedge.

She stood a long time looking at it. Iruli's claws tightened and eased on her shoulder. Nothing in her upbringing had prepared her for a stone that glowed from inside a missing piece of itself. She felt as though she had walked into a sentence that had been waiting a very long time for its last word.

The face of the stone was marked in other ways. Someone had scraped it. Long pale grooves ran across it, each one the width of a finger, some fresh and bright, some dull with age. A heap of grey grains lay at its foot, as fine as ground millet. She knelt and picked up a pinch. It was gritty, dry, and curiously cold. It did not feel like dust. It felt like something that had once been burning and had forgotten how.

Beside the stone, on the floor, lay a small clay lamp. It was cold and dry and quite empty. It had been placed there neatly, in the exact centre of a square of cleaner stone. She knew at once whose it was. Smaran had never let a lamp run dry in his life. This one had been left behind on purpose, a signal that said, *I was here, and I meant to return.*

She folded it into a corner of the red cloth that held Smaran's letters.

*Grey ash,* she thought, though she did not know why. *Ash that never came from a fire.*

Then she heard it.

It was not a sound she could have named. It was somewhere between a breath and a note, very low. It seemed to come from the stone, and from the water, and from behind her own breastbone, all at once. She turned.

There was a door.

It stood on the far side of the pool, at the edge of the platform where there should have been a wall. It was a plain rectangle of dark wood, as tall as a man and half as wide, with iron bands across it and a ring in the middle. It had no frame. It had no wall around it. It stood by itself in the open air, with the dark well-shaft rising behind it, like a door that someone had carried down and set on its end.

She walked round the pool. Her sandals made no sound on the stone.

Up close the door was old. The wood was split with age. The ring was worn bright where hands had gripped it. She put her fingers on the surface and found it warm, with a faint pulse in it, slow and regular, like the beat of a sleeping animal.

*Smaran, you old fox,* she thought. *You knew.*

Above her, so faint that she almost missed it, a voice was calling her name.

"Vaanathi."

It was Sancharan, far off, from the sixth landing. He sounded strained, like a man speaking through his teeth.

She took hold of the iron ring.

It would not turn. It was cold now, the warmth gone, and as stubborn as a shut mouth. She pulled harder. The edge of the iron band bit her palm. She hissed and let go and looked at her hand. A thin line of red crossed the skin. A drop swelled on the end of her thumb, fell, and landed on the ring.

The metal drank it.

There was no other word. The blood vanished into the iron as water vanishes into dry cloth. The ring gave one small hard click.

The door swung inward by itself. There was no creak. There was only a slow, patient opening, as if it had been rehearsed for ages and was glad, at last, to be needed.

## V

Behind the door was a violet sky.

It was not like any sky she had known. It was deep and slow and full of colour. It moved like water folding over itself, rich and dark, holding every shade between the blue of a bruise and the red of a coal. Small points of light hung in it, quite still, in patterns she could not read. They were not scattered. They were set.

A smell came through the door. It was clean and cold, like the air at the top of a mountain.

Vaanathi stood very still.

*I should go back,* she thought. It was a reasonable thought. She held it up and examined it. It was the thought of a sensible person, and she had been a sensible person all her life.

Above her, Sancharan's voice rose again. It was louder now. It had a new note in it, harsh and raw.

"Vaanathi. Do not go through."

She looked up the shaft. She could not see him. She could only see the dark and the faint pale shape of the steps. A single thin shaft of grey light fell from far above, from the sky of the human world, and it lay across the water like a blade.

"I will be back before you finish shouting," she called.

"That is not funny."

"It is a little funny."

"Vaanathi..."

She turned and stepped through.

The door shut behind her. It did not slam. It closed with a soft sound, like a long breath let go, and the cold blue light of the well went out as though it had never been.

Far above, on the sixth landing, Sancharan stood with both hands flat against the air. It had begun to crack. It made a sound like ice on a river in spring, long and thin and rising. Fine lines ran out from under his palms in every direction, silver and glittering. Somewhere beyond the door a crow cried out once, thin and far away. It was the cry of a creature that had been told to keep one person safe and was doing the only thing left to her, which was to go with her.

He had not been forbidden a door in an age of the world.

He leaned in, and the ward began to break.
