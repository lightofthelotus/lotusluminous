## I

The oil bath had left Vaanathi's hair heavy and wet down her back. Nila knelt behind her on the low stool. The small brass vessel of sambrani, the fragrant resin the whole south used for smoking hair and cloth alike, was already glowing between them on its stand.

She dropped a fresh crystal of the resin onto the coals. Sweet, dark smoke rose in a slow curling column. The whole room filled with a scent Vaanathi had known since before she could properly remember knowing anything at all.

"Hold still," Nila said. She had said it a thousand mornings before. "You always tip your head the moment I have the section arranged."

"I am holding still."

"You are thinking about holding still. That is not the same thing."

Nila lifted a length of hair between the two curved prongs of the hair partition tool. Its proper name was kuzhal kothi, though no one in the palacehold had called it that in years. The old brass tool was worn smooth and dark with generations of exactly this use. It was thin as a tuning fork and just as precise. She held the section out over the rising smoke. She turned it slowly, patiently, letting the sambrani work its way through each strand rather than simply drying it. It was slower than sitting in the sun. It was also, Vaanathi had always privately thought, the only part of her morning that asked nothing of her at all.

"You have that look again," Nila said, watching the smoke climb. "The one where you are thinking about something a hundred years older than whatever is actually troubling you."

"I keep thinking of something Smaran told me once. About why this city is called Anantapuram." Vaanathi's eyes stayed closed, following the thought rather than the smoke. "Most people assume it is only flattery. A dynasty naming itself grand things, the way dynasties do."

"Is it not?"

"Not entirely, or so he claimed." Vaanathi's voice settled into the particular careful cadence she used for old lessons, half memory and half performance, the way a good teaching survives its teacher by being repeated in exactly the shape it was first given. "Before there was a throne here at all, this ground was already old. Older than any single ruler's claim to it. The first settlers who found this place did not build here because the land was fertile, though it is, or because the river ran close, though it does. They built here because the sky above this exact ground held still in a way it did not hold still anywhere else they had tried."

"Held still."

![Sambrani Morning](09-01-sambrani-morning.jpg)

"That is the old word for it. Smaran never fully explained what it meant, only that it was the word that survived, generation after generation, when so much else about that founding was lost or smoothed over." Vaanathi opened her eyes, watching the smoke curl and vanish toward the ceiling. "An empire rises. An empire falls. But something about this particular ground, he told me once, does not rise and fall the way empires do. It simply holds. I found that a stranger comfort than it probably ought to be, coming from a man who spent his whole life surrounded by records of exactly how many empires had already fallen."

"You say that as though it comforts you even now."

"It does," Vaanathi said simply. "Some things are worth being exactly this small. Not everything has to be large to matter. I believe that was rather his whole philosophy of record-keeping, if I am honest."

Nila's hands did not pause in their work. "Tell me more. About the old founding. I do not believe I have heard that version before, and I have lived in this palace nearly as long as you have."

"There is not a great deal more to tell, honestly. Smaran said most of it was never written down at all. It was only ever spoken, teacher to student, the way this hair-drying is spoken and shown rather than written." Vaanathi considered a moment, choosing her next words with the same visible care Smaran himself used to bring to anything he judged worth saying properly. "There is one thing more, though. He told me the old astronomers, before they were an office anyone thought worth funding, were not simply counting stars for the pleasure of it. They were watching for something specific. He never told me what. He said only that whatever they watched for, they never once stopped watching for it, generation after generation, long after the throne itself had forgotten why the watching had ever mattered."

"That sounds like rather a heavy thing to teach a young princess."

"Most old lessons are heavier than they first appear," Vaanathi said. "That is usually how you know they were worth teaching at all, rather than merely dressed up to sound that way."

Vaanathi was quiet a moment, turning that over the way she turned over most things now. "I do not know where Sancharan has gone," she said at last, into the quiet. "He was here when I fell asleep. He was not here when I woke. He left only a note."

"What did the note say?"

"That he would try to return soon. That he had gone somewhere he could not yet explain." Vaanathi opened her eyes, watching the smoke curl and vanish toward the ceiling. "I have grown rather tired of notes that explain nothing, Nila. I had hoped that particular habit of his had ended along with the rest of his hiding."

"Perhaps some hiding takes longer to finish than a single confession allows for." Nila lifted another section of hair, turning it with the same unhurried care. "You have known him, properly known him, for what. A season. Whatever this is he has gone to do, I doubt it is the kind of thing a man learns to share easily, even with someone he has decided to stop hiding from."

"That is very generous of you, on his behalf."

"I am not being generous to him," Nila said. "I am being honest with you. There is a difference, and I would rather you heard the honest version from me than the generous one from anyone else in this palace." She set the kuzhal kothi down at last, the final section dry and fragrant, and rested her hands lightly on Vaanathi's shoulders. "He will come back. I do not say that to comfort you emptily. I say it because I have watched the way that man looks at you. Men who look at someone the way he looks at you do not simply leave."

Vaanathi reached up and pressed her own hand briefly over Nila's. She said nothing more. There was nothing more that needed saying, and some kinds of comfort were better received in silence than answered aloud.

***

She found the note again later, sitting alone in her own chambers once Nila had gone. She read it a third time, as though a third reading might yield something the first two had not.

"I have gone somewhere I cannot yet bring you, though I promise you a full account when I return. There are others like me, Vaanathi, and something has happened that I must discuss with them plainly, before it becomes something I can no longer discuss at all. I will try to return soon. Trust me a little longer."

"Sancharan." And beneath it, smaller, as though added after some hesitation, "Shani."

She folded the note carefully. She set it beneath the small clay lamp on her table, where she could see it without needing to unfold it again. She told herself, without entirely believing it, that a little longer was a distance she could bear.

## II

The chamber Sancharan called them to was not the great hall where he had first carried Vaanathi. Nor was it the small close room where he had tended her afterward. It was a third space within Anaadikottai, his own dwelling beyond the mortal sky, one he rarely had cause to use. It was a wide circular chamber ringed with seats worn into the stone by a use far older than the fortress's own memory of itself.

Five others were already there when he arrived. Each was haloed faintly by a light that belonged to no single colour. The low murmur of their conversation stilled as he entered.

"You called us," said a voice bright and warm as a struck bell. Surya rose from his seat with the particular restless energy of someone who found stillness, even here, faintly beneath him. "That alone is worth remarking on, Sancharan. I do not believe you have called a gathering in longer than most of these mortals' whole dynasties have stood."

"I would not have, if I had a choice in the matter."

"Ominous," said a second voice, quick and clever. It belonged to a slight figure who had not stopped writing something invisible in the air with one finger since Sancharan entered. Budha did not look up. "You have gone quiet and grave, which on you looks less like wisdom and more like a man suffering a particularly bad transit. Out with it."

"Budha."

"I am simply observing." Budha finally looked up, eyes sharp and delighted. "Observation is my entire purpose, as you well know. I cannot help it if what I observe is occasionally unflattering."

A third figure sat with the patient stillness of someone who had never once in his existence been in a hurry. He inclined his head. "Let the man speak before you finish dissecting him, Budha." Guru's voice carried the particular warmth of someone who had spent an eternity being asked for counsel and had never once resented the asking. "Shani. Or perhaps Sancharan, since that is evidently the name you have grown fondest of wearing. Whatever troubles you, it troubles the rest of us equally, the moment you decide to share it."

"Lohitan tells me some of you already suspect part of it," Sancharan said. He glanced toward the low bench where Lohitan sat with his arms crossed, wearing an expression of studied, insufferable patience. He realised, too late, that this had been precisely the wrong opening to choose.

"Lohitan tells everyone everything eventually," said a fifth voice, low and rich. It belonged to a figure seated slightly apart from the others, jewelled and unhurried in a way that made the whole chamber feel, briefly, more beautiful for her simply being in it. "It is the one flaw in an otherwise excellent temperament." Shukra smiled, and the smile carried a warmth that made even Budha's restless finger still for a moment. "Though I confess, old friend, I am considerably more curious about the rumour reaching me from his direction than about whatever grave business brought you here to share. Something about a mortal."

Lohitan, to his credit, did not so much as blink. "I merely mentioned that our friend here has developed an unusual attachment to a particular princess. I did not embellish. I rarely need to."

"That is not why I called you," Sancharan said, trying to end it there.

"No," Lohitan agreed, entirely unrepentant, "but it is considerably more entertaining than whatever grim news you are about to deliver. Permit an old friend one moment's amusement before you ruin the evening for everyone." He looked around the circle with evident relish. "You should have seen him, holding her hand across two horses like a boy who had never once considered that a saddle might be a more practical place to keep both hands occupied."

![Council of Nine](09-02-council-of-nine.jpg)

"I did see something of the sort," Surya said, delighted. "I see rather a lot, being where I am. I confess I did not expect it of you, Sancharan. You have spent centuries being the one among us least susceptible to exactly this particular foolishness."

"If we are quite finished," Sancharan said, with the specific patience of a man who had known these people considerably too long to expect otherwise, "I would like to discuss the reason I actually asked you here."

"Oh, we are never quite finished," Budha said cheerfully, "but go on. I promise to only interrupt if you say something genuinely stupid."

Sancharan told them. The wedge cut into the ring, identical at every anchor he and Vaanathi had found. The scar on Lohitan's own stone, fresh enough to still smell of the hand that made it. And the growing, specific dread that had settled into him since the night he was forced to bend Anaadi's own patience around a mortal life. For the first time in longer than he could comfortably measure, the fortress had answered him with something that felt less like generosity and more like warning.

"I do not yet know what it would mean to see that wedge cut all the way through," he said finally. "I have theories I do not care for. That it may signify a point where an anchor's binding could be severed, rather than merely tested. If I am right, and if whoever is testing them learns to complete what they are only, for now, still practising, then we are no longer discussing a court's private grief, or one family's old grievance. We are discussing whether Bhumi survives the losing of one of us."

The chamber went very quiet.

"You are certain of this," Guru said. All trace of his earlier warmth had gone still and grave.

"I am certain of nothing," Sancharan said. "That is precisely what troubles me. I have watched hands more careful than mine spend centuries testing far smaller thresholds than this one. I do not know, with any real confidence, how close whoever is doing this has already come to finding what they are looking for."

"Then it is fortunate," Shukra said quietly, "that you at least know now to be afraid of the correct thing."

## III

They spoke a long while after that, in the particular unhurried way that beings who did not truly keep mortal time could afford to speak. Sancharan learned, across that long conversation, considerably more than he had arrived already knowing.

He learned that Budha's own anchor, hidden somewhere among the busiest trade roads south of the empire, had shown no sign of tampering at all. This troubled Budha rather more than reassurance ought to have, given how carefully everyone else's had been tested. He learned that Guru's anchor had likewise gone untouched. It was kept in the quiet company of scholars who had never once questioned why their oldest library never seemed to age or wither, how many ever years and wars passed through the city around it. Shukra's anchor had also shown nothing. It was hidden in plain sight within a garden so beautiful that no one had ever thought to look past its beauty for anything else.

Only Sancharan's own, and Lohitan's, had been disturbed.

"That is not comforting," Sancharan said.

"No," Guru agreed. "It suggests a hand working with some particular order in mind, rather than simple opportunism. Whoever tests these anchors began with the two oldest griefs among us. Yours, and Lohitan's."

"Which asks a question none of us have said aloud yet," Budha put in, quieter now than his earlier restlessness had allowed. "Whether the hand doing the testing is working toward something, or working *away* from something. Toward theft, or away from a fear none of the rest of us have been told about yet."

Sancharan thought of Viyamban's palm-leaf record. He thought of eight seals a lineage had once been sworn to protect. He thought of a man who had spent decades understanding fear far more intimately than Sancharan had ever permitted himself to. He said none of it aloud. It was not, he judged, a story that belonged to anyone but himself and Vaanathi yet. He had learned, across this strange season, to be considerably more careful about which truths he handed to a room before he understood them fully himself.

"I do not have an answer for that tonight," he said instead. "Only the wedge, and the fear, and the certainty that I would rather bring you all this warning now than discover too late that I should have."

"Two are missing tonight," Surya observed, glancing around the circle. "Beyond the ones who never properly attend these gatherings at all."

"The Moon has its own reasons for staying elsewhere tonight," Sancharan said. "I did not think it wise to press the matter, given everything."

"It rarely leaves its tides for long, in any case," Guru said, mild and unconcerned. "It has always been that particular discipline. I would not read anything into the absence beyond the ordinary shape of its nature."

"And the twins," Shukra added, "are, as ever, otherwise occupied. One cannot summon a shadow to a gathering of light, however politely one asks. Rahu and Ketu keep their own hours, and their own company, and have done since before any of us thought to expect otherwise of them."

"They are not true worlds, after all," Budha said, almost gently, for him. "Only the places where two paths happen to cross. One can only wait for the crossing to happen again."

Sancharan absorbed that in silence. He filed it away beside everything else this long night had handed him. Rising at last to take his leave of the five of them, he found that he was considerably more frightened, and considerably less alone in that fear, than he had been when he arrived.

## IV

Vaanathi walked the western gardens alone that evening, restless in a way the sambrani smoke had not managed to fully settle. She rounded the low hedge near the old fountain without any particular thought for who else might have chosen that same quiet path at that same quiet hour.

The old man saw her first.

She would think, turning the moment over for days afterward, that she had never in her life seen a face do quite what his face did in that first instant. He went the colour of old ash. His whole body went still in a way that had nothing to do with dignity. It had everything to do with a man who believed, for one entire suspended breath, that he was looking at something the world should not have permitted him to see.

"You," he said. The word came out of him broken, barely a word at all. It was more the sound a man makes when a wound he thought long closed tears open without warning.

"Forgive me," Vaanathi said, startled by the intensity of it, taking an instinctive step back. "I do not believe we have been introduced."

He did not answer. He stood frozen at the fountain's edge, staring at her with an expression she had no ready word for. It held equal parts horror and a terrible, aching relief, as though her simply standing there, breathing, undamaged, was a fact his own eyes were struggling to accept as real.

![Viyamban Recognises Vaanathi](09-03-viyamban-recognition.jpg)

"Sir," Vaanathi said again, more carefully now. The particular caution she had learned across this whole strange season rose in her without being asked. "Are you unwell? Shall I call for someone?"

"No." The word came out steadier this time, though his hands, she noticed, had not stopped their faint trembling. "No, Your Highness. Forgive me. I mistook you, for a moment, for someone else entirely. An old man's eyes, playing an old man's tricks." He bowed, too quickly, too deeply for the courtesy to feel entirely genuine. He turned before she could ask him anything further. "I will trouble you no longer."

He walked away without looking back. His pace carried the particular controlled haste of a man forcing himself not to run. Vaanathi stood alone at the fountain's edge a long while afterward. The evening had gone suddenly colder around her than the hour alone could account for.

She did not know his name. She had not thought, in the strangeness of the moment, to ask it. Something in the set of his retreating shoulders had told her plainly enough that he would not have given it if she had.

But she knew, standing there with the fountain's quiet murmur the only sound left in the garden, that she had just watched a stranger look at her the way one looks at a ghost. Whatever had put that look on his face was not something an old man's tricks alone could account for.

She thought, unbidden, of a torn page in an old genealogical volume. She thought of a mark she had first found pressed into wax on an archive door. She thought of every question this season had handed her that still did not have an answer.

"Whoever he was," she thought, walking back toward the palace's lit windows with rather more haste than dignity strictly allowed, "he knew exactly who I was. And whatever he thought he was looking at, when he looked at me, it frightened him considerably more than it should have frightened anyone meeting a princess for the first time."

## V

She did not go to her own chambers. She went, instead, still half-running, to the narrow stair above the scribes' hall. A single lamp still burned behind Ratnan's shuttered window, at an hour when any sensible under-steward ought to have long since been asleep.

He answered the door with the particular startled swiftness of a man who had not entirely stopped expecting bad news to arrive at odd hours. His alarm only deepened at the sight of her standing there alone, unescorted, her hair still faintly touched with the scent of sambrani smoke.

![At Ratnan's Quarters](09-04-ratnan-quarters.jpg)

"Your Highness. Has something happened?"

"I do not entirely know," Vaanathi admitted. She found, saying it aloud, that the admission cost her more than she had expected. "I met a man in the western gardens tonight. An old man. I do not know his name. I do not believe I have ever seen him before in my life. But he looked at me, Ratnan, as though he had seen a ghost. As though my simply standing there, breathing, was a thing his own eyes could not quite forgive themselves for witnessing."

Ratnan's face, already pale in the lamplight, went a shade paler still. He gestured her toward the room's single stool, the same courtesy he had offered her and Sancharan once before. He busied his trembling hands with lighting a second lamp before he trusted himself to speak again.

"Describe him to me. Everything you can remember. I have learned, these past weeks, that the smallest detail is often the one that matters most, however foolish it feels to mention at the time."

She did, as precisely as memory allowed. The ash-grey pallor. The trembling hands he had tried and failed to still. The particular controlled haste of his retreat. The way he had bowed too quickly and too deeply for the courtesy to feel like anything but flight dressed up as manners. She described the way his voice had broken on a single word, barely a word at all, more the sound a man makes when an old wound tears open without warning. She described, too, the strange doubled thing in his face. The horror sat so close beside the relief that she had not, in the moment, been able to tell where one ended and the other began.

Ratnan was quiet a long moment. His hands twisted together in the anxious way she remembered from the night they had first come to question him. When he finally spoke, his voice had gone careful in a manner that told her, before the words themselves arrived, that he was choosing them with unusual precision.

"It could be a great many men, Your Highness. This palace holds no shortage of old men with old fears."

"But you thought of someone specific. I watched you think of him, just now."

"I thought," Ratnan said slowly, "of the man who called on me, after Smaran's death. The one who claimed friendship with my father, though I have never once been able to confirm it. I have grown, in the weeks since, considerably less inclined to trust the claim than I was the evening I first heard it." He hesitated, some old caution warring visibly with whatever instinct had brought the thought to his lips at all. "I could not tell you it was the same man. I never saw your stranger tonight, only heard him described secondhand, from your own frightened accounting of him, which is a poor foundation to build a certainty on. But something in what you describe sits uncomfortably close to something I have not been able to stop thinking about myself, these past weeks."

"What is his name? The man who visited you?"

Ratnan told her. The name meant, in that moment, precisely as little to Vaanathi as it had meant the first time she had heard it spoken aloud in this same small room, sometime and a lifetime ago. It was an old, unremarkable name, belonging, as far as she knew, to an old, unremarkable man the court had long since stopped troubling itself to remember.

"I do not think that is who I met tonight," she said. She found, even saying it, that she could not have told anyone precisely why she believed that, beyond a stubborn, unexamined instinct that refused to let the two men sit easily together in her mind as the same person. "But I confess I no longer entirely trust my own certainty about very much, this particular season."

"Nor do I, Your Highness," Ratnan said quietly. "I have found that certainty is rather a harder thing to keep hold of, since Smaran died, than I ever imagined it would be. I used to think a fact was simply a fact, once you had confirmed it carefully enough. I no longer believe that. I think, now, that a fact is only ever as steady as the last hour in which no one troubled to test it. This palace has grown considerably too fond, of late, of testing things quietly, in the dark, where no one is watching closely enough to notice the shape of what has changed."

They sat with that a while, the small room's single lamp burning low between them. Vaanathi found, walking back through the sleeping palace some while later, that she had gained no answer worth the name for her trouble. Only a second uneasy shape to set beside the first. And the growing, unshakeable sense that this palace was keeping considerably more secrets from her than she had any hope of counting on two hands.

As she passed through the narrow alcove, her memories came rushing back. How close he had held her there. How he had shielded her, his breath warm and quiet against her ear. How much she longed, tonight, simply to see that tired face again. How much she wished she could go back to that one suspended moment, held so close against him. She remembered his heart beating so near her own that night, close enough to feel. She remembered his arms around her, as though she were the single most precious thing in his entire universe. She had wanted him even then, before she had known what he truly was. Knowing it now only made the wanting harder to carry.

![Alcove Memory](09-05-alcove-memory.jpg)

She thought of Sancharan, wherever he had gone, discussing whatever grave thing he could not yet bring himself to discuss with her. She thought of the old man's ash-grey face, and of Ratnan's careful, unfinished sentences. She missed Smaran too. He had always been the one person she could turn to when a question rose in her. He either had an answer himself, or knew exactly which record would.

She thought of that name that meant nothing and everything at once, depending entirely on which hour of this long, strange season she happened to be standing in when she tried to weigh it. She thought, too, of how strange this season, which had begun with a single missing palm leaf, had grown month by month into a maze that no one could see or explain. Herself included.

Whatever answer waited at the end of all these unfinished threads, she understood, climbing at last toward her own dark, waiting chambers, royal in every ordinary way that offered her no comfort tonight, it was not an answer this night intended to hand her freely. It would have to be earned, the way every other answer this strange season had first demanded to be earned. One uneasy, unfinished conversation at a time.

## VI

Viyamban had spent three days waiting for a death that never came.

He had expected word by the first evening. A physician summoned in the night. A quiet, careful silence spreading through the palacehold, the particular hush that settles over a household before anyone has agreed to say the word aloud. He had heard nothing of the kind. No summons. No hush. Only the palace's ordinary noise, unbroken, as though nothing at all had happened within its walls that week.

He had told himself this meant she was resting. Kept from public view, perhaps, while the court decided how much of the truth it wished to manage. He had sent quiet questions through the few channels still open to him, an old steward here, a kitchen girl there, none of them close enough to the truth to be dangerous, all of them close enough to notice whether a princess had been carried to the royal infirmary, or to somewhere quieter still. He had learned nothing. Only silence, and the silence had begun, by the second day, to trouble him more than bad news would have.

He had told himself, walking the gardens tonight for no reason he could have named honestly, that he did not expect to find anything there either.

He had not expected to find her.

Seeing her whole, breathing, standing in that garden with the particular stubborn brightness he remembered so well from the one time he had watched her argue with a minister across a crowded hall, had undone something in him he had not realised was still capable of coming undone. He had known, of course, that she could not have died quietly. A death like that does not stay contained, not for a princess, not in a court this size. He had known it with his mind for two full days. His body had not believed it until his own eyes gave it no further room for doubt.

He did not understand how she had survived.

The preparation had been careful. He had made it himself, the way he had made the leaf that took Smaran, drawing on knowledge his family had kept alive in secret for longer than the throne above them had kept anything alive at all. It did not fail. It had never once failed him, in all the careful years he had spent learning exactly how it worked, and exactly how thoroughly it left no trace of its working behind.

And yet she stood there. Breathing. Frightened of him, he had seen that plainly enough in the last moment before he fled her, but frightened the ordinary way a woman is frightened of a stranger's strange behaviour, not the way a body is frightened when it has narrowly escaped what his preparation should have made inescapable.

Something had intervened. He did not yet know what. He thought, walking the long corridor back toward his own quiet rooms, of the scholar who had arrived the same season the killing began, whose name he had heard only in passing, whose face he had never once troubled himself to study closely, because a wandering scholar had seemed, at the time, so entirely beneath his notice. He wondered, for the first time, whether that had been a mistake.

He was old enough to know that when the impossible happens once, it is rarely simple luck. It is far more often a hand no one has yet learned to look for.

***

He thought, walking, of his father.

Not the frail old man of his final winter, voice gone thin as old cloth, though that memory came too, uninvited, as it always did. He thought instead of an earlier evening, decades gone now, when his father had first shown him the palm-leaf record kept safe beneath a false floor for longer than either of them had been alive to witness its keeping.

*Eight, you will learn to find,* his father had told him, tracing the old inscription with a finger grown crooked by then, though his voice had held perfectly steady. *The ninth you will never need to search for. It has never left where it was first set, and it will not, so long as the one bound to it still walks beneath this same turning sky. But there is a danger besides, my son, older than the anchors themselves, and it is the one our family was truly sworn to watch for. A bloodline. One capable, in the fullness of time, of unmaking what none of us are meant to unmake. I do not know its face. I know only that when it comes, it will not announce itself as a danger. It will announce itself as someone entirely ordinary, entirely beloved, doing nothing more sinister than living the life it was born into.*

*What must I do, when I find it?*

![Viyamban Father Memory](09-06-viyamban-father-memory.jpg)

*Whatever the finding requires,* his father had said, and there had been no cruelty in the saying of it, only a terrible, aching tiredness, the tiredness of a man who had already spent his whole life carrying a duty he had never once been permitted to set down. *Our name was erased once for a claim we did not press. It will not be erased a second time for a duty we failed to finish. Protect what must be protected, Viyamban, whatever the cost of the protecting. I do not ask you to enjoy the asking. I only ask you to be the one who answers it, when your own hour comes.*

His own hour had come. He had believed, three nights past, that he had answered it.

He understood now, walking alone through a garden gone cold and quiet around him, that the answering was not yet finished. Whatever had reached into that room and pulled her back from what should have been certain, it had not simply saved a princess. It had bought her family's old, patient danger a little more time to grow into whatever it was fated to become.

He would need to be more careful. He would need to understand what had intervened, before he risked reaching for her a second time. And he would need, he thought, walking on into the dark with the particular grim patience that had carried his family's duty across three erased generations, to remember that patience, however sorely it was tested, remained the only inheritance he had left that had never once betrayed him.

*Forgive me,* he thought again, to no one, to the cold garden, to the memory of a father long dead who had handed him a duty with both hands and neither comfort nor a strategy besides. *I am not finished yet.*
