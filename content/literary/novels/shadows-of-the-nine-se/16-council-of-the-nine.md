## I

The lantern stopped a short way below the fire. A voice called up, thin and breathless.

"Highness? Highness, please do not let him hit me."

Lohitan lowered his staff by about a finger's breadth.

"I know that voice," said Vaanathi. She stood up from behind the rock. "Ratnan?"

The under-steward came the last few steps on his hands and knees. His face was grey. His waist cloth was torn at the hem, and one sandal was gone. He carried a leather case slung across his chest. He sat down on the red stone and put his head between his knees and breathed like a bellows.

"I took... a fishing boat," he said, between gasps. "From the north landing. The man said... he would only go to the first bend. I walked the rest. I have been walking since the afternoon." He looked up. "The lady Nila said I would find you here."

"Nila sent you?"

"She gave me the boat fare. And a message." He fumbled at the case. "She said to say it exactly. *Tell her the moon is young, and the roads are long, and she should come home before the guest arrives.*" He pulled out a folded leaf. "And this is from His Grace. He sealed it himself."

Vaanathi broke the wax. Her father's hand was square and plain.

*The Samudrapuram embassy has been seen off the coast. Two ships. The ambassador will land within five days. Charudatta has announced it at court as a gift of heaven. I have said nothing. Come back at once. I do not like this. A.*

She read it twice and passed it to Sancharan. He read it and gave it to Lohitan, who held it upside down for some time before handing it back.

"I cannot read," said the red man cheerfully. "What does it say?"

"That my suitor is coming," said Vaanathi, "and that no one in the palace has the least idea why."

"Is he handsome?"

"I have not the slightest idea."

"Find out." Lohitan grunted. "It affects how hard I hit him."

Ratnan had turned a pale green. "I have more," he said weakly. "The man you asked me to meet, Sundar. He did not come. I waited at the colonnade two nights. He has not been seen since the supper. His room is empty. His bed was not slept in."

"Did anyone ask about him?"

"The minister's household said he had gone home to a sick mother." Ratnan's mouth twisted. "I knew his mother is dead. I went to her burning, three years back, with a gift of rice."

"I see."

"I did as you said," said Ratnan. "Before he vanished. I told him you were grieving in your rooms and bored of the scholar. He listened to all of it and he smiled. And he said, *the minister will be pleased.*"

"Good."

"Was it?" Ratnan looked at her with wide, miserable eyes. "I have been wondering. If I am a good liar, does it make me a better man or a worse one?"

"It makes you a useful one tonight." Vaanathi crouched in front of him. "Drink some water. Lohitan will not hit you. Will you?"

"I make no promises," said the red man.

"He is joking," said Sancharan.

"He is not," said Lohitan, "but I will try."

## II

"We cannot go back tonight," said Sancharan. "Not by the river. And there is something that cannot wait."

"The Council," said Lohitan.

"Yes."

"They have to be told."

"They have to be told, and asked, and persuaded." He rubbed his forehead. "I had hoped to bring them one thing. A name. A proof. I have none. I have a stone and a knife and a dead man who called me *Judge.*"

"That will do." The red man turned to the stone. "I will call them."

He laid his hand flat against the carved face, over the wheel. The gap at the bottom began to glow. It was a dull red at first, then orange. Fine cracks spread outward from it, tracing the spokes. A deep humming rose in the air. It was so low that Vaanathi felt it in her ribs.

"Stand clear," said Lohitan.

The air above the stone opened.

It did not tear, as cloth tears. It parted, like a curtain pulled aside, and behind it lay the violet sky. A floor of pale glowing stone began a pace from the stone, where a rocky hilltop should have been.

"This is how we come and go," said Sancharan. He had taken her elbow without seeming to notice. "Each of the nine places has a door. The one in the stepwell is only the one you found."

"I thought it was the only one."

"It was the only one that opened for you."

Ratnan had backed away with both hands up. "I will wait here," he said. "I will wait very well. I am excellent at it."

"Stay with the crow," said Sancharan.

Iruli gave a hoarse remark and refused to move.

"She will come," said Sancharan. "Please keep your head down."

They stepped through.

## III

The great hall had no roof. It had never needed one. Overhead the violet sky stood like a bowl, and the fixed stars turned on it very slowly, each one in its place. The wheel was laid in dark stone across the floor. Round its rim, nine thrones sat in a ring.

Vaanathi had seen it once, in haste, in fear. She had not seen it filled.

Three were taken already. The first was a man in a saffron-gold cloth. He sat on the gold seat with one leg crossed over the other, and he did not look at any of them. His skin was warm gold-brown. His dark hair fell past his shoulders. A faint light seemed to rest on him, a soft warm glow, like the edge of a lamp. He was tapping one finger on the arm of his throne.

The second was young and slight, in a pale tan wrap. He sat in the green seat with his feet drawn under him. His hands were moving in the air as he talked to nobody, shaping something quick and complicated. His eyes were as sharp as a bird's.

The third was an older man, broad and calm, in a deep yellow cloth the colour of turmeric. His hair was loose and streaked with grey. His beard was short and almost white. He sat with his hands on his knees. He looked at Vaanathi when she came in, and his look was mild, and went straight through her.

"Late," said the man in gold.

"I called as soon as I could," said Lohitan.

"I did not mean you. I meant my son."

Vaanathi's breath stopped. She turned her head. Sancharan had not moved. His face was perfectly still.

"Father," he said.

It was the first time she had heard him say it. It came out flat. There was something under it like a long scar.

"You bring a mortal into the great court." The gold man did not rise. "You, who have been sitting out the pace of the world for two hundred years. You say you will leave it to the keepers. And now you stroll in with a girl at your side and a crow on your shoulder, as though it were a tavern."

"It is not a tavern."

"It is not. Neither is it a public road. Sit down."

Sancharan did not sit. He crossed to the grey-blue throne with its cracked arms and stood in front of it. Then, slowly, as though stepping into cold water, he lowered himself into the seat.

The other two seats were filled by the time he had done so. She had not seen them come. A woman in a white wrap sat on the ivory throne, dark-haired and quiet, with a faint warm light round her shoulders. A tall figure in dull smoke-grey stood motionless behind the two seats at the back, which no one had filled. It might have been a man or a woman. It did not move. It did not speak.

"The Shadow Pair do not answer," said the young man in tan, quickly, seeing her look. "They have not for a thousand years. I am Budha. I talk a great deal. You are Vaanathi, of the house of Anantha. We have been looking forward to meeting you with various degrees of dread."

"That is Surya," said Lohitan, jerking his chin at the man in gold. "Do not stare at him. He hates it."

"I do not hate it." Surya's voice was crisp. "I find it tiresome."

"And this is Guru," said Budha.

The grey-bearded man bowed his head slightly. "We are glad you are well," he said. He had a soft, slow voice. "We were afraid that you might not be."

"And this is Shukra," said Budha, "who will be kind to you whatever the rest of us do."

The woman in white said, "Sit, child. You have walked a very long way."

A low stool had appeared beside her. It was plain and round. Vaanathi sat. She felt ashamed of how glad she was.

One throne remained, pale as a pearl. It stood across from Sancharan. It was empty. The dust on its arms lay thick.

"Now," said Surya. "Report."

## IV

Lohitan went first. He did it standing, with his staff in both hands, and he did not spare himself. Four hundred years on his hill. Every dark moon, the same. A shape in the dark. The ground going soft. He told it in a voice like a cart on a stone road, and when he had finished, his red face was dark with shame.

"I could not stop them," he said. "I would like that remembered. I do not say it to be forgiven."

"Noted," said Surya.

Sancharan went second. He spoke slowly, evenly, in the manner of a man laying out coins on a table. The flood. The keeper's death. The stepwell, the ward, the door. The bench. The three sentinels. The knife, the ring of ash, the word *Judge.*

Budha could not keep still. He leaned forward at the word *bench*. He sat up at the word *debt*. By the end he was on his feet, hands in the air, walking in a small tight circle.

"The blood wakes the shard," he said. "That is established. The shard drinks the blood and begins to count. Count what? Time. A great deal of it. How fast?"

"I do not know," said Sancharan.

"We can measure. Surely we can measure. I would need one night in her presence with an instrument, a water-clock, two measuring bowls of different make." He turned to Vaanathi with a look of fierce delight. "How long were you in the fortress the first time?"

"About an hour."

"And how long outside?"

"I do not know. When we came out, it was almost dawn. We went in a little past midnight."

"An hour became five." Budha spun to face the throne. "It is not even close to the worst it could be. It is a *grace period.*"

"You find that amusing?" said Guru.

"I find it fascinating. It is not the same."

"Sit down, child," said Surya. "You are making the mortal dizzy."

Shukra spoke for the first time. "She is exhausted," she said. "She has not slept in three nights. I would like it noted, before we go on, that the girl is not a specimen."

"No one said she was."

"Budha did, in every way but the word."

"I am sorry!" said Budha. "I am sorry. I will sit."

Surya had been listening with his head tilted back and his eyes half shut. Now he opened them. They were very bright, and not unkind. They were the eyes of a man who had been tired for a very long time and was too proud to show it.

"The line of Anantha has failed," he said. "It has been three hundred years. The keeping was a burden we gave them. We said, *keep faith.* They have kept it as one keeps an old coat, out of habit and without remembering why. Now a keeper is murdered. A stone is scraped. An outsider walks into the great court without a summons." His voice was level. "I propose that the line be set aside."

"Set aside?" said Vaanathi.

"Put to sleep. Gently. Until the danger has passed."

She stood up. The stool went over with a soft clatter.

"You mean me."

"I mean the whole house. You most of all."

"You would kill me?"

"I would not use that word." Surya looked at her steadily. "I would place you where the blood could not be used. It is not a punishment. It is a precaution. The shard has begun to count. Every day you are free, it counts faster."

"No," said Sancharan.

He had not raised his voice. But he had stood, and the air in the great court seemed to change. A coldness came into it, a slowness. Vaanathi felt it in her teeth.

"She is under my watch," he said.

"You do not decide that."

"I weigh. It is my office. I have weighed this. She is not the danger. She is the one thing in this whole affair that has been entirely honest." He turned his head. "You would put her away because it is tidy."

"I would put her away because it is wise."

"It is the same mistake you made with the Tower family."

The silence was awful.

"We do not speak of that," said Surya.

"We do not speak of it. That is how it was allowed to happen." Sancharan took a step towards the gold throne. "I stood in the rain and said nothing. You said nothing. All nine of us said nothing. And three hundred years later, the child of that family is killing the child of ours, and we are still not speaking."

"You do not know that."

"I know he is the only one who has had the means."

Surya had risen. For a moment, the two of them faced one another, the warm bright figure and the grave dark one. She saw how alike they were. She saw the shape of the jaw, the set of the shoulder. She saw that neither would yield.

## V

"May I speak?"

It was her own voice. She had not planned it. It came out thin and clear in the great stillness, and every head turned.

"This is not your place, child," said Surya.

"I am the heir of the Accord. You said so yourself." She had to hold on to the back of the stool. "I am not asking you to listen. I am asking whether I may ask a question. It is a very short one."

Budha clapped his hands together once, softly, and covered his mouth.

"Let her," said Guru.

"I do not see..."

"Let her speak, Surya." The grey-bearded god did not raise his voice. "It will take less time than refusing."

Surya sat down.

Vaanathi looked round the ring of seats. She looked at the gold face, the green, the ivory, the red. She looked at the pale empty throne and the two shadowy figures at the back. Last of all she looked at the man in the grey-blue chair, who sat with his hands on the cracked arms and did not meet her eyes.

"Our plate says that every world ends," she said. "It says that something waits at the end of each one. It has no hatred. It is only the going-out of things."

Nobody spoke.

"It says that ours has been kept from it by the nine."

"Yes," said Guru gently.

"What about the others?"

"The others?"

"The other worlds. The ones that have gone." She swallowed. "Were they not also alive?"

Silence.

"I am not clever," said Vaanathi. "I do not know the sky. I only know that I have been told all my life that a thing should be paid for what it has done. A deed has a weight. That is what the Judge says. That is what *he is.* And I would like to ask him." She turned. "You weigh everything. You weigh a king's mercy and a ferryman's death and a bowl of rice. Who weighs the Accord, Judge?"

The great court was quiet as a held breath.

"If nine of you stand between one world and the dark," said Vaanathi, "and every other world is left to burn, is that fair? Is that balanced? Is that what you are for?"

Nobody answered. Budha's hands had come down. Lohitan stared at his feet. Shukra put one hand to her mouth. Even Surya had gone absolutely still.

It was Guru who spoke at last. He spoke slowly, with great care. "That is a very old question," he said. "It has been in this room for a very long time."

"Has anyone answered it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because we were afraid of the answer."

She turned back to Sancharan. He had not moved. But his face had lost every trace of colour. His eyes, at last, had come up and met hers, and what was in them she could not have named. It was not anger. It was nearer to recognition, the look of a man who has been carrying a closed box for a very long time and has just heard someone knock on the lid.

"It is the question I have not asked," he said. His voice was barely audible. "For three hundred years."

"Will you?"

"Not tonight." He closed his eyes. "But you have set it down in front of me. I cannot say I did not hear it."

## VI

The council was adjourned in uneasy peace. Surya withdrew the proposal, not gracefully. He ordered that no one of the Anantha line should enter the great court again without the summons of the nine. He gave Sancharan until the dark of the moon to find those who had scraped the stones. He said it in a tone like a closing door.

Then he looked at his son, for a moment, in a way that none of the others could see, and said very quietly, "You look tired, boy."

"I am."

"Rest."

"I will."

"You will not." The gold god turned away. "But I have said it."

They went out the way they had come. The air above the red stone closed behind them, and the cold night met them like a hand. Ratnan was waiting by the embers, asleep upright against a boulder, with Iruli standing on his head.

The crow looked at Vaanathi. Then, with great slowness, she lowered one foot and put it, very gently, on the top of his hair.

"Highness?" Ratnan woke with a start. "Is it done? Did I miss it?"

"It is done."

"What time is it?"

"I do not know," said Vaanathi. "I went in a little before midnight."

She looked up. The sky above Sivandha Kundru was not black. It was pale. The east was streaked with rose. A light wind came off the plain, smelling of dew and thorn, and a rooster somewhere far below was crowing the morning.

"We were gone an hour," she said.

"It is nearly dawn," said Ratnan, in a faint voice.

Behind her, Sancharan had stopped. His face in the first grey light looked older than she had ever seen it.

"An hour became six," he said, very quietly. "It is not a grace period any longer."

"What does that mean?"

"It means that the shard has begun to count in earnest." He did not look away from the east. "And that every step you take in that place costs more than the last."
