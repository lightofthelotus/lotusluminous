## I

The blade was a finger's width from her chest, and it was not moving.

Vaanathi stood and looked at it. She did not breathe. She could not have said whether she was able to. The point was a small bright wedge of iron, hanging in the air, with a thread of lamplight caught along its edge. Behind it the hooded man hung leaning forward, one foot lifted, his sleeve frozen in a curl of dark cloth. She could see the weave of the cloth. She could see a single grain of something grey caught in a fold.

Nothing else moved.

She tried to lift her hand and found that she could, but slowly, as if the air had turned to warm honey. Her sleeve dragged against her wrist. Her eyelids came down and went up again with a long soft weight. Whatever held the corridor had its fingers in her too, a little. It did not hurt. It was only very still.

Across the corridor, a servant carrying a lamp had stopped mid-step. The flame stood straight up, like a yellow leaf. Above her head, Iruli hung in the air with her wings half open and her beak parted in a scream that made no sound. Dust glittered in the lamplight. Each speck sat where it was, quite still.

She heard a footstep beside her. She did not turn at once.

"Do not be frightened," said Sancharan.

"I am not." She was surprised to hear her own voice. It sounded thin and slow, as if it came through water. "I am only waiting to be."

"That is a reasonable order of events."

He stepped between her and the blade. He was moving at his ordinary pace. That was the strangest thing. In this stopped world, he alone was as he had always been. He crossed the space as casually as a man walking through his own house, put out two fingers, and took hold of the hooded man's wrist.

The wrist did not give. It was like holding a branch. He turned it, gently, and the knife slid out of the stiff fingers. Sancharan took it by the blade, glanced at it, and set it down on the stone floor.

"Poorly made," he said. "A butcher's knife. He did not even clean it."

"You have stopped time."

"I have." He pushed back the man's hood with the back of one hand. "In this corridor. For a short while. It is not something I can do for long, and I do not like to."

"Why not?"

"Because it is never free." His voice was flat. "Everything I stop, I owe. I will pay it later, somewhere else. It is not a matter of taking breath. It is a matter of ledgers."

She looked at the man's face.

It was a stranger's. He was in his thirties, thin and sallow, with a patchy beard and a long nose. His eyes were wide open and fixed. In the corner of his mouth, a thread of spittle had caught the light. He wore a dark wrap and rope sandals, the dress of a labourer or a pedlar. There was nothing about him that she would have remembered.

Except for the grey grain at his wrist, and a small smudge on the palm of the hand that had held the knife.

She bent. It was a ring, a circle drawn in dull ash, as neat as a seal. Inside it was nothing at all.

"What is that?"

"A mark." Sancharan did not look at it. "I have not decided whose. I will."

"You know."

"I know the ash." He knelt, and with one finger lifted the grey grain from the sleeve. It lay in the crease of his fingertip. "It is the same as the pinch you took from the stone."

"I have it." She put a hand to her sash. "It is here."

"Keep it." He let the grain fall. "Now stand up, Vaanathi. There is something I promised."

## II

They sat on the floor of the corridor, facing each other, with the frozen man standing over them like a statue in a courtyard. Neither of them found this odd. It was very quiet. She could hear her own pulse and nothing else.

"I said I would tell you my work," said Sancharan.

"You did."

"I will say it plainly. I am not good at saying it any other way." He had folded his hands in his lap, one over the other, and was looking at them. "I am the one the old tongues call Shani. I am the slow one. I keep what is owed."

She waited. Nothing came after.

"That is all?"

"It is a great deal." A small dry smile came and went. "I am a god of time, Vaanathi. Of consequence. Of what follows from what you did. Every deed you do has a weight, and I keep the scale. I do not punish. I do not reward. I only see that what is due arrives."

"A god."

"It is a word. It is a poor one. I use it because you will understand it."

"You are *the* Shani."

"There is only one."

She said nothing. She sat with her hands in her lap and tried to find her own thoughts. They seemed to be scattered across the corridor like dropped beads.

"I am supposed to kneel," she said at last.

"Please do not."

"I am supposed to be afraid."

"You may be, if it is useful. It generally is not."

"I thought you would be taller."

"I am quite tall."

She gave a short, cracked laugh. It surprised her. She put her hand to her mouth, and he looked up at last. His eyes were dark and level and a little anxious.

"I have been trying," he said, "to find a way to say this for four days. I did not find one. I am sorry for that. I rehearsed it on the road, and again on the stair, and each time it sounded like a lie."

"You told me you were a scholar."

"I told you I was called Sancharan, and that is true. It is a name I gave your first king, long ago, on a hill where this palace now stands. I told him to use it if his house were ever in danger. Someone has used it. That is why I am here."

"My father."

"I think so. I am not sure. The seal was broken in a room I could not see." He lifted his shoulders. "I came on foot, because I do not like to be noticed. I stayed in a guest room, because it was convenient. I wore a scholar's cloth. I did none of it to deceive you. I did it because I have found that people are more honest with a man who does not look like a storm."

"And Iruli?"

"Is Iruli."

"Is she a god too?"

"She would hate that." For the first time he smiled properly. "She is a crow. She has been with me for a very long time. She keeps my appointments and insults my enemies."

"That is all?"

"She is also the only creature who has ever told me I was wrong and been right."

Vaanathi looked up at the bird, frozen in the air with her beak open. Her yellow eye gleamed. In that moment she looked like exactly what he had said. She looked outraged, and loyal, and very small.

"So the bench," said Vaanathi slowly. "In that place. When my blood fell."

"Yes."

"It aged."

"It aged a thousand years in a dozen breaths. Time is mine, and in that place it is held very carefully, and your house's blood has a taste that it does not forget." He paused. "I have been wondering how to say this kindly. I will not manage it. When the blood of Anantha enters that fortress, something wakes that was put to sleep. It is a debt. It runs forward from the moment of waking. I do not yet know how large it is."

"What is the debt?"

"Time. A great deal of it. In the end, I think, more than anyone can afford."

"The carving," she said. "The wheel. The nine spokes. The nine seats in that court."

"Yes."

"You are one of the nine."

"I am one of them." He did not seem surprised that she had worked it out. "There are nine of us who keep this world. We each hold a part of the weight. Your earth is balanced between us, the way a child's top is balanced on its point. If one of us were to fail, it would wobble. If two, it would fall."

"Fall where?"

"Out of the pattern." He opened his hands. "That is as much as I can tell you now. It is not that I wish to hide it. It is that I am not sure which parts of it your father knows, and I would rather he told you than I."

"He knows something."

"Your house made a promise. Your father keeps it. He keeps it badly, because he was never told all of it." He said this gently. "I think he has been very lonely."

She thought of the closed face on the throne. She thought of the broad hand on the top of her head.

"Yes," she said. "I think so too."

She sat with this for a moment. Then she said, in a very small, very level voice:

"Well. That does explain the bench."

There was a pause.

Sancharan made a sound. It began somewhere in his chest, and she watched it fight its way up and out of him against every instinct he had. It was a laugh. It was a real one, short and astonished, and it seemed to take him entirely by surprise.

"I beg your pardon," he said.

"I am sorry. I did not mean..."

"No. Do not apologise. I have not laughed in..." He stopped. He pressed the back of his hand against his mouth. His shoulders were shaking. "Two hundred and fourteen years. I counted. I do not know why."

"Is it so bad?"

"It is very bad. It is very good. I have forgotten what it does to a man."

She was laughing too. She could not stop. It rose in her like water and came out in gasps, cracked and hiccuping, and tears ran down her face. They sat on the stone floor of a stopped corridor with an assassin standing over them, laughing like children, and neither of them could say why.

## III

When it was over, they were both quiet.

"I do not understand why you cannot touch me," she said. "If you are a god. If you can hold the whole of time in a corridor."

"Because of what I am." He had gone still again. "When I take hold of a living thing, I know its time. All of it. The beginning and the end lie side by side in my hand, and the thing feels it. A leaf curls and goes brown. A flame bends and dies. A man grows old on the spot. It is not malice. It is only that I cannot hold a thing without its ending coming near."

"But you held my hand last night."

"I held it for the length of three turns of a cloth." His voice was low. "I was as quick as I knew how. I was very careful, and still I felt it. Your hand got older in mine. A very little. A day, perhaps."

"I did not feel it."

"No." He looked at her. It was an odd look, puzzled and troubled and strangely naked. "That is the thing I wished to tell you. I have not said it to anyone."

"Tell me."

"When I look at any living creature, I see the end of it. I cannot help it. I looked at the minister the first time I saw him and I knew within a few years how his story closes. I looked at your father. I look at the crow. I see it as you see the edge of a table." He took a breath. "When I look at you, I see nothing."

"Nothing?"

"The edges are blurred. I cannot find the end. I do not know what it means." His hands tightened. "It has never happened in all my days. I find it very disturbing."

"Does it mean I will not die?"

"I do not know. I do not think so. It may mean that something is hiding it. Or that something has not yet been decided." He shook his head. "I only know that when I look at you, I cannot see where the road stops. And I find that I do not want to stop looking."

Her heart was beating too hard. She had wanted, all her life, for someone to tell her a truth without folding it first. Now someone had, and it was far too large to hold. She stood in the middle of it, and did not know what to do with her hands.

She did not move. The corridor was perfectly still. Dust hung in the air between them, gold and silent, like stars in a well.

"That is not a thing you should say," she said.

"I know."

"Why did you?"

"Because I have broken a ward and stopped the world and laughed, and I am clearly not myself." The faintest trace of humour touched his voice. "Because you are going to be angry in a moment, and I would prefer that you were angry at the truth."

"Angry about what?"

"That it is impossible."

She flinched. He saw it. He did not look away.

"What I am cannot be with what you are. A mortal who lives beside me ages and wastes. I have seen it happen to those who loved me before. It is not cruelty, and it is not cause for sorrow. It is only the weight of the thing." His voice was very gentle. "I will not do that to you. I cannot. Do you understand?"

"I understand that you have decided."

"It is not a decision. It is a fact."

"A fact you were quite sure of until you could not see the end of my road."

He opened his mouth. He closed it again.

"That," said Sancharan, after a long moment, "is not a fair thing to say."

"I know." She got to her feet. "It is very likely true."

## IV

He rose too, slowly, and picked up the knife. He weighed it in his hand, then slid it into the folds of his waist cloth.

"It is time," he said. "I cannot hold this much longer. When I let go, the man will finish his stroke. I will stop it. I will not be gentle about it. You will want to look away."

"I will not."

"As you like."

He stood in front of her. His back was straight and still. She saw his hand lift at his side, the fingers spread, like a man about to close a door against a wind.

The air in the corridor shifted.

It was the faintest thing. A breath of cold. The lamp flame trembled. The dust began to fall.

The hooded man's foot came down. The knife was not in his hand. He stumbled. His empty fingers closed on air, and for a moment his face was pure astonishment. Sancharan took him by the throat and the wrist and lifted him off the floor.

The man did not scream. He did not struggle. He hung from the god's hand like a cloth on a line, his rope sandals scraping the stone. His eyes, wide and pale, went from Sancharan's face to Vaanathi's and back.

Then a strange thing happened. The fear went out of them.

"Judge," said the man.

His voice was soft, almost fond. A thin trickle of dark blood ran from the corner of his mouth.

"I know you," said Sancharan.

"We know you. We have always known you." The man smiled. His teeth were stained. "The dark of the moon is coming, Judge. You cannot weigh us. You are not clean."

"What are you?"

"A servant." The smile widened. "Of what comes after all of you."

His whole body convulsed. Sancharan let him go. He fell, folded, and lay on his side with his hands curled to his chest. A faint bitter smell rose from his lips. In a few breaths the light left his eyes.

Iruli landed on the dead man's shoulder. She looked at him a moment and turned her head away.

"Quickly," said Sancharan, low and fast. "Listen. When they come, say that a man attacked you and I stopped him. That is all. Say nothing of the ring, or the grain, or what he called me. I will tell your father what he needs to hear, in my own way, and in my own time."

"You are asking me to lie to the guards."

"I am asking you to be brief. It is not at all the same thing."

"It is a little the same."

"Then it is a very small lie, and I will carry it." His eyes met hers. "I have carried larger."

Footsteps came pounding down the corridor, the shouts of guards, the clank of iron. Torchlight spilled across the stone. Vaanathi stood with her bandaged hand pressed to her mouth and did not speak. Sancharan stood beside her, very straight, with the dead man at his feet.

"He knew who you were," she whispered.

"Yes."

"They all know."

"Not all." His eyes were on the ring of ash, fading on the dead man's open palm. "Only the ones who have been waiting."

She turned to look at him. In the torchlight his face was hard and tired and very old, and she saw for the first time exactly what it was that he carried.

"Waiting for what?" she asked.

"For me to arrive," said Shani.
