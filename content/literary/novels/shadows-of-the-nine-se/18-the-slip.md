## I

At noon the sky-tower threw almost no shadow.

Vaanathi stood at the foot of it with her hand on the dark door and looked up. The creeper hung straight down the stone, glossy in the white light. There was no wind. A single hawk turned high over the east wall, and its cry was thin and far.

"He will know we are coming," she said.

"He has known since last night."

"Then why does he not run?"

"Because he thinks he has not finished." Sancharan stood a little behind her, with Iruli on his shoulder. He had not slept. She could see it in the set of his mouth. "A man like that does not leave a task half done. He has the patience of the very devout."

"Is that what he is?"

"It is what I would call it."

She pushed the door. It swung inward without a sound, as before. The hinges had been freshly oiled. The same dry smell met her, old paper and cold ash, and for a moment she hesitated on the threshold. She had the cloth folded small in her sash. It lay against her skin like a coin of ice.

"I can go first," said Sancharan.

"No." She put her foot on the first stair. "He asked me to come. I would like to be the one who does."

They climbed. The spiral wound up and up. She did not count the steps. She had counted them once, with fear and a small warm trust, and she would not do it again. At the sixtieth step she stopped, because she had stopped there before, and she heard Sancharan stop behind her.

She did not turn.

"I am all right," she said.

"I know."

She went on.

The trapdoor stood open. Pale light fell through it from above, and with it came a sound she had not expected. It was a thin, dry, rhythmic scrape, steady as breathing. Someone was sweeping.

She climbed through into the round chamber.

Viyamban stood in the middle of the floor with a short broom of palm ribs. He was sweeping the stones. He did it slowly, in long even strokes, with his back to her. He was stooped. His grey hair hung down. He had on the weathered brown wrap and no sandals. At the sound of her step he stopped.

"Ah," he said. "Child."

He turned. His face, in the white daylight from the arched window, was just as she had first seen it. It was lined and mild and tired. His large eyes were wet. One hand shook a little on the broom.

"I am so glad you came back," said Viyamban. "I have been so afraid you would not."

## II

She did not answer. She crossed the floor and stopped in front of the stone sighting-table. She took the cloth from her sash. She unfolded it slowly and laid it on the scored surface, smoothing it flat with both palms, so that every line showed.

*My dear girl. My dear girl. My dear girl.*

He did not move. He stood with the broom in his hand and looked down at the cloth. The light from the window lay on it like a thin gold sheet.

"I found it last night," she said. "In the garden. Under the jasmine."

"Did you?"

"You said, *sleep well, little princess.* You said it very kindly."

"I did."

"You watched us."

"I could see the Judge's shoulders." His voice was gentle. "One cannot hide those."

She waited. She had thought a great deal, on the way, about what she would say. She had planned to be hard. She had planned to be exact. She had expected to find the words standing ready, like soldiers.

They were not there.

"Why did you write *forgive me?*" she said instead.

"Ah."

He set the broom against the wall. It was the smallest movement. He did it as an old man does, with a hand on the stone for balance. And then, quite slowly, something happened to his back.

It straightened.

It was not a sudden thing. It was like watching a long-bent branch come free of a weight. The shoulders rolled and settled. The head came up. The shaking hand grew steady and was still. When he turned to face her, he was a hand taller than she had thought, and his eyes were no longer mild. They were as clear and hard as polished flint.

"Because it was true," said Viyamban. "I am sorry. I am entirely sorry. I would do it again."

The room went very quiet.

"You killed him."

"I did."

"You killed Smaran."

"Yes." He did not blink. "In a cup of honey water, with bitter root and a sleeping herb. I sat with him while it worked. I held his hand. He did not suffer. His nails were pink at the end, I think. I looked."

"You looked."

"I wished to be sure." A small muscle moved in his jaw. "I have loved him since I was seven years old."

She had no breath. She could not find it. She heard, very far away, a faint rustle of wings as Iruli shifted on the beam.

"He came to me with a leaf in his hand, and he was so pleased," said Viyamban, quietly. "He had worked it out, you see. Not all. Enough. He knew that I kept something. He knew the mark. He said, *I have found a thing, old friend, and I think we are meant to look at it together.* He had tears in his eyes. And I knew that if I told him, he would tell you. And if he told you, you would do the thing that the sky says you will do."

"What thing?"

"I will not say it."

"I want to know."

"Ask your Judge." His eyes flicked past her. "Ask him what he fears from your blood. He has not told you. He has been very careful not to."

She turned her head. Sancharan was standing at the head of the stair with his hands at his sides. His face was perfectly closed.

"Is that true?"

"I do not know what he means," said Sancharan.

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only one I have." His voice did not shake. "He is trying to make you doubt me. It is what I would do in his place."

"He is right," said Viyamban softly. "That is what I would do. But I would not lie to do it."

She stood between them. The scored stone was cold under her hand. The cloth lay there, white, with its tidy rows of stolen love. She thought she might be sick.

"The leaf from the genealogy," she said. "The first leaf. Did you take it?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because it names my father." For the first time, his voice cracked. It was the thinnest of cracks, quickly smoothed. "It names the House of the Tower. It names those your founder drove out. It was the only page in that great book where my family's name was written, and it was written in shame. I wanted it for myself. I wished to be the last one who read it."

"You could have asked."

"Asked whom? Your father? A man who has paid my stipend for twenty-six years and never once climbed my stair?" He laughed, a short dry sound with nothing in it. "I do not blame him. He did not know. That is the horror of it. They none of them knew. They sat on their gold seat and ate their rice, and my family sat in the cold and scraped a stone that holds up a god. For three hundred years. Alone."

"I did not know."

"No." He looked at her. For a moment something close to pity came into his face, and it was worse than anger. "You are not to blame. You are only the last in a line. It is a pity. It is truly a pity. You have your grandmother's eyes."

## III

"Viyamban," said Sancharan.

It was a quiet word. It was said without heat. But the old man stiffened as if he had been touched on the neck.

"Judge."

"You know what I am."

"I have known since you stood in this room and looked at the wall." He met the god's eyes. "I have read the old writing. I have seen the name *Sancharan* in the margins of my father's books. I knew you the moment you spoke it."

"Then you know that I will take you before the nine."

"I know that you will try."

"You are not in danger from me." Sancharan came forward, slowly, one step at a time. "I do not punish. I weigh. And I wish to hear all that you can say before I do. You have killed a man. You have tried to kill a woman. You have done it for reasons that you believe. I would like to know them."

"It would not help you."

"It might."

"I have made my reasons known. Not to you." The old man had backed a step, and then another. He had come to the far wall, where the lattice of vine hung over the carved wheel. He stopped with his shoulders against the green. "It is not enough that I tell you. It must be believed. And you are the one being that cannot be told a thing, Judge. You must see it for yourself."

"Then show me."

"I intend to."

His hand moved. It went to the lattice behind him and closed on a cord that hung among the leaves. She saw it, a thin brown rope, nearly lost in the creeper. He did not pull it.

"Do not," said Sancharan.

"Why? What will it do?"

"I do not know. That is why I say it."

"You are a very cautious man." The old man smiled. It was a weary, rueful smile, and it was almost the old one. "I have always admired it. It is why I am sorry I cannot give you what you ask. Not yet. I have not finished."

"Viyamban."

"Child." He looked past the god. His eyes held hers. "I would like you to know something. I did not wish to poison you. I tried to make it painless. It was meant to be sweet. If the bird had not come, you would not have felt a thing." He drew a breath. "I am only sorry that you will not believe that."

"I do not."

"No." His hand tightened. "But I am sorry."

He pulled the cord.

Nothing happened.

She had time to think it. It was a clear, quiet thought. *Nothing happened.* The vines hung straight. The light lay on the floor. The broom stood against the wall.

Then the floor moved.

It was the stone sighting-table. It had not been fixed to the floor. It turned, on a pivot she had not seen, with a deep grinding sound, and the whole round slab beneath it swung downward like a lid. A square black mouth opened in the floor. A dry cold draught came up. There was a sound inside it like a long slide, the rush of something polished and steep.

The old man was already at the edge.

"Stop him!"

She did not know which of them she shouted to. Sancharan was already moving. He crossed the chamber in two strides, one hand outstretched, and she saw the air shiver round him, the first cold breath of the thing he did. Time began to thicken.

"Do not," said Vaanathi.

He stopped.

It was only a half-step. His arm was out. His fingers were a hand's breadth from the old man's wrap. The thickening held, and began to thin. She saw his face. It was white, strained, and dreadfully clear.

"If I stop it, I can take him."

"You told me what it costs."

"I can pay."

"Not here. Not with the debt running." She heard her voice crack. "Not for him."

He looked at her. It lasted perhaps the length of a breath.

Then he let it go.

The air in the tower sprang loose like a released spring. Viyamban's wrap, caught in the god's fingers for a bare instant, tore with a dry sound. A long strip of brown cloth came away in Sancharan's hand. The old man staggered, flung out one arm, and caught himself on the very lip of the hole.

He turned his head.

"Thank you," he said. He sounded surprised.

And he stepped backward into the dark.

## IV

The sound of his sliding went on for a long time. It was a smooth, rushing hiss, fading downward, the sound of something that has been used many times and well. Then it stopped. A very faint echo came back, and nothing after.

The stone slab rose. It swung back into its place with a deep, slow grind. The pivot clicked home. The lines of the sighting-table lay as they had lain before.

Vaanathi was across the floor before she could think. She dropped to her knees and put both hands on the cold stone. She pushed. She hammered on it. The slab did not move.

"It is shut," said Sancharan, behind her. His voice was strangely flat. "He will have gone out through the old drains. There are tunnels all under the east wall. I can feel them." He knelt, put a palm on the stone, and withdrew it quickly. "He has done this many times."

"We have to go after him."

"We will."

"He killed Smaran."

"I know."

"You let him go."

"I did not let him." He drew a slow breath. "You asked me not to stop the world. I did as you asked. I am not sure which of us made the mistake."

She sat back on her heels. She was shaking all over. For a time she could do nothing but look at the floor.

"I am sorry," she said.

"Do not."

"I should have let you."

"No." He turned his face aside. He was holding a strip of torn cloth in his two hands, turning it over. "I would have done it. I would have stopped the world, and caught him, and put him before the nine, and paid. I would have been very sure. I was sure when I set the debt on myself in the corridor. I was sure it was a good use." His voice was very low. "I do not like how sure I was."

"I stopped you because I was afraid."

"Of what?"

"That you would not stop at stopping." She lifted her head. "That you would do the rest."

He did not speak. For a long moment the two of them knelt on the bare stone, with the light slanting from the window and the dust hanging gold in the air.

"I would not have killed him," he said at last.

"You do not know that."

"No." Something moved in his face. "I do not."

It was a bleak thing to hear. She did not know whether she was glad of it or sorry. She got to her feet.

On the low wooden table by the inner wall, beside the clay lamp, lay a bundle of palm-leaf strips. She had seen them before, on the first day. She had not looked at them. Now she crossed the room and picked them up.

They were charts. They were drawn in dark ink in a quick fine hand, the nine-spoked wheel again and again, with small marks beside each spoke. She turned the first. The second. Down the margin of each ran a column of dates. They were the same dates as those on Smaran's second leaf, in the same order. Beside each, in the old man's own writing, he had put a single word.

*Stepwell. Hill. Tower.*

She read down the column. The first date was six weeks old. A line was drawn through it. The second was less than two weeks old, and it too was struck out. The third had a circle round it, in red ink, hard and thick.

It was the dark of the moon. It was twenty days away. It was also the day her father had said, in the hush beneath the throne, that a stranger had asked for her to be pledged.

Beside the red circle, in the same small hand, was written a final line.

*The cut. The heir must be there.*

She put the leaf down very carefully.

"Sancharan."

"Yes?"

"The betrothal is at the tower."

"I know."

"On the dark moon."

"Yes."

"It is not a wedding." She heard her own voice, thin and far away, as if it were someone else's. "It is a door. They are going to bring me here, and ask me to speak a vow. And they are going to cut the third stone."

He said nothing.

"That is why he tried to kill me. He knew. He knew before any of us what the prince's letter meant." She stared at the red circle. "He has been trying to stop it. The only way he knew."

"And he may be right."

"No." She turned. "He may be right about what it is. He is wrong about what to do."

For the first time that day, something that was not grief crossed Sancharan's face. He looked at her with a strange, steady attention, the look of someone who has heard a bell he was waiting for.

"Then what would you do?" he said.

"I would not go to the tower alone." She folded the chart and put it in her sash, next to the cloth. "And I would not go without knowing what the cut is."

## V

They came down the stair slowly, one behind the other. Neither of them spoke. At the bottom she stood in the open doorway and let the noon light fall on her face. It felt like a rebuke. The weeds were bright. The hawk had not moved.

Iruli went ahead into the scrub, a small dark flicker, and returned a minute later. She dropped something at Vaanathi's feet. It was a sandal. It was old and plain, of rope and wood, much worn at the heel.

"His," said Vaanathi.

"He will have lost it in the drain." Sancharan crouched and turned it over. There was red clay caked in the sole. "He did not even stop to look for it."

"He was in a hurry."

"He was not." Sancharan stood. "He was in his own place, going where he has gone a hundred times. That is a man who has made all his arrangements."

She looked at the tower. The creeper hung down it in its neat lines. It was very quiet. It looked like any other ruin on any other wall. It looked like a thing that nobody had ever loved.

"I will tell my father," she said. "I will tell him all of it. The cloth, and the chart, and what he said. We will have the drains searched. We will have every gate watched."

"Yes."

"It will not work."

"No." He glanced at her. "But it is what one does."

"What else is there?"

"Tonight," said Sancharan, "I will tell you about the night the wheels broke."

She turned.

He was looking away from her, at the east wall, at the long grey line of the stone. His face was calm. It was the calm of a man who has put his hand on a cold latch, and has decided to lift it.

"You said after the tower."

"I did." He took a breath. "I find I would like to say it before I have time to change my mind."

"Then say it."

"Not here. Not under that." He did not glance at the tower. "When it is dark. When we are in a place with a roof and a lamp. I would like to have something to look at that is not the sky."

"Where?"

"Your room," said Sancharan. "If you will have me. With the door open. And your friend in the next room, so that nobody can say you were alone."

She almost smiled. It surprised her. It was a very small thing, and she held on to it.

"That is the most proper proposal I have ever received," she said.

"I am out of practice."

"Come at dusk."

"I will."

She turned and went down the weedy track towards the palace, and he followed, at a decent distance, with the crow on his shoulder. Behind them the tower stood with its door open and its stair empty and its great carved wheel hidden under green, and did not make a sound.
