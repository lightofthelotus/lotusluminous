## I

The treasury was the oldest part of the palace. It lay below the council hall, behind two iron doors and a corridor so low that Sancharan had to bend his head.

Old Bhargava met them at the second door. He was a stooped man with a thin white fringe of hair and a ring of keys as wide as a cooking pot. He had held the treasury for forty years. As far as Vaanathi knew, nobody had ever seen him smile.

"The bell," he said, when she told him. "Highness, I would rather you did not."

"I only wish to look at it."

"No one looks at it. It is not on the inventory." He pressed the keys to his chest. "My master's master did not look at it. My own master told me to leave it be. He said it was not ours."

"Whose is it?"

"The throne's, he said. But not to use." His voice dropped. "There is a saying, Highness. A grandmothers' tale. They say the bell sounds for liars."

"Does it?"

"Nobody has tried. It has not made a sound since before the founder's grandson was born."

"Then there is nothing to fear," said Sancharan.

Bhargava looked up at him. It seemed to be the first time he had noticed how tall the visitor was. His mouth worked. He looked at the princess, and she looked back at him steadily. At last he gave a small, heavy shrug and turned the key.

The inner vault was a windowless room. Chests stood against the walls. Rolled cloth lay in stacks. A row of gold vessels glowed dully in the lamplight. In the far wall, at the height of a man's shoulder, there was a small niche, and in the niche hung the bell.

It was smaller than she had imagined, no bigger than a coconut. It was made of dark bronze gone green at the edges, and it hung from a bracket by a short chain. It had no clapper that she could see. A band of tiny marks ran around the rim.

She lifted the lamp.

The marks were the wheel. It was cut nine times, all the way round, and each time the wedge was missing at the bottom.

![The Symbol on the Treasury Bell](02_06_TreasuryBell_Symbol.jpg)

"Nine," said Sancharan, quietly.

"Nine of what?"

"I do not know yet."

She took the bell down. It was heavier than it looked. The chain slid through her fingers. She cupped the bowl in one palm and held it to her face.

It was cold. But it was not the cold of metal. It was the cold of a stone at the bottom of a deep well, where nothing has changed for a thousand years.

Bhargava made a small sound.

"Nothing is happening," said Vaanathi.

"No," said Sancharan. "Try speaking to it."

"Speaking?"

"People talk to what they hope will answer. It rarely hurts."

She felt foolish, and more foolish because she did not feel foolish enough. She gripped the bell and said in a clear voice, "My father rules justly."

Nothing.

"The river is rising."

Nothing.

"I am not afraid."

The bell stayed silent in her hand. Its green rim caught the lamp and gave it back.

"Either you are not afraid," said Sancharan, "or the bell does not mind."

"I am afraid," said Vaanathi. She said it before she could stop herself. It was true, and it felt oddly good to put down.

The bell did nothing at all.

She hung it back in its niche. Her hands wanted to stay on it, the way one lingers on a friend's shoulder. She made them let go.

"Smaran wrote *ask the bell*," she said. "He did not write *and it will answer*."

"No."

"Perhaps it is not that kind of asking."

"Perhaps it is only a bell," said Bhargava hopefully. "A very old one. With a very old design."

"Perhaps." Sancharan studied the green rim for a long moment. Then he joined his palms to the old treasurer. "Thank you, sir. You have been of great help."

Bhargava did not know what to say. He locked the vault with unsteady fingers and went off down the corridor without his usual stately pace. Vaanathi watched him go.

"He was afraid of it."

"He was afraid of being asked to remember something," said Sancharan. "It is not always the same thing."

## II

It was Vaanathi who thought of the beams.

It came to her at the third watch of the night, while she lay awake going over the ledger entries one by one. Smaran's note had said *ask the bell*, and the bell had said nothing. But Smaran never left only one road. He had taught her that when she was six. If you wanted to hide a thing from clever people, he said, you hid it in two places and made one of them easy.

She sat up.

When she was small there had been a game. Above the main beam in the scribes' hall there was a ledge so high that nobody ever looked at it. She used to climb up on his shoulders and leave things there: a seed, a cowrie, a twist of tamarind. The next day he would climb up a ladder and leave her a reply. The game had lasted three years. They had never told anyone.

*Above the lamp-beam,* he used to say. *Where nobody looks up.*

She dressed in the dark and went out into the passage. Nila was sitting on the floor against the opposite wall, with her knees drawn up and her chin on her arms.

![Vaanathi Meets Nila](03_02_Vanathi_Meets_Nila.jpg)

"You do that too often," said Vaanathi in a whisper.

"Sit in corridors? It is the only honest place in a palace. Everyone passes through." Nila looked up. A small pale stone on a cord at her throat caught the light from the passage lamp and seemed to hold it a moment too long. "You are going out."

"Only to walk."

"At this hour, in the wrap you wear to court." She did not press. She rose in one smooth movement and smoothed her skirt. "Where is the scholar?"

"Nila."

"I only ask because I would like to know where my friend is going, and with whom. I do not ask because I mean to stop you."

Vaanathi hesitated. Then she said, "He is a difficult man."

"Yes. I thought so." Nila's mouth gave a small crooked curl. "He is also an old sort of difficult. Some people are heavy because they have carried a great deal for a great while. You must be gentle with the heavy ones. They do not know how to put anything down."

"You have spoken to him?"

"No." Nila looked at the wall. "I only know the kind."

It was an odd thing to say. Vaanathi turned it over and found nothing to hold on to. Before she could ask, her friend had squeezed her hand and gone off down the passage, quick and quiet, towards her own room.

She went to find Sancharan.

He was awake. He stood in the corridor outside the guest room they had given him, as if he had been waiting. Iruli sat on the lintel above him, a small round shadow.

"I thought you would come," he said.

"I thought you slept."

"Rarely."

"I know where Smaran left the rest."

He did not ask how. He fell in beside her, and they went through the sleeping palace without a lamp. The corridors were cold. Their feet made no sound on the stone, though she suspected his made less.

The scribes' hall was a long room with a row of tall windows. By day it rang with the scratch of iron styluses on leaf. At night it was a dim cavern that smelled of neem oil and old palm leaf. A huge beam ran across the ceiling, thick as a man's body and black with age. A heavy oil lamp hung from it on a chain.

"Up there," said Vaanathi, pointing. "On top of the beam. There was a ledge."

Sancharan looked at the beam. He looked at her.

"You were small."

"I was six."

"You are not six now."

"I know how to climb a ladder."

There was a ladder, of course. It stood against the wall, where the scribes used it to trim the lamp: a plain thing of bamboo and cord. She dragged it into place. He held the bottom with both hands, and she began to climb.

She was two-thirds of the way up when her foot slipped.

The cord had rotted in the damp and gave under her weight. The rung twisted. She gasped and clutched the rail, and for one dizzy moment she felt the whole ladder start to slide sideways.

Then it stopped.

It did not stop the way a ladder stops when someone catches it. It stopped as if a thought had been dropped into the room. The slide hung in the air. The rung under her foot felt as firm as a stone step. She stood on it, shaking, with her cheek against the bamboo, and heard nothing but her own breath.

"Are you hurt?" said Sancharan from below.

"No."

"Take your time."

She took it. She went up the last few rungs carefully. She did not look down, and she did not mention that the ladder had not moved. At the top she peered over the edge of the beam and found a flat ledge, thick with a hundred years of dust.

In the dust lay a bundle.

It was wrapped in a scrap of red cloth and was about the size of her hand. The cloth was soft with age and stiff with oil.

Iruli landed beside her on the beam with a soft thump and put her head on one side.

"Did you know?" Vaanathi whispered.

The crow said nothing, in the way that crows do.

## III

They read the letters in the scribes' hall by the light of the great lamp. Sancharan lit it with a spark struck from a flint he had apparently been carrying all along. Vaanathi sat on the bottom rung of the ladder with the red cloth open on her knees.

![Smaran's Letters](03_03_SecretRoom.jpg)

There were three leaves.

The first was in Smaran's hand, small and exact, but written in haste. She could see it in the way the lines tilted.

*My dear girl,*

*If you are reading this, I was right to hide it, and I am sorry I was right.*

*Four days ago I found a letter between two border treaties. It carries a mark: a wheel with a wedge gone. I have seen it before, in a ledger I was forbidden to open when I was young, and I have since found it cut round the rim of the old bell in the treasury. I do not know what it means. I have put the letter back where I found it, so that no one sees me carrying it. But I know where it leads. For as long as the palace has stood, the crown has paid a stipend to a House of the Tower. I think it keeps something for us. I think it has kept it all this time.*

*I have told no one but the warden.*

Vaanathi looked up. "The warden."

"Read on," said Sancharan.

*The warden of the sky-tower is called Viyamban. He is the last of an old line of astronomers. He is also my oldest friend. We were boys together, in the school behind the east wall. He is frail now, and shy of people. He will not come down. He knows what the mark means. He told me once, many years ago, that his house keeps the sky for the throne. I did not understand him. I think I was meant not to.*

*He gave me half an answer when I climbed to him. Go and see him. Say that I sent you, and that I still wish to know what the Tower keeps.*

*Do not tell the minister. I cannot say why. Only that when I spoke of the mark in his hearing, he asked me twice to forget it. A man who asks you to forget something has already remembered it very well.*

She stopped. Her mouth had gone dry.

"He suspected Charudatta," she said.

"He suspected someone had a reason to stop him from asking. That is not the same as a name."

The second leaf was shorter. It was a list of dates in a column, with one word beside each. She did not understand it. She saw only that the dates were far apart, and that each line had been cut with a different stylus, some deep, some faint.

The third leaf was the one she read twice.

*If you find this and I am not here to explain it, do not believe it was my heart. I have a good heart. The healers have said so for thirty years. I do not say this out of vanity. I say it because they will tell you otherwise, and I would like someone to have known.*

*I am not afraid. I am only tired, and curious. It is a bad combination in a man of seventy-one.*

*The rest I have not yet written. I am still counting.*

*Be kind to Ratnan. He is a good boy. Trust him with small things only. The big things frighten him, and a frightened man is a poor guardian.*

*I think I shall sleep tonight. I have not slept properly in three days.*

*Your old friend,*  
*Smaran*

She sat very still. The great lamp ticked as it burned. Outside the tall windows the sky was beginning to pale.

"It does not say who," she said.

"No."

"He knew he was being followed."

"He knew he was close to something. I do not think he knew what."

"He says he is still counting." She looked at the second leaf again, at the column of dates. "Counting what?"

"I do not know." Sancharan sat down on the floor, an arm's length from her, with his back against the wall. He looked suddenly, strangely tired. "But I think it is the only thing he did not finish."

She folded the letters back into the red cloth. Her hands were not quite steady. She noticed that he watched them and did not offer to help. It was the most thoughtful thing anyone had done for her in a week.

"Do you ever tire of that?" she asked.

"Of what?"

"Holding yourself back. Watching and not touching. You do it all the time."

He was quiet for a moment. When he answered, his voice was low.

"Every moment," he said. "It is not for you to notice."

"I notice."

"I know." He got to his feet and held out neither hand nor arm. He only stood there. "That is the difficulty."

## IV

She thought of the three leaves. She thought of the ledger, the stipend, the House of the Tower. She thought of a frail old man who would not come down from his stone room, and of a minister who had asked Smaran twice to forget.

They came out into the corridor with the first grey light. The bundle was tucked into her waist cloth. Iruli went ahead on the air, a small black arrow, and vanished round the corner.

Vaanathi stopped.

Someone was waiting at the far end, beneath the last lamp, a green wrap over one shoulder and his hands folded. He had been standing very still.

"Princess," said Minister Charudatta. "You are up early."

"I could say the same of you."

"I never sleep well when the moon is nearly full." He smiled, the thin smile she had come to know. He did not look at Sancharan. "I was told you had been seen near the scribes' hall. I thought I should make sure nothing was amiss."

"Nothing is amiss."

"I am glad to hear it." His eyes dropped to the cloth at her waist, then rose again so smoothly that she wondered if she had imagined it. "There is a matter I wished to raise. Your father tells me you will guide the scholar. It is a generous office. I only wish to advise, as an old friend of the house, that the sky-tower is not a suitable place to bring a guest."

The corridor went quiet.

"I did not say anything about the sky-tower," said Vaanathi.

"No?" Charudatta's smile did not change. "I must have misheard. These old ears. Forgive me. It has been a long week for all of us."

He joined his palms and bent his head, and she watched him walk away down the passage at his unhurried pace. Neither she nor Sancharan moved until he had turned the corner.

"He knew," she said.

"Yes."

"I have spoken of the tower aloud in only two places. In the archive this morning, with Ratnan standing by. And in the hall tonight, where there was no one."

"The hall was empty," said Sancharan. "That does not mean the walls were."

The first bird of the morning began to sing in the garden. It was a clear, ordinary sound, and it did nothing to warm her.

Sancharan looked up at the pale square of the window. "The tower stands on the east wall. We can reach it before the court wakes. I would rather meet the warden before anyone has had time to tell him we are coming."

"Then we go now," she said.

"Before he can make us stop."

"Before he finds a reason."

She put her hand over the bundle at her waist and felt the three leaves crackle against her skin. They were the last words of a man who had spent thirty years setting other people's secrets in order. He had told her where to go. He had told her whom to trust.

She was beginning to think he had been wrong about one of them.
