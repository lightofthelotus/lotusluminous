## I

She slept until the sun was high and woke with a stranger's hand on her wrist.

It was Nila. She sat on the edge of the bed with Vaanathi's left hand turned palm up in her lap. She was examining the grey-blue cloth bound round it.

"This is very fine weaving," she said. "Whose is it?"

"Give it back."

"In a moment. I want to admire the knots." Nila turned the hand to the light. "Three turns and a tuck. Neat. A man did this, and he was frightened while he did it, because the last turn is too tight." She let go. "A scholar's cloth, torn from his shoulder. I have been sitting here for an hour working it out. I did not wake you because you were smiling in your sleep."

"I was not."

"Very slightly. At the corner."

Vaanathi sat up. Her head was heavy and her whole body ached as though she had been carried up a mountain. Sunlight lay in warm bars across the floor. A bowl of water stood on the low table, and beside it a plate of cut mango, and Nila had thought of both without being asked.

She ate and drank. Then, because she could not help it, she told her.

She told her about the west gate, and the stepwell, and the nine landings. She told her about the scratches on the wall and the stone that glowed from inside a missing wedge. She told her about the door in the open air, and the violet sky, and the three tall figures with no faces. She did not tell her what the bench had done. She did not know why. It seemed too strange to say aloud.

Nila listened without a word. She sat with one knee drawn up and her arms round it, and her face gave nothing away. When Vaanathi finished, she was silent for so long that the lattice shadows crept a finger's width across the floor.

"A door," she said at last.

"Yes."

"At the ninth tier."

"Yes."

"And you went through it."

"I know. I know. He told me not to."

"I was not going to scold you." Nila's voice was light, but there was something very careful under it. "I was only thinking that I am glad you came back. Please do not go down there again."

"You sound like him."

"I sound like a friend." She rose and went to the window. She stood looking out over the lower court, where a line of washermen were beating cloth on the stones. "What did he say, when you asked who he was?"

"That he would tell me. Not in that place. When we were out."

"Has he?"

"Not yet."

"He will." Nila turned. "When he has to. Some people cannot stop being what they are until the moment it matters. Then they cannot stop at all."

"You talk as if you know him."

"I know the type."

"You said that before."

"It is a good phrase. I will keep using it." The corner of her mouth lifted, and then settled. "Do you like him?"

"He is impossible."

"That is not what I asked."

Vaanathi looked at her bandaged hand. "He will not touch me. He says that what he touches, ends. Last night he took my hand to bind it, and then he stepped back as if I had scalded him. He says it as though it were a law." She heard her own voice crack and stopped. "I do not even know what I am saying."

"You are saying that you wish he would."

Silence.

"Yes," said Vaanathi, very low.

Nila crossed the room and sat down beside her again. She did not take her hand. She only sat close, shoulder to shoulder, as they had sat as girls on the roof, counting stars.

"Some endings are only a change of place," she said. "The tide takes a thing out, and in a night or a month it gives it back, further up the shore. That is all I am going to say. I have no business saying even that."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean that you should not decide a thing is impossible because a frightened man has told you so." She stood. "Now eat your mango. I will have the washerwomen bring hot water. You smell of a well."

## II

The cloth came at midafternoon.

It was brought by a boy of about eight, one of the kitchen helpers, who stood in the doorway with his thumb in his mouth. He said a woman at the garden gate had given it to him and told him to put it in the princess's hand. She had given him a copper coin. He had not seen her face.

Vaanathi took the bundle. It was a square of plain white cotton, folded small. She waited until the boy had gone and Nila had closed the door. Then she opened it.

The writing was dark ink on cloth. It slanted a little to the right. It was small, exact and neat.

*My dear girl,*

*I write to you from beyond what my body allowed. I am alive. I will not say how, nor why. Believe me, because you must.*

*Do not tell the scholar of this letter. He is not what he seems. I fear he is the thing I was warned of. Come to the sky-tower tomorrow, at the third watch, and come alone. All will be explained.*

*Your old friend,*
*S.*

She read it three times.

"He is dead," Nila said quietly. She was standing at her shoulder. "You saw him burn."

"I know."

"Then this is a cruelty. Or something worse."

"It is a forgery." Vaanathi laid the cloth flat on the bed and smoothed it with both hands. "Look at the hand. It is a good copy. Whoever made it had a sample. But he never wrote on cloth. He said ink sank into it and the letters ran. And he never signed with a single letter in his life. He wrote his full name. Always. Even on grain accounts."

"What else?"

"*My dear girl.* That is how he began the letters in the beam. Whoever wrote this knew it, or guessed it. And..." She frowned. "*I write to you from beyond what my body allowed.* That is not Smaran. He would never put a flourish like that in a letter. He would have said, *I am afraid I am not dead*, and he would have been dry about it."

Nila laughed. It was a short, startled sound. Then she covered her mouth, looking guilty.

"I am sorry. It is only that I can hear him."

"So can I." Vaanathi's throat was tight. "Whoever did this did not know his heart. They only knew his hand."

She took it to Sancharan.

He was in the lower garden, sitting on a stone bench beneath a flowering tree with his hands loose on his knees. Iruli was on the branch above. The grey-blue cloth he had torn his shoulder-strip from hung a little shorter on one side now. She noticed it and looked away.

He read the note without speaking. His face did not change. When he had read it twice, he folded it along its creases and handed it back.

"A good forger," he said. "They know his habits. They know nothing of him."

"That is what I said."

"Then we agree." He stood. "What did you intend to do?"

"Not go."

"Good."

"But whoever sent it expects me to." She turned the cloth over in her fingers. "They wanted me alone at the third watch in the sky-tower, with a warning in my head about you. They wanted me to doubt you."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because if you doubt me, you will not bring me. And you are of much more use to them alone." He met her eyes. "I would also point out that one sentence in that letter is true."

"Which?"

"*He is not what he seems.*"

She said nothing. The tree above them rustled. A petal drifted down and settled on her wrist.

"I am not asking you to trust me blindly," said Sancharan. "I am asking you to wait for tonight."

"Tonight."

"I told you I would answer. I shall."

She looked at him for a long time. Then she tucked the cloth into her sash, next to the ash and the letters, and nodded once.

## III

The emperor's private supper was held on the roof of the east wing, under an awning of striped silk. It was a small affair. It had been so since Vaanathi was a girl. There were four low tables, a ring of woven mats, and a dozen oil lamps in bronze stands. A single steward served, and four servants waited at the stairs.

Her father sat at the head. Her mother sat at his left, in plain blue with her hair in a low knot. Vaanathi sat on his right. Sancharan, as a guest of the throne, had been given the place opposite. Iruli had taken possession of the awning beam above his head.

Charudatta came last. He bowed to the emperor and to the empress and gave Vaanathi a long, grave look of sympathy. He sat beside Sancharan.

"I hope the scholar is finding the palace hospitable," he said.

"Very."

"And the river?"

"Troubled."

"A sad thing, a troubled river. I hope it can be mended." Charudatta smiled a little. "The people say it is the shadow. I tell them it is silt. They do not believe me."

"You may be right," said Sancharan.

"I often am."

"It is a gift," said Sancharan, "and a danger."

The steward set out the rice. It came on broad green leaves, with a ladle of lentil broth and a dish of tamarind greens. A servant went round with the buttermilk, in small clay cups, spiced with crushed pepper and curry leaf. It was cold. The evening was warm. Vaanathi was suddenly very thirsty.

A cup was set at her place. She raised it to her lips.

Iruli dropped.

The crow came down from the awning in a black rush and struck the cup out of her hand. It flew across the roof. It hit the stone parapet and burst. Buttermilk spattered the mats. Manasa gave a short cry. A servant stumbled back.

"Iruli!" said Sancharan.

The crow landed on the parapet, wings half spread, and screamed.

"That is a very ill-bred bird," said Charudatta, brushing his sleeve.

"It is," Sancharan agreed. "She does not usually break things."

The empress laid a hand on her chest. "Child. Are you hurt?"

"No, Amma. Only startled."

"I am so sorry." Her mother was pale. "It is only a bird. It is only..."

A thin, thready sound cut her off.

It came from beside the parapet. A small grey cat, one of the half-wild creatures that kept the granaries clear, had crept up to the spilled buttermilk. It was lapping it up with quick pink flicks of its tongue. As they watched, it stopped. It stood very still. Then its legs buckled, and it rolled onto its side, and its whole body drew itself into a tight shuddering ball.

Nobody moved.

"Do not touch it," said Anantaraja. He had risen. His voice was very quiet. "Nobody touch it. Nobody leave."

The cat gave one last long shiver and lay still.

The silence on the roof was complete. A lamp spluttered in its bronze stand. Down in the city, a temple bell rang the hour, unhurried and ordinary.

Sancharan crossed to the spilled cup. He crouched and lifted a shard of the clay. He held it close to his face, breathed once, and set it down.

"Bitter root," he said. "A great deal of it."

Vaanathi felt her stomach turn over. The vial was still in her waistband. It pressed against her hip like a stone.

"Who poured it?" said the emperor.

The steward went grey. "I... Your Grace. The cups came from the pantry. They were already on the tray."

"Who set the tray?"

"The pantry girl. Kamali."

They brought Kamali up the stairs. She was fifteen, slight and shaking, with oil-stained hands. She fell on her knees as soon as she saw the emperor. She said she had set out the cups at dusk, as usual, from the big jar. She had seen nobody. Nobody had told her anything. Then, between sobs, she said that the steward of the minister's household had come through the pantry that afternoon, and had been told to wait there for a message. She had thought nothing of it.

Everyone looked at Charudatta.

He rose from his mat. His face was grave and a little tired. He did not look afraid.

"Your Grace," he said, "I will have my steward questioned this hour. I will have every man in my household questioned. If any servant of mine has put hands to this, he will answer for it." He turned to Vaanathi and bowed. "Highness, I am more grieved than I can say."

"Thank you, Minister."

"It would be the act of a fool, to poison the princess in the emperor's own house with my steward standing nearby." A flicker crossed his mouth. "I do not think the one who did it intended me to be foolish. I think they intended me to be blamed."

It was a clever speech. It was exactly the right speech. It left a small cold doubt in her mind, like a grain of sand in a sandal.

"Seal the pantry," said Anantaraja. "Seal the kitchens. Nobody leaves the palace until I say."

## IV

Her mother caught her on the stairs.

Empress Manasa had come down alone, without attendants, one hand pressed to the wall. She was not a woman who ran. She had never in her life been seen to hurry. But she reached the landing before Vaanathi could escape, and caught both her hands, and held them against her own chest as though she were warming them.

"Say you will stay in your rooms," she said.

"Amma."

"Say it. Only for a few days. Let your father's men find whoever did this."

"I cannot promise that."

"I know." Her mother's grip tightened. Her small hands were cold and damp. "I knew you would say so. I raised you to say so, and I am sorry for it." She gave a short shaky laugh. "I wanted a daughter who would not be afraid of the court. I did not think of the rest."

"I am not afraid."

"You are. And so am I." She lifted Vaanathi's bandaged palm and looked at the grey-blue cloth, and her thumb moved over the knot. "That is not a servant's work."

"No."

"It is a fine weave." Manasa lifted her eyes. They were very dark and very kind. "Be careful with the scholar, my dear. I do not say he is a bad man. I say he looks at you the way your father looked at me, when we were young and did not yet know what we were."

"How did he look?"

"As though he were trying very hard not to." Her mother let go of her hands. "Go on. Go and be careful. I shall sit with the cat and say a few words. Somebody should."

"Amma, it is only..."

"Yes, I know. Only a cat." Manasa smiled, and for a moment it was Vaanathi's own smile, tilted and rueful. "That is exactly why somebody should."

## V

The corridor outside the dining roof was dim and empty. Servants hurried past with lamps. Guards were moving in pairs. Somewhere below, a man was shouting.

She had come away because she could not breathe on that roof.

Sancharan found her at the end of the passage, by a narrow window. He stood a short way off, as he always did. He did not speak.

"The cat," she said, at last.

"I know."

"It was only a cat."

"They are all only something. That is how one learns what they were." His voice was low. "I am sorry."

"If the crow had not..." She did not finish.

"If she had not, I would have." He leaned against the wall beside her. "I knew the cup was wrong before it reached your lips. I could taste it in the air. It had the wrong weight."

"The wrong weight?"

"A thing that is about to end has a different feel. It is very slight. Most people do not notice."

"You notice."

"It is my work." He said it without any heat. "Tonight I will tell you what my work is."

She looked at him. In the faint light from the window his face was tired and drawn and very still. He was standing as far from her as the narrow passage allowed. She wondered if he knew that she could see the pulse in his throat.

"You said tonight," she said. "It is tonight."

"So it is."

"Tell me."

"Not here." He glanced up and down the corridor. "In a place where there are no walls. Somewhere nobody can hear."

"Where?"

"I have not decided."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the best I have." A thin smile touched his mouth. "I am in a corridor with a woman who was nearly poisoned at supper. I am allowed one bad answer."

She laughed. It cracked in the middle. He did not move, but something eased in his shoulders.

A sound came from behind them.

It was very soft. It was the scrape of a sandal on stone. She turned her head. At the far end of the corridor, where the lamps did not reach, a shape detached itself from the darkness. It was a man, hooded, in a plain dark wrap. His face was hidden. One hand was held low, close to his thigh, and something in it caught the faint light and gave it back.

Iruli screamed from the rafters.

Sancharan moved. She had never seen him move so fast. He crossed the corridor in one stride and put himself in front of her. But the man was already coming, quick and low and silent, and the blade rose.

It came down towards her heart in a long bright arc.

And everything stopped.
