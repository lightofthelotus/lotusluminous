## I

The petitioner had been talking for an hour.

Vaanathi stopped listening when he said his flooded fields felt like a man buried alive. She understood him perfectly well. She simply could not do anything about it.

She sat where she always sat, on the left of the throne, one step below it. It was close enough that nobody forgot whose daughter she was. It was far enough that nobody thought she had a say. Sunlight came through the high windows in long gold bars. The dust in them never settled.

"The river has been rising since the dark of the moon," the man said. His name was Maadan. He was headman of a village on the eastern bank, and he had walked four days to stand in this hall. His feet were wrapped in rags. "Nobody has seen water do this. It comes up at night. By morning my rice is gone."

This was the third trouble in a month.

First, a granary roof had fallen in at Kolur on a day without wind. Then a ferryman had drowned in a river so calm that children walked across it. Now the eastern fields lay under water. Everyone in the hall knew what people were calling it. Nobody said it aloud.

Vaanathi had read the reports on all three. She had read them twice, at night, with a lamp and a cup of cold buttermilk. They had nothing in common. Kolur lay in the west and the river in the east. The ferryman had been a careful man. The granary had been new. A clerk had written in the margin of one report, *no cause found*. A second clerk had written the same words under another. It was the sort of coincidence that made her want to count things.

Her father listened the way he always did, without moving. Anantaraja was a broad man with a greying beard and a face like a closed door. He had ruled for twenty-six years. In all that time Vaanathi had seen him lose his temper twice.

When Maadan finished, the emperor tilted his head a little.

"Send an officer to the eastern embankments before the month turns," he said. "And find out why this news reached me before it reached the revenue office."

It was a fair answer. It was not what Vaanathi had been about to say.

She wanted to open the crown's grain stores that afternoon. She wanted to feed those families now. An officer would ride out, look at the water, and write a report. The report would go to a clerk. The clerk would send it to another clerk. By then the families would be eating roots.

She drew a breath. The court knew the princess was about to tell what she thought. Their eyes moved to her all at once. Yet, even before whe could open her lips to utter her thoughts as words, she was interrupted.

"Your Grace need not trouble the princess with embankments," said Minister Charudatta.

He said it gently, and he said it before she spoke. The whole hall looked at him. This was only after it looked away from her.

Vaanathi closed her mouth. She had learned years ago how much a closed mouth can say. She let this one say a great deal.

Her father did not look at her. He never did in court. She knew why, though he had never explained it. If he looked at her, the hall would look too. People would start to wonder whether the throne's daughter thought she could advise the throne. So he acted as if she needed no protection. It was kind. It was also maddening. She had never decided which she minded more.

Charudatta was already stepping back into his place. He was a lean man in forest green, with a trimmed moustache and a narrow, watchful face. His smile was very small. He had made it a hundred times in this hall, and it never reached his eyes.

![Charudatta bows before the king as Vaanathi looks on](03_01_Charudata_Meets_King.jpg)

"Next," the herald called.

Nobody came forward.

The herald called again. A clerk by the pillar shifted his feet. Maadan was led away, still holding the end of his torn cloth, and the doors boomed shut behind him.

Then the side doors opened, and Ratnan came in at a run.

## II

The under-steward of the royal archive ran across the emperor's hall faster than anyone was allowed to move in it. His face was the colour of old rice. He forgot to bow until he was three steps past the place where bowing mattered.

"Your Grace." His voice cracked. "Smaran has not opened the archive since yesterday's evening bell. We broke down the door."

![Ratnan bursts into the court hall with news of Smaran's death](01_01_CourtScene.jpg)

Nobody in the hall moved.

"He is dead, Your Grace. In the reading chamber. He was alone."

For a moment there was no sound at all.

Then the whispers started. They were too low to trace to any one mouth. *Shadow.* *Bad hour.* Old Bhargava the treasurer had feared the sky for years. Now he said it plainly, to nobody in particular. "The season has turned wrong."

Vaanathi did not believe in seasons that turned wrong. She did not believe in shadows. But she watched grown men go grey. These were men who led armies and taxed whole provinces. An hour ago they had been arguing about embankments. Now they were afraid of a word.

Fear does not need belief, she thought. It only needs a story old enough that nobody remembers who told it first.

"There will be a quiet inquiry," Anantaraja said. "The keeper served this house for thirty years. He will be honoured. There will be no talk of omens in my court. Is that understood?"

It was not a question. The whispering dropped. It did not stop.

Vaanathi watched the faces instead. Bhargava stared at his hands. The general's jaw was tight. A young officer by the door had gone pale and kept touching the hilt of his sword, as if it might help. Only one man in the hall looked calm. Charudatta stood with his hands folded, his head bowed, and his eyes lowered like a man at prayer. He did not look surprised.

She filed that away. She had nothing to put it beside yet.

Vaanathi rose from her seat.

"Father. I would like to see the chamber."

He still did not look at her. "For what reason?"

"His records touch my work on the border rivers." It was a thin reason. She knew it, and she knew he knew it. "I would like to know that nothing is missing."

A long pause. The bars of sunlight had moved a hand's width across the floor.

"Go," he said.

He said it to the air in front of him. She suspected he had planned it that way. He liked to leave a door unlocked rather than hold one open.

## III

The reading chamber smelled of neem oil and old palm leaf. Underneath, there was something colder. She did not look at it too closely.

Smaran lay beside his writing table as if he had lain down to rest. His writing styluses were set out in their usual order. He kept everything in order. It was most of who he was. The lamp had burned down to a hard black wick.

She looked at the door first.

Two guards stood outside it. They told her twice that it had been bolted from the inside. The iron bar had dropped into its cradle. They had needed a ram to get through. The room had one door. Its one window was no wider than her forearm. Nothing could have come in. Nothing could have gone out.

Ratnan hovered on the threshold, twisting his hands.

"When did you last see him?" she asked.

"At the evening bell, Highness. He said he would finish early." He swallowed. "That was the strange part. He never let a lamp run dry. He used to scold us for wasting oil. And that lamp was lit before dark and burned right down."

"Was he ill?"

"Tired. Not ill." Ratnan looked at the body, and quickly away. "He looks peaceful. I keep thinking that is wrong. I cannot say why."

She knew what he meant. She did not say so.

She looked at the table instead. A palm leaf lay in front of the empty seat. A stylus rested beside it, and the imprints on the leaf was still dark. Smaran had been writing when he stopped. But the leaf in front of him was blank. There was only a faint imprint on the wood beneath it, where the stylus had carved on the leaf.

He had not been writing on that leaf. He had been writing on another one, and it was not here.

"Did anyone move anything?" she asked.

"No, Highness. The guards would not let us. They only broke the door and looked."

She bent over the old man's hands. They were stained to the second knuckle, as they had always been. Close to his fingers, the air had a faint bitter smell. It was not preservative fluid. It was not neem. She breathed it in once and stood up. Whatever it was, she did not want to know it was there.

She had known Smaran all her life. When she was nine, he had shown her that the old ledgers were wrapped in cloth soaked in neem oil, to keep the silverfish away. He had let her sit on this floor for whole afternoons, reading old records the way other children listened to stories. She remembered the weight of the bundles in her lap. She remembered that he never once told her to be quiet.

She knelt at the shelf beside his table. It held the great genealogy of the royal line. She knew that shelf better than her own bedroom. She had run a finger down those leaves a hundred times.

Something was wrong.

It took her a moment to see it. One bundle was thinner than it should be. A single leaf had been torn out, close to the cord. The edge had been smoothed flat. The bundle had been pushed back into place as though nothing had happened.

Someone had taken a page from the book of her own family. And they had taken care that nobody would notice.

She knew which leaf it had been. It was the one at the very top, before the names began. She had always skipped it as a child. It held only a few lines of old script, and a drawing she had never looked at properly.

"Highness?" said Ratnan.

"Nothing." She stood and brushed off her hands. "Have the body carried to his family. See that the lamp is left exactly where it is."

"Yes, Highness."

She told the guards nothing about the leaf. She was not sure why.

## IV

Charudatta was waiting in the corridor outside.

She was not surprised. He had a way of being wherever a conversation was about to start.

"Your father is generous," he said. "I would not rely on it again. Grief is a poor guide, Princess. It sees patterns that are not there."

"And what do you see, Minister?"

"A tired old man with a weak heart, in a room he never left." He smiled. "The inquiry will say so."

"I am sure it will."

"You will forgive me for saying so," he went on. "The court is frightened. A frightened court needs one steady voice. Your father is that voice. It would help him if the rest of us were quiet."

"Do you include me in the rest of us?"

"I include everyone, Princess. That is what makes it fair."

He bowed. It was a little too shallow to be respect and a little too deep to be rude. He had spent thirty years learning exactly how far a bow could tilt. Then he walked away, and she watched him go.

She did not believe him. She did not think he believed himself either.

There had been a moment in the chamber, while she knelt at the shelf, when she had heard a step behind her. It had been Charudatta, standing in the doorway. He had not come to look at the dead man. He had been looking at her hands.

She had not thought much of it then. She thought of it now.

## V

Nila was waiting in Vaanathi's rooms, sitting on the window ledge with her knees drawn up. She had her own way of arriving. She was always there before you, and she never seemed to have hurried.

"I heard," she said. "I am sorry. I know what he was to you."

"Who told you?"

"Everyone. The cooks. The washermen. A donkey." Nila's mouth turned up and then straightened. "It is all over the palace. Half of them say it was the shadow. The other half say it was the minister."

"Which half do you belong to?"

"The half that listens and says nothing."

Vaanathi sat down heavily on her bed. Late light came through the lattice in squares. Somewhere below, a servant was singing a work song, off key. It sounded absurdly ordinary.

She told Nila everything. The locked door. The torn leaf. Charudatta in the corridor. Nila listened without interrupting, her chin on her knees. When Vaanathi finished, she was quiet for a while.

"You will go back," she said.

"Tonight."

"I thought so." Nila unfolded herself from the ledge. "Then I should tell you something. It is probably nothing."

"Tell me."

"Tonight is a ganda." Nila said the word as one says the name of a dangerous dog. "It is a bad joint between two lunar houses. A crossing where one ends and the next begins. My grandmother would not start a journey on such a night. She would not even plant a seed."

"Your grandmother also thought owls carried messages."

"Some of them do." Nila did not smile. "I am not telling you to believe it. I am telling you that it is the kind of night when things begin, and not well."

"Things have already begun."

"Then they will go on." Nila looked out of the window at the deepening sky. "There is something else. The scholar from the south arrives tonight."

Vaanathi frowned. "What scholar?"

"Your father sent for him after the flood at Kolur. A man who reads omens. He is coming quietly, by the river gate, after dark." Nila turned back. "Who explains omens quietly, Vaanathi?"

"Someone the emperor does not want the court to hear."

"Yes."

"Then I should meet him."

"You will. Your father will see to that." Nila's voice had gone dry. "He always arranges for you to meet people he has already decided about."

Vaanathi laughed. It surprised her. It was the first time all day.

Nila rang for a servant and, without being asked, ordered rice and curd and a plate of fried plantain. "You have not eaten since dawn," she said. "I counted. You eat the same when you are angry as when you are sad. Nothing."

"I am not sad."

"Then you are very angry." Nila pressed a bowl into her hands. "Eat. A princess who faints in a corridor is no use to anyone, least of all to a dead man."

Vaanathi ate. The curd was cool and sour. It was the first thing all day that tasted of anything. She noticed that Nila did not eat. Nila never seemed to eat much. She did not seem to sleep much either. Whenever Vaanathi woke in the night, she would find Nila awake in the next room, sitting at the window and watching the sky as though waiting for it to say something.

"You are very good company in a crisis."

"I am very good company at all times," said Nila. "Crises just make it easier to notice."

## VI

Vaanathi went back that night, alone, with a small oil lamp. She told nobody, not even Nila.

The corridor was empty. The guards had been moved to the front gate for the scholar's arrival. The chamber door was shut again. Ratnan had sealed it with a disc of wax, in the proper way. It would stay shut until someone with authority gave leave to open it. She had watched that done a hundred times. She knew the royal seal. It showed a sunburst under a crown.

This was not only that.

She raised the lamp. The flame caught the wax and made it glow. Beside the royal seal, someone had made a second mark. It was small and careful. It had been cut into the wax with a fine point, not pressed in. It was a circle with a hub and spokes, like a wheel. One slice had been taken cleanly out of it, as if someone had bitten the edge.

Vaanathi had never seen it before.

She had grown up in every corner of this palace. She was certain of that. She had hidden in its cellars and climbed its towers. She had read its oldest records on the floor of the room behind this door. She knew its carvings, its seals, its crests. She did not know this.

She tried the thought from the other side. Perhaps a guard had done it. Perhaps Ratnan had, in a fit of grief. But a guard would not know how to cut a wheel so cleanly. Ratnan would not have dared to touch the royal wax. And neither of them would have left it behind for her to find.

That was the part that chilled her. The mark had not been hidden. It had been placed where the next person to come would see it. Perhaps that person was meant to be her.

She stood in the empty corridor for a long time. The lamp shook in her hand. She noticed it, and she did not like it.

The wax had been sealed at dusk. The guards had left at dusk. Nobody had come down this corridor since. Yet someone had stood here, in the dark, with a fine point and steady hands. They had left their mark beside the king's own.

![Vaanathi sees the symbol](01_06_Vaanathi_Sees_Seal.jpg)

Whatever the court had decided to call this, it was not finished.

Far off, near the river gate, a bell began to ring. It was the signal for a visitor. The southern scholar had arrived.

Vaanathi cupped her hand around the flame and went to see the arrival or whatever it meant to be.
