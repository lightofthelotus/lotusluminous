Smaran lit the lamp at dusk and did not put it out.

The archive was quiet. The guards had gone to their supper. Outside the one small window the sky went from orange to grey. He sat at his writing table with a palm leaf in front of him and the lamp close by, the way he always sat.

The leaf was old. He had found it three days ago, tucked between two border treaties that nobody had read in a hundred years. It smelled of neem oil and dust. In one corner someone had drawn a small round mark. It was a wheel with a wedge cut out of one side.

He had seen that mark before. He was sure of it. Once, as a young man, in a ledger he was not allowed to open. Then never again. Now it was here. It was on a letter too, and on the old bell in the treasury.

Smaran was seventy-one. He had spent thirty years keeping other men's secrets in order. He was not afraid of secrets. But this one frightened him a little. It had waited a very long time for someone to read it.

That afternoon he had hidden his letters above the scribes' hall, where nobody ever looked up. It was the only clever thing he had done in years.

The leaf grew warm under the lamp. It gave off a faint, bitter smell. Old leaves do that, he told himself.

He rubbed his eyes. The flame was burning low. He ought to fill it. He did not move.

He thought of Vaanathi. She had been a girl who sat on this floor for whole afternoons. She was a woman now. If she ever came looking for him, she would know where to look. He had made sure of that.

There was a soft scrape from the wall behind the shelves. It was the old service door. Everyone believed it had been bricked shut.

Smaran did not turn around. He was not a brave man. He picked up his brush and kept writing, slowly, in his best hand.

Behind the shelves, a hand rested on an iron ring. Whoever it belonged to was in no hurry at all.
