Before there were kings, before there were names for the rivers or the roads across the dark between worlds, there was only balance, the slow, ceaseless turning of forces older than memory, each holding its place, each keeping its measure, asking nothing of the others.

Within that balance, unbidden, Bhumi came to hold what no other world had held before it: soil to receive, water to nourish, fire to warm, air to carry breath, and the vast quiet of space to hold room for what had not yet been born. The five moved together here, not against each other, and where they moved together, life did not merely survive. It multiplied, forgot itself, remade itself, and asked questions no other world had lived long enough to ask.

The Nine did not seek this. They only watched it happen, the way one watches a flame catch in dry grass, and having watched, they could not turn away. An accord was made, one that has outlasted every empire built beneath it: none would touch what Bhumi was becoming. Each would only guard it, lending their long, patient rhythms to its turning, one to its harvests, one to its tides, one to its fevers and its fortunes, one to the slow arithmetic of consequence that mortals would come to call karma. Not gods to be begged. Not powers to be worshipped. Only a balance, keeping a fragile, improbable world from tipping into the dark.

But balance, once understood, can be read. And what can be read can, in time, be bent.

It did not take mortals long. A few generations of watching the sky was enough to notice its patterns; a few more to guess at its causes; and among the many who watched only to understand, there were always a few who watched to take. They could not touch the Nine themselves, no mortal hand reaches that far. But the Nine had left anchors on Bhumi's own soil, in stone and bloodline and memory, so their influence could be felt without their presence being required. And where there is an anchor, there is always, eventually, a hand that reaches for it.

This is not the story of gods. It is the story of what mortals do with the space gods leave behind.
