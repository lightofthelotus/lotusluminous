## I

The dispute was a small one. Two farmers, a shared channel, a diverted flow of water that had left one field green and the other cracked and pale. It was the kind of case the palace usually settled in an afternoon, without ceremony, without an audience worth mentioning.

Veeran asked to sit in.

Anantaraja did not object. Why would he. A visiting prince curious about how a foreign court settled its small quarrels was, on its face, nothing more than good manners dressed as interest. Veeran sat a respectful distance from the throne, said nothing for the first hour, and listened the way a man listens who intends to remember every word.

The two farmers argued their case the way farmers always did. Loudly. With old grievances folded into new ones. The elder man claimed a right older than the younger man's own birth. The younger man claimed the elder had grown greedy with age, taking more than his fair share simply because no one had yet found the courage to stop him.

Anantaraja listened, weighed, and was halfway to a ruling when Veeran spoke for the first time.

"Might I ask one question," he said, mild, deferential, exactly the tone of a guest who knows he is a guest. "Not of the men. Of the channel itself."

Anantaraja gestured for him to continue.

"How old is the diversion stone. The one that splits the flow between the two fields."

The elder farmer answered before the younger could. Older than his grandfather, he said. Older than anyone living could properly account for.

"Then the stone has moved," Veeran said, "or the ground beneath it has. A channel does not simply decide, of its own accord, to favour one field over another after generations of dividing the water evenly. Something shifted. I would want to know what, before I decided who was owed an apology."

It was, Anantaraja admitted afterward, a better question than the one he himself had been about to ask. The court engineer was sent to examine the stone. The stone, it turned out, had indeed shifted, nudged out of true by a season of hard rain two years past, favouring the elder farmer's field by inches no one had thought to measure. Neither man had lied. Neither man had cheated. The water itself had simply changed its mind, the way water sometimes does, and no one had noticed until the difference grew too large to ignore.

The stone was reset. Both fields would drink evenly again by the next season.

The farmers left satisfied, which was rare enough on its own. And the court, watching, understood that the visiting prince had not merely observed a ruling. He had improved one.

## II

It happened again the following week. And the week after that.

Never anything large. A boundary dispute between two weaving families. A disagreement over which merchant's stall had the older claim to a particular corner of the market. Small matters, the kind that filled the ordinary rhythm of governing a kingdom, and in every one of them, Veeran found some small question no one else had thought to ask, and the question, more often than not, cracked the case open cleanly.

Charudatta noticed before anyone said it aloud.

"He is very good at this," he observed to Anantaraja, after the fourth such case. He said it the way he said most things, mild, unhurried, giving away nothing of what sat beneath it.

"He is," Anantaraja agreed, and there was real warmth in it, the particular warmth of a ruler who has spent decades governing largely alone and has only just discovered how pleasant it is to be occasionally surprised by someone else's mind.

Charudatta said nothing further that day. He had learned, across thirty years of service, that the moment to raise an objection was never the moment everyone else was still delighted.

## III

The council chamber was a different matter entirely, and this was where Charudatta's patience began, quietly, to fray.

The subject was banditry along the new trade road, the one the treaty had opened between Anantha and Samudrapuram. Three caravans robbed in as many months. The council debated troop placement, toll stations, the usual remedies for a problem as old as roads themselves.

"If I may," Veeran said, and by now no one in the room thought to question whether he might.

He had, he explained, faced the same trouble along Samudrapuram's own coastal roads a decade past. Toll stations invited exactly the wrong kind of attention, he said, since they announced to any bandit with eyes where the wealth would be moving and when. Better to move the patrols themselves, unpredictably, in numbers too small to look official and too well-armed to look worth the trouble.

It was sound advice. Everyone in the room could see that it was sound advice. The council adopted it within the hour, and the raids, over the following weeks, slowed to nothing.

This, too, made sense. The road was half his own kingdom's business. No one begrudged him an opinion on a problem his own caravans had suffered from as well.

It was the matter after that which sat differently.

The council turned, as it did every year, to the question of the northern grain tax, a purely domestic matter, older than Veeran's own visit, older than the treaty itself. And Veeran, without being asked, offered an opinion on that too.

Charudatta watched Anantaraja consider it. Watched him weigh it seriously, the way he weighed the counsel of men who had served this house for decades. Watched him, in the end, adjust the tax rate half a measure in the direction Veeran had suggested.

Nothing about it was improper. Nothing about it could be named, in open council, as anything other than a guest offering a thoughtful outside perspective. And yet Charudatta left the chamber that evening with a tightness behind his ribs he had not felt in years, the particular discomfort of watching a door open slowly enough that no single moment of the opening could be called an intrusion.

## IV

Vaanathi felt it too, though she came to it from a different direction entirely.

She had taken to reviewing the council's minutes each week, a habit inherited from Smaran, who had taught her that the truest record of a court's priorities was never in its speeches but in what got written down afterward and what did not. She noticed, reading three months of minutes in a single sitting, how often one name now appeared in the margins. A note here. A suggestion there. Small at first. Then less small.

She raised it with her father directly, which was not something she did lightly.

"He has an opinion on the grain tax now," she said. "The grain tax has nothing to do with Samudrapuram."

"He has good instincts," Anantaraja said. "I am permitted to value good instincts, Vaanathi, regardless of where they were born."

"I am not asking you to distrust him. I am asking you to notice how quickly the distance between guest and counsellor is closing, without anyone having formally decided it should."

Her father studied her a moment, the way he studied most things that unsettled him without quite convincing him. "You sound," he said, not unkindly, "like Charudatta."

"I am aware," Vaanathi said, "of exactly how alarming that should sound to both of us."

## V

She found Charudatta in the archive two days later, of all places, examining a ledger with the particular scowl he reserved for numbers that refused to agree with each other.

"You are reading the minutes as well," he said, without looking up. It was not a question.

"I am."

"Then we are, for what I believe is the first time in either of our memories, in complete agreement about something." He set the ledger down and regarded her properly, and something in his face, careful as ever, allowed itself one small, wry crack. "I have spent fifteen years, Princess, finding fault with nearly everything you have done. You have spent the same fifteen years finding fault with nearly everything I have said. I confess I did not expect a foreign prince's charm to be the thing that finally united us."

"I would not call it united."

![Vaanathi and Charudatta find common ground](16-02-vaanthi-charudataa.jpg)

"Call it what you like. I call it common ground, and I intend to make use of it while it lasts." He rose and came around the low writing bench between them, unhurried. "He has not done a single improper thing, Vaanathi. That is what troubles me most. A man who overreaches carelessly can be caught and corrected. A man who overreaches this well leaves nothing to correct. Only a slow accumulation of trust that no single moment earned outright."

"My father called it good instincts."

"Your father is not wrong that they are good instincts." Charudatta's mouth thinned. "That is precisely what makes them worth watching."

They stood a moment in a silence that neither of them quite knew how to occupy, two people who had spent a decade and a half sharpening themselves against each other, discovering, awkwardly, that the same blade could be turned outward.

"I do not enjoy agreeing with you," Vaanathi said finally.

"The feeling," Charudatta said, "is entirely mutual. I recommend we not make a habit of it."

"Agreed."

Neither of them smiled. Both of them, walking away in opposite directions a moment later, very nearly did.

## VI

Kanaka, meanwhile, had found a project of her own, and her project was Sancharan.

She had decided, within her first week at Anantapuram, that the southern scholar was the single most serious person she had ever met, and had further decided that seriousness of that particular calibre existed only to be tested.

"You never laugh," she informed him, in the archive, where she had taken to visiting with a regularity that had nothing to do with any interest in ledgers. "I have watched you for three days now. Not once."

"I have found several things amusing," Sancharan said, not looking up from the leaf in front of him.

"Found them amusing where. Inside your own head, where no one else is permitted to witness it?" She perched herself on the edge of his writing bench, which he had asked her, twice now, not to do. "That does not count. Amusement that never reaches your face is just a very well-hidden headache."

"I was not aware amusement required an audience."

"It requires a face, Scholar. Yours simply refuses to cooperate." She tilted her head, studying him the frank, unhurried way she studied everything. "I am going to make you laugh before I leave this city. I have decided. You may consider it a diplomatic objective, equal in importance to the treaty."

"I wish you luck with the negotiation."

"I do not need luck." She hopped down from the bench, entirely unbothered by his lack of encouragement. "I have never once failed at anything I have formally decided to do."

She left him with that, and returned the following day with a small carved puzzle box from her own kingdom, insisting he solve it in front of her, narrating his every failed attempt with such open delight that two passing clerks stopped to watch. She returned the day after that with a story about her brother's childhood that Sancharan suspected, correctly, was designed purely to embarrass Veeran rather than entertain anyone else. She returned a third time simply to sit across from him in silence for the better part of an hour, watching him work, until he finally asked, with the particular patience of a man who had lived several centuries longer than his current appearance suggested, whether she intended to make a habit of it.

"Possibly," she said. "You are restful to be near. Most people talk too much. You do not talk at all, which leaves a great deal of room for me to talk instead."

He found, somewhat to his own surprise, that he did not entirely mind it.

## VII

The garden, that particular afternoon, was at its best. The rains had passed through twice that week already, leaving everything green and startled and dripping.

Veeran found Vaanathi near the western pavilion, where she often retreated when the council chamber grew too crowded with opinions she had not asked for.

"I am told," he said, "that this is where a person comes when they wish to be found by no one in particular."

"You have found me regardless."

"I am aware of the irony." He fell into step beside her, unhurried, the two of them moving along the gravel path without either quite deciding to walk together. "I wished to ask you something, and I did not think the council chamber the proper place for it."

![Veeran and Vaanathi walk through the garden](16-04-garden-walk.jpg)

"That sounds ominous."

"It is not meant to. I wished to ask whether I have overstepped." He said it plainly, without the practised ease he usually wore like a second set of robes. "I have watched your minister's face grow tighter every week I remain in your council chamber. I have watched yours grow tighter alongside his, though you hide it rather better than he does. I did not come to this city to become a fixture in its governance, Vaanathi. I came to sign a treaty and go home. If my presence has become a burden your court did not ask to carry, I would rather hear it from you directly than continue guessing."

She considered him a long moment, surprised despite herself by the directness of it.

"You ask good questions," she said finally. "That is the trouble. My father values good questions more than he values caution, and you have an endless supply of both."

"Would you have me stop asking them?"

"I would have you understand why it unsettles us when you do not." She stopped walking, turning to face him properly. "Charudatta and I do not agree on very much, Veeran. We have not agreed on very much in fifteen years. But we agree on this. Trust that arrives too easily is trust no one has properly tested. I do not doubt your intentions. I do not yet know them well enough to doubt or trust them at all. That is the honest answer, and I suspect it is not the one you were hoping for."

"It is a more honest answer than I expected," he said, "which I find I value considerably more than the one I was hoping for."

They walked on in a silence that had, for the first time since his arrival, nothing performed about it at all.

## VIII

Behind them, on the far side of the garden's tallest hedge, Kanaka had decided the afternoon called for a dance lesson.

"It is a coastal step," she informed Sancharan, who had made the mistake of accompanying her that far and had not yet found a graceful way to excuse himself. "Everyone in Samudrapuram learns it as a child. You turn, and then you turn again, and on the third turn you are meant to catch your partner's hand exactly as they turn back toward you."

"I am not your partner."

"You are the only person currently present. That makes you my partner by simple elimination." She demonstrated the first turn, light and quick, robes catching the wind. "Come. It is not difficult. Even a scholar can manage three turns."

He turned, mostly to satisfy her rather than himself, and misjudged, badly, exactly where the third turn was meant to end.

She misjudged it too, laughing too hard by the second turn to properly finish the third, and the two of them collided in the particular graceless way of two people who had each been more confident than skilled, Kanaka's hand catching his sleeve instead of his hand, her own balance going first, his following a half-beat after in the doomed effort to catch her.

They went down together in an undignified heap of silk and startled laughter, exactly as Vaanathi and Veeran rounded the hedge from the other side.

![Kanaka and Sancharan collide in the garden](16-05-garden-collapse.jpg)

For one long, suspended moment, no one moved. Kanaka lay half across Sancharan's chest, her hand still tangled in his sleeve, both of them breathless with laughter that had not yet noticed it had an audience. Sancharan looked up first, and found Vaanathi standing very still at the mouth of the path, her face arranged into something that was trying very hard, and not quite succeeding, to look like nothing at all.

Veeran, beside her, said nothing. He did not need to. His raised eyebrow said everything required of him.

Kanaka was the first to recover her voice. She sat up, entirely unbothered, brushing grass from her sleeve as though she had planned the whole tumble from the start.

"He is a terrible dancer," she announced to the garden at large. "I thought you should all know."

Sancharan, still on the ground, did not immediately find an answer to that. Vaanathi, still at the mouth of the path, did not immediately move.

The garden, unhelpfully, stayed exactly as green and startled and dripping as it had been a moment before, entirely indifferent to the four people now standing in it, each of them working out, in their own private and unhurried way, exactly what they had just walked into.

No one, for the moment, said another word.

## IX

It was Veeran who spoke first.

"Kanaka." His voice had lost its ease entirely. He lent his hand towards hers. "Get up."

She did, unhurried, brushing grass from her sleeve with the same lack of concern she had shown a moment before. "It was a dance step, brother. Badly executed. You need not summon the treaty delegation."

"I am not summoning anyone." He did not look at his sister as he said it. He looked at Sancharan, still rising from the gravel, and something in the look was no longer the polished courtesy he wore for councils and dinners. It was older than that. Plainer. "You will forgive me, Scholar, if I ask what exactly I walked in on."

"A misjudged turn," Sancharan said. "Nothing more."

"My sister does not misjudge turns. I have watched her dance since she was old enough to stand upright." Veeran's jaw was tight. "I would like to understand why she is currently laughing on the ground of a garden she was not laughing on ten minutes ago, and why you are the reason."

"Veeran." Kanaka's voice had an edge in it now too, though hers was closer to exasperation than alarm. "I threw myself at a man who did not ask to catch me, and he failed to catch me well. That is the whole of the crime. You are embarrassing yourself."

Vaanathi had not moved from the mouth of the path. She had heard almost none of it.

"You are not hurt," she said. It came out strangely, too flat, aimed at Kanaka but not quite landing as a question.

Kanaka blinked at her. "Hurt? From a stumble in wet grass?" She laughed again, though more carefully this time, reading something in Vaanathi's face she could not place. "I have taken worse falls off my own front steps, Princess. I promise you I am entirely whole."

"Nothing happened," Vaanathi said. "When you touched him. Nothing at all."

"I touched his garment," Kanaka said slowly, the way one speaks to someone who has asked a question in the wrong language. "What did you expect to happen?"

Vaanathi did not answer that. She could not answer that, not with Veeran standing close enough to hear every word, not with Kanaka watching her now with open, honest confusion rather than any of the delight she had worn a moment ago. The question that actually sat behind her teeth had nothing to do with dancing, but everything to do with an unmaking fire which almost cut off her hand few days ago. And there was no way to ask it aloud that would not require explaining a great deal more than any of them were owed.

"I only meant," she said, forcing something calmer into her voice, "that it looked a rather hard fall."

"It was a soft fall," Kanaka said, still watching her carefully. "Grass is forgiving. I am not certain what fall you were imagining, Princess, but I promise you it was considerably less dramatic than whatever is currently happening on your face."

Sancharan, standing now, said nothing at all. He understood, better than either sibling in front of him, exactly what question Vaanathi had actually been asking, and understood too that he could not answer it here, in daylight, in front of a prince who had already decided to be suspicious of him for entirely the wrong reason.

"The fault is mine," Kanaka said again, filling the silence the way she seemed to fill every silence, turning to address her brother properly now. "I have spent a week trying to make this man.." she paused searching for the word which would better describe what was it that she had been attempting to do with Sancharan at all, and then continued.

"well.. a man! I have failed at every attempt so cleanly that today I resorted to physical fun, and even that appears to have completely failed, since he is still not laughing. Not to mention, it made both of you very angered for all the wrong reasons." She looked pointedly at Sancharan. "Are you laughing, Scholar?"

"No," Sancharan admitted.

"See. A complete failure on every measure. Veeran, I would like it noted for the record that this was entirely my doing, and that the poor man's only crime was being draped in a garment within my reach."

Veeran did not look entirely satisfied. He held Sancharan's gaze a moment longer than courtesy required, the particular measuring look of a brother filing away a debt he had not yet decided how to collect. But he said nothing further, and after a moment, with visible effort, he let the matter go.

"We should return," he said instead, to Vaanathi, though his eyes stayed on Sancharan a heartbeat longer than his words did. "Before the garden produces any further diplomatic incidents."

Vaanathi did not argue. She turned to go, and did not trust herself to look back at Sancharan as she did, because she was no longer certain what her own face would say if she gave it the chance.

## X

She found him that evening, in the archive, alone, exactly where she had known she would.

He looked up when she entered, and something in his face settled, as though he had been expecting this visit since the garden and had simply been waiting for the day to finish deciding when to send her.

"You did not ask your question this afternoon," he said.

"I could not ask my question this afternoon. There was an audience." She did not sit. She stood on the other side of his low writing bench, arms folded, and found, to her own irritation, that she had rehearsed nothing. "The rings did nothing to her, Sancharan. Nothing at all. She fell across your chest laughing, her hand in your garment, and your rings sat there as quiet as river stones. Mine tried to take my hand off for reaching for yours over a border ledger."

"I know."

"You know." She heard her own voice climbing and did not entirely try to stop it. "That is becoming a habit of yours. I ask you something that costs me a great deal to ask, and you answer me with two words and expect that to be the whole of the transaction."

"It is not the whole of it." He set down the leaf he had been working, giving her his full attention in the unhurried way he gave most things, which she had once found steadying and now, in this particular mood, found almost unbearable. "The rings did not react to Kanaka because Kanaka has nothing bound to them to react to. She is a stranger to what I carry, in the truest sense of the word. You are not. You have not been a stranger to it since the night they answered you instead."

"That is not an answer. That is a very elegant way of not answering."

"Then ask me more plainly, and I will try to be more plain in return."

She opened her mouth, and what came out was not, in the end, plain at all.

![Vaanathi confronts Sancharan in the archive](16-06-archival-confrontation.jpg)

"You collect them," she said. "Has anyone ever told you that. Amudha loved you enough to die for it. Kanaka cannot walk past you without inventing a reason to sit beside your work. I have watched you be kind to precisely no one on purpose, and somehow every woman within reach of you ends up rearranging her whole afternoon around the privilege of your company. I do not know if you do it deliberately. I have decided, most days, that you do not. But I confess there are other days where I am considerably less certain, and I would like, for once, to hear you account for it instead of simply enduring it with that infuriating patience of yours."

He was quiet a moment, and she watched him actually consider the accusation rather than simply wait for it to finish.

"I have had a very long time to think about why people find their way to me," he said. "I do not think it is deliberate, on my part or on theirs. I think it is curiosity, mostly. A stillness that looks like it might be hiding something interesting behind it, and people, being people, cannot help wanting to find out what. Amudha wanted to know what made a stranger so quiet in a market full of noise. Kanaka wants to know what it would take to make a serious man laugh. Even you, Vaanathi, came to that archive floor the first time because a stranger's silence was a puzzle you could not walk past unsolved." He held her eyes. "I do not think I collect anyone. I think I am simply a door that happens to look shut, and shut doors have always tempted the curious more than open ones."

"That is a very tidy explanation for something that keeps happening to you regardless."

"It is the truest one I have."

She sat, finally, across from him on the floor mat opposite his own, some of the fight going out of her shoulders though none of it left her voice entirely. "I do not know what to do with how human this feels," she said, quieter now. "You keep telling me what you are. Older than the stars have names for. A guardian, passing through, on a scale I cannot properly picture. And then you notice when I have not eaten. You remember which cushion I prefer. You sit here being jealous of nothing and possessive of no one and somehow still manage to make me feel every single ordinary, undignified thing a woman is supposed to feel about a man who will not simply tell her where she stands."

"I am sorry for the undignified feeling," he said, and there was no mockery in it at all. "I have never once been sorry for the rest."

She looked at him a long moment. Then, before she had entirely decided to, she asked the question she had actually come to ask, the one hiding beneath all the others.

"If it came to it," she said. "Her or me. Which."

He did not answer quickly, and she was glad of that, because a quick answer would have told her it was rehearsed, and she did not want a rehearsed answer.

"You," he said finally.

"Why."

"Because Kanaka would grieve me and go on to a full life, untouched by anything I carry, exactly as she should. You would not have that luxury even if I vanished from this palace tonight and never returned. You already know what I am. You have already been struck by what protects me. Whatever happens from here, you are bound to the weight of it whether I choose you or not, and I would rather stand beside the person already carrying that weight than leave her to carry it alone while I spared myself the discomfort of choosing." He said it plainly, the way he said most true things, without reaching for anything softer to wrap it in. "What I feel with you is not romance, Vaanathi. I would not insult you by dressing it as romance. It is simple arithmetic. You are already inside the cost. I will not pretend otherwise for either of our comfort."

She sat with that a while, turning it over, and found, to her own surprise, that she believed every word of it, and that the believing hurt rather less than she had expected it to.

"I put together so many probable answers to my questions. And even my last probable guess was not even close to what you answered just now," she said finally.

"I did warn you."

"You did." She almost smiled, despite everything the afternoon had cost her. "In a year from now, I hope to be better at predicting your answers, Sancharan."

"I will do what I can," he said, "to earn your precision."

She left the archive that evening lighter than she had arrived, though she could not, walking back through the darkened corridors, have said exactly why.
