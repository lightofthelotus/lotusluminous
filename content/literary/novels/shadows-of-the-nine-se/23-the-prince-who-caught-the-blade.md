## I

He came up from the harbour on foot.

She had expected a procession. Every prince she had ever heard of arrived on an elephant, under a canopy, to the sound of drums. This one walked up the main road of Anantapuram at midmorning with a dozen sailors behind him and a single banner of sea-green silk. He did not look at the crowds. He looked at the houses. He looked at the people in the doorways, at the thin faces and the lamp-blackened lintels. And when a woman with a basket stumbled in front of him, he caught her elbow and carried the basket himself for fifty paces, talking to her the whole way.

Vaanathi watched from the gatehouse roof, with Ratnan crouched at her elbow like a nervous cat.

"Is he always like that?" she said.

"I have no idea, Highness. I only know that the people are following him."

It was true. The crowd had begun to drift in his wake, silent at first, then murmuring. Children ran alongside. A man who had stood grey-faced at a door for eleven days stepped out into the road and fell in beside the sailors, as if drawn on a string.

"What is he carrying?" said Vaanathi, narrowing her eyes.

"A sack, Highness."

"What is in it?"

"Rice." Ratnan's voice was awed. "He has twenty ships in the harbour. The sailors are saying it is all rice. They are unloading it now. He sent a message to the harbour master that it was a gift to the city, and that the price was nothing at all."

She leaned on the parapet. She did not know what she felt. It was a curious mixture. She was relieved. She was suspicious. She was annoyed at being relieved.

The prince had reached the gate. He stopped in the shadow of the archway and looked up. His eyes found her at once, as though he had known exactly where she would be. He smiled.

He was perhaps twenty-six. He was not very tall, and he was lean as a rope, with the loose easy build of a man who spends his days on moving decks. His skin was a warm bronze, burned by salt wind. His hair was short and dark and curled tightly. He had a strong jaw and a neat moustache, and a face that was made for laughing. He wore a fine cotton wrap edged in sea-green, a single string of pearls at his throat, and nothing else. A pair of plain gold bands circled his upper arms.

"Princess Vaanathi," he said. His voice carried up the stones, warm and easy. "I would know you anywhere."

"We have not met."

"No. But I have heard the legend." He bowed, a real bow, from the waist, with a flourish of one hand. "They say you stopped a river from turning red. They say you spoke to a village and the village believed you. I came across a sea to see if it was true."

"It was nothing."

"I doubt that very much." The smile widened. "May I come up?"

She could not think of a reason to say no. She heard herself say, "Please."

He took the stairs three at a time. He came out on the roof flushed and laughing, with his hair blown wild. A small, round girl came puffing up behind him, clutching her skirts in both fists, and stopped dead at the sight of the view.

"Oh," said the girl. "Oh, it is *so big.*"

"Kanaka," said the prince, with brotherly despair. "Manners."

"I am sorry. I am so sorry. I have never seen so many roofs." She turned, flushed and radiant, to Vaanathi, and made a clumsy bow that sent a pearl hair-ornament sliding out of her loose wavy hair. "I am Kanaka. I am his sister. He said I was not to talk. I always talk. You are even more beautiful than he said."

"Kanaka."

"Oh, I have done it again." She clapped a hand to her mouth, and her dimple deepened. Her eyes were bright and quick and unguarded, and she wore her pearls, her hip chain and her sea-green band as if she had thrown them on in a hurry. She had a small shell stud in her nose. "I am very happy to be here."

Vaanathi found, to her own surprise, that she was smiling.

## II

The throne hall had been swept and hung with jasmine. Her father sat on the gold seat with a shawl round his shoulders, and her mother sat in the empress's chair beside him. Manasa had put on her best blue wrap. She had also put on a small wry smile, as a woman puts on a jewel to cover a bruise.

Prince Veeran knelt on the lowest step of the dais. He did not do it as the ambassadors did, with a stiff bend of the knee. He went down on both knees and lowered his head until it almost touched the carpet. Then he rose and looked up, with open, brilliant eyes.

"Your Grace. I bring greetings from the court of Samudrapuram, and from my father, who is too old to travel. I bring twenty ships of grain. I ask that you take them, not as a gift, but as a debt that I would like to owe."

A murmur rose in the hall. Charudatta stood a little behind the throne, in forest green, and the corners of his mouth turned up in the faintest quiet satisfaction.

"You are generous," said Anantaraja. His voice was frail. "I do not understand the debt."

"It is a poor one, Your Grace. I have come to ask your daughter's hand. I know it is a bold request. I have thought of nothing else for a year." He bowed his head. "It seemed to me that if I asked, I should also be able to give."

"A year?" said Manasa gently.

"I heard of the princess long before I knew her name." He looked at her, and his face was suddenly very young. "A trader came to our port. He had been in Anantapuram. He said there was a girl who sat at the left of the throne, and said nothing, and missed nothing. He said that when she looked at a petitioner, the petitioner told the truth." He smiled. "I have a great respect for people who listen. We have so few of them at sea."

Vaanathi felt the colour come up under her skin. She was glad that the lamps were low.

It was a pretty speech. It was a very pretty speech. It was warm, and funny, and humble. It contained not a single thing that she could argue with, and she found, listening, that she wanted to believe it. She was twenty-four. She had sat for twenty years in the shadow of the throne. Nobody had ever said such things to her in public.

She glanced at the back of the hall.

Sancharan stood by a pillar, with his arms folded and the crow on his shoulder. He was not looking at her. He was looking at the prince. His face had not moved. But his eyes had narrowed by a bare hair, and the muscle at the corner of his jaw was tight.

The prince followed her glance. His eyes rested on the tall grave figure for a long moment. Then he smiled.

"And you must be the scholar," he said.

"I am called Sancharan."

"An excellent name. It means one who walks, I believe." Veeran said it lightly. "We have a proverb in my country, sir. I think you would like it. *The slow one arrives last, and stays longest.*"

The hall did not notice. A few courtiers smiled politely. But Vaanathi, who was watching, saw Sancharan's hands go quite still against his arms, and the crow on his shoulder stiffen, every feather rising.

"It is a good proverb," said Sancharan.

"I thought so. I have always liked it."

"Does your country have many?"

"Hundreds. We are a people of tides. We have a great deal of time to make them up." The prince bowed, easily, and turned back to the throne. "Forgive me, Your Grace. I am talking too much. It is the sea. It is very hard to be quiet when one has been looking at nothing for three weeks."

The tension broke. The court laughed. Her father inclined his white head. Charudatta clapped, once, softly.

And Kanaka, who had been standing beside her brother with wide bright eyes, said in a clear carrying whisper, "That was *lovely,* Veeran. You did not say anything silly at all."

## III

The great dining hall had been made ready in three hours. The long rows of woven mats had been laid down the full length of the stone floor. Each mat held its own small low table, with a leaf and a bowl and a brass cup. The oil lamps stood in clusters on bronze stands. Jasmine hung in garlands from every pillar, and the whole room was so sweet it was nearly stifling.

She sat cross-legged on the mat beside her father's, with her hands folded in her lap. Veeran sat on her right. Kanaka sat beyond him, hugging her knees, devouring a plate of plantain with an air of pure delight. Her mother was across the way. The old treasurer sat at the far end with a look of alarmed gratitude, as if he could not believe he was allowed to eat in the same room as a prince.

Sancharan had been given a place low down, near the door. She could see him by turning her head. He sat with his hands on his knees. He was not eating.

"You are looking at him again," said Veeran.

"I am sorry?"

"The scholar. You have glanced at him four times since we sat down." He tore a piece of flat bread and offered it to her, with an air of absolute innocence. "I only mention it because I was hoping you might look at me five."

She laughed. She could not help it. It escaped her before she had decided, a short helpless sound, and she saw his eyes light.

"There," he said. "I have waited all day for that."

"For what?"

"For you to forget to be a princess." He turned his cup in his hands. "It lasts about three breaths. I think it is the best part of you."

"You are very forward."

"I am very frightened." His voice dropped, and the teasing went out of it. "You do not know what it is like. I have crossed the sea to ask a woman I have never met to marry me. I have had three weeks to think about it. I have decided that I will either be the best thing that ever happened to you or the greatest fool in the world. I would very much like it to be the first."

"Why me?"

"Because you listen."

"That is not a reason."

"It is the only one I have ever found good." He looked at her steadily. "I have spent my whole life among people who talk. My father talks. My councillors talk. My sister..." He glanced aside, and Kanaka, her mouth full, beamed at him. "My sister talks more than all of them. And not one of them hears. I have wished, all my life, to be heard."

"I am not sure I do that."

"You did it to a village of strangers, on a red riverbank. The headwoman told the harbour pilot, who told my steward, who told me." He leaned a little closer. "She said you gave her your name. Do you know how long it has been since a person in power gave anyone a name?"

She was silent. The lamps flickered. She was aware, very sharply, of the closeness of him, the warm salt scent of his skin, the scrape of his sleeve against hers. It was the first time in days that anyone had come near her without flinching away. She did not know whether she was grateful or ashamed.

Her eyes went, of their own accord, to the door.

Sancharan was looking at his hands.

"I would like to ask you something," said Veeran. "And you may refuse. It is about the dark of the moon."

Her heart gave a single hard knock.

"What about it?"

"It is the old custom in my country. The betrothal is made at a high place, under a clear sky, on the night when there is no moon. It is thought to be lucky. It means that the two who pledge do so in the dark, with nothing to see by but one another." He smiled. "I told your father it was an old custom. I confess it is rather new. My grandmother invented it. She was very romantic."

"At the sky-tower."

"I was told it was the highest point in your city. Is it not?"

She felt the blood leave her face.

"Who told you?" she said.

"Your minister. In his letter." He looked puzzled. "Is something wrong?"

"No."

"I am sorry. Have I offended you?"

"No. It is only that..." She stopped. She did not know what to say. In the heavy noise of the hall, with the lamps and the laughter, it was so hard to think. "It is very kind. I will think about it."

"That is all I ask."

He did not press. He turned and began to tell her, with an air of great confidence, about a time when his sister had fallen off the end of a pier. She laughed again. She did not hear one word of it.

## IV

The toasts began when the leaves were cleared. Bhargava rose and spoke, haltingly, of the princess's return. A general spoke of the harvest. Someone made a long, blurred speech about the sea. Charudatta, in his smooth voice, proposed the health of the guest from the coast.

Her father stood to answer. It was a labour. He put his hands on the low table and heaved. The whole hall went silent. He lifted his brass cup, and it shook.

"I thank Prince Veeran," he said. "I thank him for grain. I thank him for..." He paused. His voice was a thread. "For the courage it takes to cross a sea."

"To His Grace," said Veeran, rising.

"To His Grace," said the hall.

She lifted her own cup. It was filled with plain water. She had sent for it herself. She was about to drink when she saw the servant.

He was a thin young man in a plain brown wrap, one of the dozen who moved among the mats with water jugs. He had been working the far end of the hall. Now he was walking down the central aisle, quite slowly, with a jug in his left hand. His right hand hung at his side. It was a neat, slack hand. It held nothing at all.

He had stopped, ten paces from her. The lamplight fell across his face. It was hard and young and strangely peaceful. He met her eyes.

He smiled.

It was the smile of the man in the corridor. It was soft and fond and full of regret. And then his right hand moved, with a quick, flat snap of the wrist, and the lamp-flames flickered, and a streak of bright iron crossed the hall.

She saw it come. She saw it with perfect clarity, like a thing in a dream. It turned over once in the air, turned end for end, glittering. It was coming for her throat.

She did not even have time to scream.

A brown hand came across her vision, quick as a striking snake.

It closed on the blade.

It was not on the hilt. It was on the blade itself, the sharp part, the part that cuts. It caught the knife in mid-air, in the flat of his palm, with a sound like a clap. Veeran stood in front of her, one hand lifted, with the knife held tight in his bare fist. The iron blade lay across his skin. There was no blood. There was no mark.

The silence in the hall was total.

Then Kanaka shrieked. A man fell backwards over his table. Guards poured down the aisle. The thin young servant did not move. He stood where he was, with his empty hand still half-raised, and the same quiet smile on his lips.

"Take him," said Veeran. He had not raised his voice. He sounded merely a little tired. "Take him alive. I would like to know who sent him."

They took him. He did not resist. He went down on one knee with his hands open, in the manner of a man who has done what he came to do. As they dragged him from the hall, he turned his head, and his eyes went, not to the princess, but to the prince.

Something passed between them. It was a very small thing. It was a flicker, a lowering of the eyelids, a kind of acknowledgement. It was gone before anyone could have said they saw it.

Veeran opened his hand.

The knife dropped out of it and rang on the stone floor. His palm was perfectly smooth. He looked at it with an expression of mild surprise.

"Odd," he said. "I did not feel a thing."

## V

The hall exploded.

She did not hear most of it. She heard her mother's cry, and her father's hoarse voice, and the clamour of a hundred men all shouting at once. She felt Kanaka's arms round her waist, hot and shaking. She felt a hand under her elbow. It was the prince's. He had turned to her, with that same easy smile, though it was a little grey at the edges.

"Are you hurt?"

"No." Her voice came out in a croak. "No. You... your hand."

"It is quite all right. I have always been rather hard to cut. It is a family trait. My mother said we were made of leather." He laughed, a shaky laugh, and rubbed the back of his neck. "Forgive me. I am a little unsteady. I think I may need to sit down."

"Sit," said Kanaka, dragging at his arm. "Sit, sit, you fool, you could have been killed!"

"I did not think about it. It was a knife. It was coming at her. I only thought..." He looked at Vaanathi, and for a moment his face was entirely open. It was young, and frightened, and glad. "I could not let it."

He turned and sank to one knee before the emperor, there on the stone, among the spilled cups.

"Your Grace," he said. "Forgive me. This is not how I wished it to begin. But I have seen it now. I have seen that she is not safe." He bowed his head. "Allow me to guard her. I ask nothing. Only that I may stand between her and the dark until your own men are sure. I will do it for as long as you allow."

Anantaraja was staring at him. His thin hands trembled on the edge of the table. His white head was bowed.

"I..." he said.

"Say yes," said Charudatta softly, at his shoulder. "It is the only wise answer."

Manasa made a small sound.

"Yes," said the emperor, at last. It came out as a whisper. "Yes. You may."

The hall roared. They cheered. They had waited eleven days for something to cheer, and here it was, a prince with a white-edged grin and an unbleeding palm.

She sat in the middle of it, and the noise washed over her, and she found that she was shaking.

The terrace was cool. It ran along the north wall of the dining hall, with a low parapet and a view of the sleeping lower court. A moth beat against the single lamp. She had gone out for air, and nobody had stopped her.

Sancharan was waiting at the far end, against the stone. He did not look surprised.

"You were not close enough," she said.

"I was in the far corner. I could not have reached her in time. I would have had to stop the world." His voice was low and even. "I had my hand up. I was about to. Then he moved."

"He saved my life."

"He did."

"You do not sound pleased."

"I am very pleased that you are alive." He did not turn his head. "I would like to know why I am not pleased about the rest."

She stood beside him. Below them the lower court lay in moonless dark, with the pale squares of the paving stones.

"I liked him," she said. "I am sorry. I liked him very much. It is a terrible thing to say. He is so..." She stopped. "He listens."

"I watched."

"I know you did."

"I am glad that someone does." There was no bitterness in it. It was only a plain statement, like a stone set down. "I am not able to give you that. I would like you to have it."

"Do not be kind."

"I am trying not to be."

She turned. Her eyes were hot. "Did you see his hand?"

"I did."

"Tell me."

"A knife thrown at a throat, caught by the blade. A palm that is cut in a breath. I have watched men catch knives for a thousand years." He looked at her at last. His face was perfectly grave. "It is possible. It is rare. A trained man with a leather glove, or a tolerance for pain. But I saw it, Vaanathi. I was looking. He did not flinch."

"Because he is brave."

"Perhaps." He drew a breath. "But the blade met his palm edge-first. It should have laid it open to the bone. And the flesh did not so much as dent."

She said nothing.

"Courage does not make a blade blunt," said the Judge, very quietly. "And I have never in all my days known a man who thinks about whom to save, and does it, and has no fear in him at all. It is not natural. It is something else."

"What?"

"I do not know." His hands closed on the parapet. "That is what troubles me. I was not afraid of him, until I realised that I could not tell what he was."

Beyond the garden wall, the sea horn sounded once more from the harbour, long and low, like a creature turning over in its sleep.
