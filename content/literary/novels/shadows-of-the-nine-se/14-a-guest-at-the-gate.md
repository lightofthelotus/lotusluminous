## I

At that late hour, some distance north of the palace, Viyamban stood alone at the top of his family's old sky-tower, and felt the night go wrong around him.

He could not have named it more precisely than that, not at first. He had spent two nights this week exactly as he spent most nights, on the tower's crumbling upper platform, running his palm slow and patient along the worn stone parapet where the mark was cut. The cosmic wheel, broken and dark wedge. He was testing it the way a physician tests a pulse he has grown to know too well. The mark here had always been faint. Dormant, he had come to call it privately, in the pages he kept for no eyes but his own. Whatever the tower had once guarded, it had guarded it so long ago that the guarding itself had gone to sleep, leaving only the mark behind like a scar outlasting the wound.

It was not asleep tonight.

The stone beneath his palm gave a single low shudder. It was so faint he might have dismissed it as his own pulse, if he had not felt it a hundred times before in stone that had no pulse to give. He went very still, the way he always went still when something in the world finally moved. His restless eyes, which never settled on anything for long, fixed now on the dark line of rooftops to the south, toward the old archive wing, and did not move again for the better part of a minute.

"There," he said quietly, to no one, because he had long ago stopped minding that no one was ever there to hear him.

He had felt disturbances before, small ones, the give of a stone tested too roughly, the flinch of a ward brushed by careless feet. This was not that. This had the shape, faint but unmistakable to a man who had spent his entire lifetime learning to read exactly this language, of something old and guarded answering a threat with everything it had. Not tested. Struck. And struck hard enough that even a half-asleep mark two streets distant had felt the shiver of it through the ground itself.

This was the part of the old accord his own family roots had never quite managed to teach out of him. No matter how many years the palace spent pretending his line had nothing left worth knowing. The nine marks were not nine separate things, scattered and indifferent to one another. But probably, a stranger reading about them in some careless court record might assume so, in all fairness, by the way it gets described. They had been carved, all of them, under the same old promise, in the same old age. A promise cut in nine places at once does not fully forget that it was ever a single vow. A struck string on one side of a hall will set a slack string humming faintly on the other side of the hall, if both were tuned once from the same true note.

![Viyamban Tower](14-01-viyamaban-tower.jpg)

He had felt this exact humming twice before, and both times within the same handful of seasons. They happened close enough together that he had begun, privately, to wonder whether whatever slept beneath this city was waking rather than merely stirring. The first had come the night the whole court whispered of the princess's fall into some forgotten stepwell beyond the northern road. A fall that should have broken her and had not. But had left her, by every account that reached him afterward, strangely unmarked for the height she was said to have dropped. The second had come months later, sharper and closer than the first. The palace physicians where the ones who spoke of it, in careful lowered voices. A fever no one could name had nearly taken the princess in a single evening, only to release her again by morning as suddenly as it had come.

He stood very still now, remembering the that night properly for the first time, rather than merely recalling that it had happened.

He had not learned of that fever secondhand, the way he had learned of the stepwell fall. He had caused it. His own hand had sent the treated cloth message into her chambers. His own careful preparation, his own reasons he had never once let himself doubt were necessary. He had felt the tremor that same night. He had actually told himself, in the days after, that it was nothing. It must only be an old stone settling, a coincidence of timing too convenient to be anything but his own guilty imagination reaching for a meaning that was not there. He understood now, standing here with a deep thought, the tremor still not fading beneath his palm, that he had been wrong to dismiss it. Something had answered his poison that night, swiftly and with a violence to match his own. The princess had lived not by the failure of his preparation but by the success of whatever stood between her and him. He had sent death to her chambers and something had it turn around. Something, close enough to this very tower, that even a dormant mark forsaken by many generations and lifetimes, had felt the parry land.

He had spent the months since believing his attempt had simply failed. He understood now, with a cold and settling clarity, that it had not failed. It had been *deflected*.

Beyond those two, he had only ever had one older account to measure the feeling of such a blow against. And that one had not been felt at all, only inherited. His family had kept a record, generations deep, of the night an ancestor of his stood watch at this same tower and saw the mark itself change beneath his hand. A single yet deep crack opening across an old unbroken symbol where no crack had been before, as though something vast had been struck once, catastrophically. That symbol, was called out to have never afterward been whole again. The record did not say what had caused it. It said only that the crack never closed, and that the man who witnessed it never afterward spoke easily of that night to anyone, not even his own son. Viyamban had long suspected, though he had never been able to prove it from any surviving page. The crack mentioned in that old account and the wedge cut into every one of the cosmic wheel marks since were the same wound. Probably, remembered differently. He always suspected that the marks he had seen in the treasury bell, in smarans ledgers, and in this very tower of his ancestors were all siblings that felt a pinch from a far off sibling.

He remembered the night smaran died, how he made a wax seal to test this. The same wheel, the same cut out wedge and pressed it on the door that no one would notice. None of the later cracks appeared on that seal. He has been studying, and very patiently at that. The older symbols seem to have born out of a pact. But why does three of them stayed in the palace. The one he remembered in the river of red water, sevvaru, far away but reachable. He had been meaning to visit the step well of Amudhakulam as well. IF his guess was right, there must be one carved there too. All of these were marks born out same pact. Bonded, beyond human imagination. A blow to whatever was keeping the pact, seemed to create a rift in all the sibling marks alike.

He did not know, standing here tonight with his palm still pressed to the cooling stone, which of the many distant marks had been struck, or by whom, or why. But he knew, for the first time with any real certainty, that whatever guarded the princess was neither accident nor superstition, and that he had already met it once, in the dark, without ever seeing its face. He knew only that whichever one it was, it had been struck close. Close enough that his own tower, half asleep and two lifetimes forgotten, had still felt it arrive, like a hand laid flat against his chest.

He crossed to the small writing table he kept at the tower's inner wall, lit the single shielded lamp he permitted himself up here, and drew toward him the private manuscript bundle. This was a bundle that, no one in the palace, not even the servants who dusted his outer chambers, had ever been permitted to see the inside of. He found the page he wanted without needing to search for it. A single column, names and dates, each entry cross-referenced against a report of some small unexplained trouble. The princess's resurrection. A bench gone strange. A fountain running the wrong way. A scholar's convenient absence. The explanations of the absence which went suspiciously smooth. For a man whose whole life had trained him to distrust smooth explanations, all of this were worth recording.

He added tonight's month, moon date. Beneath it, after a moment's consideration, he added the hour, the lagnam based on the stars positions.

Then, in the same small, careful hand he used for everything that he did not wish read at a glance, he wrote the lines beneath the date. He sat looking at it for a long while before he rearranged the manuscript and returned it to the place he alone knew to look for it.

*The southern scholar returned eleven days late from an errand no one appears able to describe in any detail beyond its own tedium. Tonight, within the same watch, the wheel itself trembled. I do not yet believe in coincidence of this particular size. I intend to stop pretending that I do.*

He blew out the lamp and stood a long while at the parapet in the dark, arms crossed, watching the black shape of the archive roof as though it might, if he waited patiently enough, finally consent to explain itself.

He was still standing there when the first grey light found the eastern sky, and the palace below him began, slowly and then all at once, to stir with a different kind of news entirely. A rider had come in overnight from the coast road, dust-white to the knee, with word that the royal party of Samudrapuram had crossed the border three days early and would reach Anantapuram's gates by noon.

Viyamban watched the palace wake to the news from his high, cold vantage, and felt, for reasons he could not yet name, no relief at all in the distraction.

## II

By the fourth hour past dawn, the road into Anantapuram had become a river of colour.

Word had run ahead of the party. It always did, in a capital city. The streets nearest the western gate filled long before the first banner cleared the horizon. Children climbed onto their fathers' shoulders. Vendors abandoned their stalls to stand at the roadside instead, unwilling to miss whatever was coming. By the time the column itself came into view, half the city had already lined both sides of the road three lanes deep.

The soldiers came first. Two full ranks of them, mounted, moving at an easy trot that still somehow managed to look unhurried and immovable at once. Their horses wore Samudrapuram's sea-green on their brows, while the cavalry's sharp iron spears glittered like silver in the warm sun. The riders themselves rode with the loose, salt-weathered confidence of men who had spent as much of their lives on ships as on horseback. They did not look at the crowd. That, more than any show of arms, was what convinced the watching city these were soldiers worth being wary of. Men who performed for a crowd wanted to be admired. Men who did not look at all, wanted only to be believed.

Behind the soldiers came the first of the dancers.

They moved in a loose, weaving formation down the very centre of the road, ankle bells striking a rhythm too crisp to be anything but rehearsed, and every fifty paces or so the whole troupe folded into a brief, dazzling display before unfolding again to keep pace with the column. A woman spun through a sequence of turns so fast her silk skirts blurred into a single moving colour. Two men matched each other step for step in something closer to combat than dance, palms striking palms in patterns that built and built until the crowd's applause nearly drowned the drums beneath it. It was, Vaanathi would later say to Nila, watching from the palace's own upper terrace, a performance clearly built for exactly this purpose. Not to entertain a court that had already decided to be impressed. To win over a city that had not yet decided anything at all.

The musicians walked among them, Indian trumpet and flute players and a man who carried nothing but a pair of small hand cymbals and somehow made them sound like an entire second drum line, weaving the whole procession's noise into something that felt, even from a distance, less like a parade and more like a single vast piece of music moving slowly through the streets. The yazh players were silent for now. Their performance was better reserved for a close, luxurious room, for ears built just for their music. A loud street was never a place for a soft melody like the zither. That aside, their walking itself made a mark on the audience. They never played, nor did they move any part of the instrument, and yet the way they walked made it part of the performance.

Then came a demonstration that made the crowd's murmur drop, briefly, into something closer to silence. A dozen young soldiers broke from the column's edge and ran through a rapid weapons drill in the open road itself, bamboo staves and short blades flashing in careful, practised defence, close enough to draw real gasps whenever a strike appeared to land closer than intended. It ended as quickly as it began, the soldiers falling back into formation without so much as a raised voice to organise them, and the crowd's gasp turned, a heartbeat later, into genuine applause. It was, everyone watching understood without needing the thought spoken aloud, both a gift and a warning, offered in the very same breath. *We can delight you. We could also, if it ever came to it, hurt you rather badly. We would prefer, very much, that it never comes to the latter.*

The elephants followed, three of them, draped in cloth dyed the same sea-green as the banners, tusks ringed in worked silver that caught the sun in long bright flashes. And at the column's true heart, riding the largest and most richly dressed of the three, came the prince himself.

Veeran of Samudrapuram sat on his elephant the way other men sat on their favourite chair, easy, unbothered, one hand resting loose on the ceremonial rail before him rather than gripping it. He wore fine unbleached cotton edged in sea-green, a single strand of pearls at his throat, and he smiled out at the crowd with the particular warmth of a man genuinely enjoying himself rather than merely performing enjoyment for an audience he had been told to win. His body stayed sharply steady instead of the swaying one would expect from a man seated high on a royal elephant, that huge, moving in its own rhythm.

A little ahead of him, in a palanquin of dark polished wood curtained in pale silk, travelled his sister.

![Procession from Samudrapuram](14-02-samudrapuram-procession.jpg)

Princess Kanaka rode with the curtains drawn back rather than closed, in open defiance of the more modest custom some courts preferred, watching the city pass with an open, curious delight that matched her brother's own. Where Veeran's charm read as practised, hers read as entirely unguarded, a young woman genuinely delighted by a city she had clearly wanted to see for years and was only now finally being permitted to.

The whole procession drew up at last before the palace's outer gate, and there, to the crowd's mild disappointment, it stopped.

Protocol at Anantapuram's gate was old and would not be hurried, not even for a prince who had just charmed half the city in a single morning's ride. Veeran signalled with a small motion of his fingers, and a pair of his own delegation officials dismounted from their horses and approached the gate on his behalf, where a formal exchange took place with all the unhurried gravity such exchanges always carried. They carried a heavy gold ring, set with a single flawless sea-pearl, taken from their prince's own hand, and presented it to Anantapuram's gate officials for inspection. Beside it lay a rolled and sealed length of fine silk cloth bearing Samudrapuram's own royal mark, pressed in wax the deep sea-green of Samudrapuram's banners. The gate official standing on the high vantage tower received and examined both with the slow, careful attention of a man who understood perfectly well that his own head answered for any mistake, checked the ring's engraving against a record kept for exactly this purpose, and withdrew at last to carry both inside for the emperor's own household to confirm.

It would, everyone present understood, take some while.

Moments later, Kanaka's palanquin was seen lowering to the ground, letting her draw back its screen. Her golden beauty shone through her flattering attire. She stood before the royal elephant where Veeran still sat. No words were spoken, only her eyes asking him plainly what he thought he was still doing up there. Prince though he was, Veeran was also a loving brother, and in that moment could not refuse a sister's request. They rarely found the chance to simply be together, let alone share an unhurried meal, when each was so often occupied with their own royal duties.

The palanquin bearers set down their poles gratefully and settled into the shade of the rest areas provided along the gate's own high wall. The soldiers dismounted and stood easy beside their horses. Even their resting formation looked like a trained army setting up its camp. And Veeran, glancing sideways at his sister with the particular look of a man about to suggest something faintly improper, tilted his head toward the market street running along the palace's outer wall.

"We have," he said, "at least an hour before anyone official troubles to notice we are gone."

Kanaka did not need to be asked twice.

They walked the street together with only two guards trailing at a discreet distance, and the city, still warm from the spectacle it had just been given, did not let them pass unnoticed for long. No one crowded them. No one forgot themselves so far as to reach for a royal sleeve or block a royal path, whatever the pearl at the prince's throat or the fine curtains his sister had left open might have invited elsewhere. But word of who walked among the stalls moved faster than the two of them did, and merchant after merchant found some sudden, urgent reason to step out from behind their own counter and stand a little straighter, a little prouder, as the pair drew near. It was not the bowing of a frightened people. It was the bearing of a proud one, offered the rare chance to show a stranger exactly what their own hands were worth, and determined not to waste it.

A weaver held up a length of cloth still warm from the loom, not thrusting it forward for a sale but simply turning it, unasked, so the light would catch the weave properly, the particular pride of a craftsman who wanted the work seen correctly rather than merely seen. A potter set down the bowl she was shaping and lifted instead, with both hands, the finest piece already fired and cooling on her shelf, holding it out at arm's length for a moment's admiring inspection before returning, unhurried, to her wheel. An old bronze-worker, hands still dark with polish, called out no greeting at all, only struck two of his own lamps together in a bright, ringing note, grinning at the answering startle on Kanaka's face as if the small joke were payment enough in itself.

The street ran thick with colour and smell both, bolts of dyed silk and rolled cotton stacked bright as flower beds, the sharp green scent of fresh jasmine strung in long garlands for the evening's temples, bronze lamps polished so bright they threw small suns back at the sky. A sweet-seller pressed a still-warm piece of jaggery candy into Kanaka's hand before either of them had thought to reach for a single coin, waving off payment with the particular fierce hospitality of a woman who considered feeding a stranger a point of personal honour rather than a transaction. It was, Veeran thought, watching it unfold on every side of him at once, a far better answer to the morning's display of soldiers and staves than any second show of force could have offered. The street was not shouting *we are no less than you*. It was simply, quietly, setting its best work out in the light and letting the visitors draw that conclusion themselves.

"Father's letters called this a wealthy city," Kanaka said, licking sugar from her palm with far less royal restraint than any formal portrait of her would ever suggest. "I had assumed he meant the palace. He did not mean only the palace."

"No," Veeran agreed, watching a group of children chase each other between the stalls, shrieking with a joy that owed nothing to any treaty being negotiated a few streets away. "A kingdom's true wealth is never in the palace, Kanaka. It is in whether a street like this one can afford to give a stranger such hospitality for nothing. Anantaraja's empire is richer than his own ledgers will ever properly show a visiting minister."

Kanaka's eyes went, not for the first time that week, to the small folded cloth he kept tucked always at his belt, close enough to hand that she had long ago stopped believing its position there was accidental.

"You are going to look at it again," she said, not quite a question.

"I am not."

![Market Walk](14-03-market-walk.jpg)

"You are. You have looked at it eleven times since we crossed the border, brother. My palanquin's veils are shut, but they are undeniably transparent too. And you are already reaching for it now." She caught his wrist before his fingers had quite closed on the cloth, laughing outright at the guilty stillness that answered her. "Two years, Veeran. Two years you have kept that painting close enough to touch, and I have never once heard you admit to anyone why."

"It was a courtesy gift," Veeran said, with the particular stiffness of a man defending a position he no longer entirely believed himself. "Anantaraja sent portraits of the whole royal household, when our fathers first exchanged friendly correspondence. I kept it the way one keeps any diplomatic gift."

"You kept your father's portrait in a drawer within the week," Kanaka said, entirely unconvinced. "You have kept hers against your own belt for two years, and you have not managed to unsee it since, whatever careful word you have found for that in your own head. I only hope, for both our sakes, that the woman herself proves half as interesting as the painting has plainly convinced you she is."

Veeran did not answer that, though the faint colour that rose at the back of his neck answered rather more honestly than any words he might have chosen.

They were still laughing over it, unhurried, entirely at ease, when a runner found them among the stalls with word that the gate had confirmed both seal and ring, and that the household awaited them.

They returned to find the outer gate thrown fully open, and the palace itself, for the first time that morning, finally ready to answer the guests it had spent the whole of the last hour patiently, formally, confirming were exactly who they claimed to be.

## III

The palace had not been idle during the wait.

Anantapuram's own performers lined the processional avenue from the gate to the great hall's own steps, arranged in careful alternation the way Charudatta himself had personally overseen the evening before, host and guest each given room to be properly seen. Kuthuvilakku lamps stood in tall brass ranks along both sides of the avenue, lit even in full daylight, the old custom that no honoured guest should walk a single step of welcome without flame somewhere near their path.

A koothu troupe opened the palace's own answer to the morning's spectacle, dancers moving through a sequence drawn from the old stories, anklets striking stone in patterns as fast and precise as anything Samudrapuram's own dancers had shown that morning. Musicians followed, veena and flute woven beneath a reciting voice old enough that half the crowd mouthed the familiar verses along with him without quite noticing they were doing it.

![Welcome](14-04-welcome.jpg)

The royal drums, standing at least six feet high, were struck by the able arms of the drummers. Their arrangements moved like a strand of pearls floating in an ocean of onlookers, hosts and guests alike.

Anantaraja received the visiting party at the great hall's own threshold, the courtesy of meeting a guest at the door rather than waiting seated upon the throne, extended to very few visitors in living memory. He clasped Veeran's forearm in the old manner before either man had spoken a single formal word.

"Your father writes that you argued for a month to be sent in his place," Anantaraja said, "rather than wait for the treaty to be settled by lesser hands and older bones."

"My father writes the truth, though he leaves out that I argued considerably longer than a month, and that he agreed chiefly to be rid of the arguing." Veeran's Tamil carried the soft coastal lilt of the eastern shore, unhurried, faintly musical. "I have wanted to see Anantapuram since I was old enough to be told it was worth seeing, Your Grace. The city has not yet disappointed me. And I have only seen it from a saddle, and, this last hour, from a market stall selling rather excellent jaggery."

Anantaraja's brow lifted slightly at that, though the warmth in his eyes did not diminish. Kanaka, a half-step behind her brother, offered the emperor a bow considerably more graceful than her earlier sugar-fingered enthusiasm among the stalls might have suggested she owned.

Charudatta, standing at the emperor's right hand in his customary place, inclined his head with the particular warmth he reserved for guests whose favour he intended to cultivate early and thoroughly. "The princess will be glad to hear Samudrapuram thinks so well of us," he said smoothly. "She has spent no small effort on the city's granaries and embankments this past year. I am certain she would value a fellow enthusiast's opinion of the results."

"Then I shall have to earn an introduction," Veeran said, with a small, easy bow toward the assembled court, "and hope the princess finds a coastal prince's opinion worth the granting."

Vaanathi, watching from her customary place a careful step below the throne, filed the remark away without deciding yet what to make of it, the way she filed most things that arrived wearing too easy a smile.

## IV

The treaty discussions began the following morning, in the smaller council chamber where the light fell kinder on old eyes and long tables.

Veeran had brought his own delegation. Three senior officials and a young cartographer who spoke rarely but listened, Vaanathi noticed, to absolutely everything. The treaty itself lay across the table in bound sheaves of prepared palm leaf, Samudrapuram's own hand distinct from Anantha's. Rounder. More hurried. Shaped by a scribal tradition grown up beside salt water rather than paddy fields.

"Anantha's strength has always been the inland harvest," Anantaraja said, tracing a line across the treaty's opening clauses. "Samba in the deep soils near the river. Kuruvai where the water runs faster and the season is short. Thaladi on the lands too slow to drain before the second monsoon. We have never wanted for grain, only for a place to send the surplus before it spoiled in the kalanjiyam, the granary."

"And we have never wanted for a place to send it," Veeran said. "Samudrapuram's soil sits too near the salt to grow much beyond coconut, and a stubborn variety of red rice no one outside the coast has much patience for. What we have instead is the sea itself. Pearl beds three generations my family has kept jealous watch over. Salt pans that produce more than our own kitchens could use in a hundred years. Teak, cut from groves inland of the delta, seasoned the slow way our shipwrights insist no other coast has properly learned." He tapped the treaty's second clause. "Your surplus grain feeds my coast through the lean months. My salt and my timber answer needs your inland provinces have always traded three kingdoms deep to satisfy. It is not a generous treaty, Your Grace. It is simply an obvious one. I find that is usually the better kind."

"You will want to see the eastern embankments while you are here, then," Anantaraja said, genuine interest warming his usually measured voice. "My engineers have spent the better part of a decade extending the great tank system beyond the river's old floodplain. An eri, a lake, wide enough to hold three seasons' rainfall against a single dry year, fed by channels cut through country that swallowed men and oxen both before the sluices were properly set."

"I have heard of it as far as Samudrapuram's own court," Veeran said. "My own engineers manage something different, out of necessity. Tidal water does not wait politely for a single monsoon the way your rivers do. We have learned to build kalingu that open and close with the tide itself, twice daily, without a single hand needing to touch them. It took my grandfather's engineers eleven failed attempts before the twelfth held against a spring tide." Something genuinely bright moved behind the practised charm for the first time that morning. "I would very much like to see your tank system before I leave, Your Grace. And I would be glad to walk your engineers through what little my own coast has learned about water that refuses to sit still."

Charudatta, seated further down the table, made a small approving note in the margin of his own copy. The particular satisfied mark of a minister who had begun, within a single morning, to see a very useful and very popular alliance taking shape exactly as he had hoped.

## V

The guest house sketch was unveiled on the third day, in the open-sided pavilion overlooking the palace's western garden, where the light was best for judging such things.

Veeran's cartographer, the same quiet young man who had listened so carefully at the treaty table, unrolled the drawing across a low stand and stepped back to let it be properly seen. Fine charcoal, across a length of prepared cloth, the way such careful work was always kept. It showed, in patient and exacting detail, the guest house Samudrapuram proposed to build on land gifted along the southern wall. A permanent residence for its ambassadors and travelling merchants both.

![Plan](14-05-plan.jpg)

"We asked your own master builders to correct us wherever we had got it wrong," Veeran said, standing beside the sketch with none of his usual easy performance, watching Anantaraja's face instead of his own handiwork. "I did not want a piece of Samudrapuram set down carelessly inside Anantapuram's walls, Your Grace. I wanted a building that belonged to both of us properly, or not at all."

The design honoured the old forms with a care that surprised even Charudatta into silence. A central mutram open to the sky, in the manner every well-built house in Anantha kept, so rain and morning light could reach the household equally. A wide thinnai running the length of the front wall, raised a full step above the street, exactly the kind of shaded seat where an old man might sit through the worst of the afternoon heat trading gossip with his neighbours. The pillars were drawn in local granite rather than any imported stone, carved in the plain, deep-cut floral pattern the region's temples favoured. The roofline swept low and tiled in the traditional manner, angled steep enough to throw the monsoon's worst straight off the eaves rather than let it pool and rot the beams beneath.

"Here," Veeran said, tracing a line along the sketch's eastern corner, "is the one piece we could not quite bring ourselves to leave out. A narrow channel, cut along the courtyard's edge, to carry the rain off toward a cistern rather than let it run to waste in the street. My own house at Samudrapuram keeps six such cisterns, enough to see the whole household through a dry season without troubling the public wells. I am told your own dry seasons run longer here than ours. I thought it a courtesy worth the extra stonework."

"You have clearly studied us rather more than a guest usually troubles to," Anantaraja said, examining the sketch with the unhurried attention he gave very few things outside his own council chamber.

"I did not wish to be merely a guest, Your Grace," Veeran said. "I would rather Samudrapuram's house stood here the way a good neighbour's house stands. Built with an understanding of the street it sits on. Not simply set down and left to explain itself."

Several of the court's older ministers, men not easily impressed by visiting princes and their practised charm, were seen afterward examining the sketch's finer details with a great deal more interest than protocol strictly required.

## VI

The great dinner was held on the fourth evening, in the palace's largest hall, and it was, by every account offered afterward, among the finest the court had seen in years.

The mats ran the length of the hall in three long rows, lit by more oil lamps than the stewards could easily account for. The air carried the mingled scent of jasmine strung along the pillars and the rich, slow-cooked fare the royal kitchens had spent two full days preparing. Anantaraja presided from the head table with Veeran seated at his right hand, the place of highest honour, and Charudatta stationed himself close enough to both to miss nothing of either man's conversation.

![Music](14-05-dinner-music.jpg)

The entertainment began once the first courses had been cleared. A fresh dance troupe took the cleared centre of the hall first, the performers' anklets striking the stone floor in patterns so quick and precise the sound itself became a kind of second music beneath the yazh player's strings. The hall fell properly silent for it, the particular silence a room gives only to performers good enough to have earned it.

![Dance](14-06-dinner-dance.jpg)

After the dance came the parai drummers, four of them, building a rhythm that started low and grew until the whole hall seemed to breathe in time with it. Then, to a rising murmur of approval, a pair of silambam performers took the floor, spinning long bamboo staves in patterns fast enough to blur, striking and parrying in a choreographed exchange that drew genuine gasps whenever a strike appeared, for one heartbeat, to land closer than intended.

![Silambam](14-05-dinner-silmbam.jpg)

"Your court keeps its old arts better than mine does, I confess," Veeran said to Anantaraja, watching the staves flash in the lamplight. "We have grown rather too fond of the sea and its songs, at Samudrapuram, and let a few of the older land arts slip. I am taking notes for my father, Your Grace, whether he asked for them or not."

Vaanathi, seated some distance down the table among the senior court women, watched the performance with the same composed attention she gave most things at court. Her eyes were drawn, more than once, to the easy warmth with which the visiting prince spoke to everyone near him, servant and minister alike, as though the difference in their standing had simply never occurred to him as worth remarking on. It was, she thought, a very good trick. If it was a trick at all. She had not yet decided which.

## VII

The attack came without warning, in the brief lull between the silambam performance and the sweet course, when half the hall had risen to stretch and the other half had not yet noticed the first half moving.

A man in the plain dark clothes of a kitchen servant crossed toward the head table with a covered tray held before him, unremarkable among a dozen other servants doing precisely the same. No one marked him as anything other than what he appeared to be until the tray was already falling and the knife beneath it was already in his hand.

The strike came fast, aimed low and sure at Veeran's side as he turned, still laughing at something Anantaraja had said a moment before. What followed happened too quickly for most of the hall to properly witness, but those close enough to the head table would tell it afterward in nearly identical terms, and each telling grew the prince a little more impossibly capable. Veeran did not call for the guards first. He moved. He caught the attacker's wrist in one hand before the blade had covered half the distance meant for his ribs, twisted with a speed that belonged to a man far more practised with violence than any travelling prince ought to be, and put the man on the floor of the great hall with his own knife pressed flat and harmless beneath a bronzed forearm before the palace guards, shouting now and closing from three directions at once, had covered half the room.

The hall erupted. Women were pulled back from the tables. Ministers rose shouting for the doors to be sealed. Anantaraja himself was on his feet with a speed that belied his years, one hand already at the ceremonial dagger he wore more from habit than expectation of ever needing it.

In the chaos, the attacker's own hand found something at his collar. A small vial, swallowed almost too quickly to see. By the time the guards reached him and hauled him upright, his eyes had already begun to glaze with whatever poison he had carried against exactly this outcome. He was dead before a single question could be put to him. Dead, the palace physician confirmed grimly, by a preparation none of Anantapuram's own healers immediately recognised.

Veeran rose from the floor himself, breathing hard but entirely composed, and accepted Anantaraja's hand up with a small, wry smile that did nothing at all to disguise how close the blade had truly come.

"I apologise for the mess, Your Grace," he said, glancing down at the ruined tray and the spreading stain of some fine sauce across the stone floor. "I understand it is considered poor manners to disrupt the sweet course."

It was, everyone in the hall agreed afterward, a remarkably composed thing to say with a dead man cooling three paces away. It did the visiting prince's reputation no harm at all.


## VIII

By the following morning, the story had run the length of Anantapuram twice over, gathering colour with every telling. Charudatta, moving through the corridors with the particular satisfied stride of a minister whose instincts had just been handsomely rewarded, found he did not need to manufacture a single word of the praise he now offered so freely.

"A prince who negotiates his own treaty clauses, sketches his own guest house with his own hand, and disarms his own assassin without so much as calling for a guard first," he remarked to Anantaraja, over the morning's first correspondence. "I have served three reigns, Your Grace, and I do not recall a foreign visitor who made quite so thorough a case for himself in so short a stay."

"He is, by any fair measure, an extraordinary young man," Anantaraja allowed, though something careful moved behind his eyes that his minister did not trouble to notice. "I confess I had expected charm. I had not expected quite so much substance behind it."

The court, for its part, had already reached its own verdict, and reached it with the particular speed courts always manage when a comparison presents itself too neatly to resist. A prince as handsome as any painted verse. As quick-witted at a treaty table as any seasoned minister. And now, since the dinner no one had stopped discussing, as demonstrably brave as any hero the old stories troubled to keep. It was whispered in the kitchens before the week was out. Repeated, more carefully, among the senior ministers. It settled, by the time a full ten days had passed, into something close to common wisdom throughout the palace.

Anantapuram had never before hosted a visitor so plainly, so completely, a match for its own princess. In beauty. In intelligence. And now, in bravery besides.

Vaanathi heard the comparison secondhand, from Nila, who delivered it with the particular careful blankness she used for gossip she suspected her friend would not welcome.

"They are already composing verses about it," Nila said. "The prince who caught a blade meant for his own heart with his bare hand. I am told one of the kitchen girls has started a wager on how long before your father proposes something rather more permanent than a treaty."

Vaanathi said nothing to that for a long moment, turning the whole strange week over in her mind. The easy smile. The careful sketch. The rehearsed grace of a man who had disarmed an assassin as smoothly as he disarmed a room. She did not yet have words for the small, persistent unease sitting beneath her own grudging admiration, and she did not try to force them.

"Let them compose their verses," she said finally. "I find I would rather watch the prince a while longer before I decide what kind of poem he actually deserves."
