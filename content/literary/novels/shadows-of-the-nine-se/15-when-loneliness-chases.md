## I

![Sancharan's Gallantry](15-01-sancharans-gallantry.jpg)

They did not stop being kind to each other. That was the maddening part, Vaanathi thought. No one warned you that a quarrel of this kind would leave that standing. Even after everything else in it had fallen, and even now, with the whole court still murmuring another man's name over its morning rice. Sancharan still rose when she entered a room. He still found the one cushion that did not leave a draught at her back, without being asked, and set it where she would sit before she had chosen where to sit. When the evenings turned close and the lamps drew moths in from the wet gardens, he still moved to stand between her and the worst of the flutter. He did it unhurried, unremarked, as though his own comfort had never once entered the accounting.

It would have been so much simpler, she thought, if he had simply stopped caring for her. She would have known what to do with that. She had a lifetime of practice on treating indifference diplomatically. What she had no practice at all in was this. A man who tended her with both hands and told her nothing with his mouth. A man who could not stop being gentle and would not start being honest, as though gentleness were the coin he had decided to pay in place of the truth he owed.

She had given him the opening. Twice now. *I found something,* she had told him, the very night he returned. She said it again three evenings later, more directly, standing over the same shared ledger with her voice pitched low enough for only the two of them. *I have not stopped finding things, Sancharan. I have not stopped being able to be told them either, should you ever wish to test that.*

He had looked at her both times with an expression she had come to recognise over the season, and to dislike in equal measure. It was the look of a man doing careful calculation behind his own eyes and not liking the result of that calculation. Both times he had said something courteous, complete and entirely beside the point. He let the opening close on its own, the way a door closes when no one troubles to hold it.

He had not asked her what she found. Not once. Not what it was, not where, not whether it frightened her, not whether she wished to be rid of the burden of carrying it alone. A man who wished to protect her from his own burden might at least have asked after hers. He had not. The not-asking had begun to sit in her chest in a way the eleven days of silence never quite had. The eleven days had been an absence. This was a presence that simply refused to reach the last small distance it would have cost him nothing to cross.

And now there was a second silence sitting beside the first, one she had not asked for and did not know what to do with. The whole palace was still finding new ways to say the visiting prince's name. She had heard it in the kitchens, at the well, twice from her own mother in a single supper. A prince as handsome, as clever, as brave as any old story troubled to keep. She did not mind the comparison for its own sake. She minded that Sancharan had heard it too, she was certain of that much, and had said nothing about it at all, not one word, as though the subject belonged to some manuscript he had already quietly closed.

"You are cataloguing his kindnesses again," Nila observed, on the sixth evening of it. She was watching her count something on her own fingers that was plainly not household accounts. "I can see you doing it. It is not a flattering habit."

"I am not cataloguing anything."

"You have counted the cushion, the moths, and I believe you have just now arrived at the dry ginger kashayam he sends up when the rain keeps you from the garden." Nila did not look up from her own work. "You are keeping a ledger of a man who will not keep one of himself. That is not affection, Vaanathi. That is the particular exhaustion of caring for someone who withholds the very thing that would let you stop keeping accounts at all."

Vaanathi said nothing. There was nothing to say that would not have confirmed every word of it.

***

He did not sleep well those nights either. No one but Iruli was awake to witness it, and Iruli, that particular evening, did not keep her own counsel for very long.

*You have heard, then,* she said, from the window ledge, in the flat, testing voice she used when she already knew the answer and wanted to see how long he would pretend otherwise.

"I have heard."

![Sancharan Iruli](15-02-sancharan-iruli.jpg)

*The whole kitchen has heard. I imagine the flower market has heard by now, and is composing its own verse.* She turned one bright eye on him. *A prince who fought off his own assassin with his bare hands. Who sketched a guest house with his own charcoal and got every beam of it right. Who charmed a street full of merchants before he had even reached the gate. I have counted at least four separate servants who have already decided, on no authority whatsoever, that your princess has finally been offered a match worth the name.*

"I am aware of what is being said, Iruli."

*Are you. You have the particular stillness of a man who has heard a thing and not yet decided what to do with the hearing of it. I know that stillness. I have watched you wear it before.* Something in her voice softened, unwillingly. *You are not angry.*

"No."

*You are not even jealous, which I confess surprises me rather more than it should.* She studied him a moment longer. *What are you, then?*

He did not answer at once. When he did, the words came slowly, the way words come when a man is testing each one for weight before he permits it out of his mouth. "I am trying to decide whether I have any right to stand in the way of something that would be good for her."

Iruli went very still.

"He is mortal," Sancharan said, to the dark rather than to her. "He can give her a life that does not require a court to be lied to on his behalf. A companion who does not carry, in his own hands, the very thing that could destroy her and everyone or everything she loves." He looked down at the rings, dull and quiet at his fingers, the way he had taken to looking at them lately, as though they were a lock he kept forgetting to stop checking if it was still locked.

His rings, his own pieces that could never leave him nor let him go astray in this human form. They stood as ordinary iron finger rings now covering most of his fingers. To an onlooker they might seem a new fashion, but to him they were more than that. A part of him, a part of what he always was, without which he might dissolve into this illusion of being a human itself.

"I have spent this whole strange season telling myself the danger was in the withholding. That if I only found the courage to be honest, I would have done right by her. I am no longer certain that is true, Iruli. Perhaps the greater danger was never the silence. Perhaps it was permitting myself to want her to know me and all of me at all."

*And so your answer is to simply hand her to the first charming stranger who arrives with an elephant and a well-drawn sketch.*

"My answer," he said, "is to consider, seriously, for the first time, that her happiness and my nearness to her may not be the same thing. That they may, in fact, be opposites."

*That is a very noble sentence,* Iruli said, *for a man who has not slept properly in six days and cannot stop touching his own rings when he says her name.*

*You said something like this to me once before,* she went on, quieter now, the testing gone out of her voice entirely. *Do you remember. You told me a wall built to spare someone grief is still, in the end, a wall. You were speaking of your silence then, and I told you so plainly enough that I did not expect to have to say it twice.* She held his eyes, unblinking. *I do not see how a different silence, dressed up this time as sacrifice instead of protection, escapes the very same accusation now. You have simply found a nobler reason to build the identical wall.*

He had no answer for that either. She had not truly expected one. She left him, eventually, to the lamp he had not lit. To her, it did not feel noble, prodding at a wound he had only just admitted to having, and she left him to it anyway, because there are some things a person must be permitted to finish deciding alone.

To Sancharan, it felt like the coldest room he had stood in since a hillside three centuries gone. Yet he made himself sit in it, turning the thought over and over the way a tongue returns, helplessly, to a broken tooth, because he had decided he owed Vaanathi the honesty of at least disclosing it to her properly, before he ever dared to hope otherwise.

## II

The break came and when it came, it came over nothing at all. Well, but this is always how such things generally come.

They were in the archive, an ordinary evening session that had once been the whole of their acquaintance. They had since become something neither of them named. They were working through a set of border correspondence Charudatta had asked to be reviewed before the next council. The rain had come and gone twice that afternoon, leaving the archive's high windows streaked and the lamp oil burning with the faint green smell wet air always gave it. Vaanathi read a passage aloud, some tedious dispute over a watershed boundary two generations settled. She heard her own voice fray at the edges without entirely understanding why, until she had already stopped mid-sentence.

"You are not listening," she said.

"I am listening."

"You are not. I have said the same line of that passage twice, Sancharan, and you have not once corrected the repetition, which for you is practically an accusation of forgery." She set the leaf down harder than she meant to. "Where are you. Or should I ask when are you."

He did not answer at once. The not-bothering, tonight, after six days of not-bothering, undid something in her that six days of patience had been holding shut by main force.

"You are always somewhere else lately," she said. The old careful evenness left her voice entirely. "You sit across from me and you are kind, you are endlessly, maddeningly kind, and you are never once here. I have watched you do this for six days now. I have given you two openings, wide enough for you to walk a bullock cart through, and you have stepped neatly around both of them. You manoeuvred as though I had only been discussing the weather. Do you have any notion what that does to me, Scholar? To sit across from someone who tends every small comfort and withholds the one thing that would actually comfort me?" The word scholar did something else entirely to both of them. It widened the distance between them, when the distance was already wide enough on its own.

"Vaanathi."

"I am not finished." She was on her feet now, though she had no memory of rising. "You asked me once, in a garden, to trust that your silence was a kind of care. I believed you. I believed you enough to lie to my own mother for it. And I find, six days into this particular silence, that I no longer know whether I am being cared for or simply being managed. The way one manages a child too young to be trusted with a grief."

"That is not what this is."

"Then tell me what it is." Her voice had dropped, and the dropping made it worse, not better. "You do not have to give me the whole of it tonight. You have never once had to give me the whole of anything. I have made my peace with that. In fact I have made a fool of myself with that so far. But right now, ask me. Ask me what I found. That is all I am asking of you. One question, freely given, and I will meet you exactly as far as you are able to walk."

He rose too, then, and crossed the small distance between the two reading tables. She watched him arrive at some decision behind his own eyes. She watched his hand lift, uncertain, toward her own where it lay closed and trembling faintly at her side, the small private tremble of a temper held one breath past where it wished to be held.

"Vaanathi, I am trying to keep you from something I am not certain I can undo once it is given—"

"Do not," she said, low, "tell me what I can bear."

And she reached for his hand herself. Not to strike it away. Only to close the last small distance he had spent six days refusing to close on his own. Her fingers found his where the rings sat cool and quiet against his skin.

![Rings Attack](15-03-rings-attack.jpg)

The light that answered was not cool at all.

It came from nowhere she could afterward describe as a direction. Blue at its heart, and something closer to white at its furious edge. It arrived more than it struck her, the way a wave arrives rather than strikes, all at once. She felt it less as pain but more as an absence, a half-breath moment in which her own hand simply stopped reporting to her. She felt as if her hand was not there at all, as if it had been violently but very quickly cut off. She heard herself cry out, distantly, as though the sound belonged to someone standing some way off. Then she was on the floor of the archive, her palm burning with a cold, deep ache. The ache had nothing in common with any burn she had ever taken from a lamp or a kitchen fire. Sancharan was on his knees before her, both his hands waiting to hold her but daring not to touch her, his face gone the colour of old ash.

"Vaanathi." His voice had cracked entirely open, all the careful arrangement gone out of it at once. "Vaanathi, look at me, let me see it, please, let me—"

"I am well." She was not certain, saying it, whether it was true. Her hand throbbed, but the throbbing was already dulling. When she made herself look at her own palm in the lamplight she found the skin unbroken, only reddened faintly, as though she had held it too long too close to a flame that had, at the last moment, thought better of what it meant to do. "Sancharan. I am well. What was that."

He did not answer. He had gone very still, kneeling there, and she watched something break open behind his eyes. Something she had never once seen him allow the light to reach before. Not in the stepwell, not in the garden, not even in the hollow silence of the night he came home eleven days late and stood in the lamplight and let her walk away from him.

"That," he said, and his voice was barely a voice at all, "was the rings. Guarding me. From you."

## III

He did not move for a long moment. Then, slowly, as though every joint in him had aged a hundred years in the space between one breath and the next, he sat back on the archive floor across from her. He put his own trembling hands flat against his knees, and began, at last, to speak.

"I told you once that I carry an anchor, the way every one of the Nine carries one. That much was true. What I did not fully tell you was that mine no longer sits where my anchor is meant to sit. I did not tell you because for three centuries I have not fully understood it myself. Not until very recently. Not until a night in Anaadi, when I was made to remember all of it at once, whole."

Vaanathi said nothing. She cradled her tender hand against her chest and made herself very still, very quiet, the way time and the world seem to stand still for anyone who has waited beyond patience to hear something long awaited.

"Her name was Amudha," he said. "She lived a very long time ago, in a village not far from here, and she loved me before I had the wisdom to stop her, and I let myself be loved before I had the wisdom to understand what that would cost her." He was not looking at Vaanathi now. He was looking at some middle distance she could not follow him into, the particular unfocused gaze of a man reciting a wound rather than a memory. "She learned what I was. Not from me directly, the way you found out. I have spent three centuries failing to forgive myself for that carelessness anyway. She learned it, and she was glad of the learning, glad in the whole. But she was unguarded, the way only a mortal heart seems able to manage. In her own nature, for which I cannot blame her entirely, she told someone. Someone whom she thought she trusted, not knowing what she was truly telling him."

"Andhakan... was his name," Sancharan continued after a pause that seemed to pain him even more.

"Andhakan," Vaanathi said quietly. The name surfaced from some deep half-remembered corner of her own reading, an old enemy's name from an older text. She watched him flinch at hearing the name, especially in her voice.

"He used her unguarded nature to find his way to me, and through me, to the one thing every mortal enemy of the Nine has always truly wanted. It is not the death of any single one of us. It is the anchor itself. The thing that binds each of us to this world, seated in the world's own ground, at a place given long ago into the keeping of those sworn to guard it." He drew a slow breath.

"He nearly took mine. He discovered something important, something that can unmake an anchor. I say discover because from whatever little I have lived, there is nothing that can be made by a man to destroy a thing such as an anchor. I still do not know, and have not yet mustered enough courage even after three centuries, to go behind what and how or from whom he discovered it. All I remember, and allowed myself to remember, is that it was an unmaking. A fire that glowed so brightly I wanted my own anchor to simply go away. The blow was sudden. The attack was sudden." He paused again, showing the physical pain of what was tearing apart inside him. Vaanathi, for a moment, felt guilty for tearing open his old wound. He continued anyway, in a further lowered and painful voice.

"The attack should have severed my anchor cleanly from Bhumi. Severed cleanly, an anchor lost is a wound the whole world eventually bleeds from, not merely me. It did not sever cleanly. My rings would not permit it. They took the whole of that unmaking fire into themselves rather than let it finish. In the failing, in the desperate half-failure of that closing, the anchor did not return to its old place in the world's ground. It fused. Into the rings. Into me. I am not guarding my anchor the way the others do. My rings now hold the anchor fused within them. I have not even attempted to study this, out of my own cowardice. I am afraid of what shall sear open if I do. As a result, I have always been guarded by my rings. They are me and I am them, in a sense, if that makes any sense to you. But they have fused with the anchor now too. So I am guarded by my anchor, and I guard the anchor. Our pact is mutual now, which is very different from the way the rest of the Nine are bound, Vaanathi. I have become one, in some manner I still do not fully have the language to explain, even to myself."

"And Amudha." She regretted the moment her lips uttered the name, even before her mind could have told her better of it.

His jaw tightened, and for a moment she thought he would not be able to say it at all. "She ran towards the unmaking fire. Not away. But right into it. She understood, in that last terrible instant, what the rings were and what they guarded. She saw an end nearing, for my rings, for what they were protecting, for what I was protecting. She understood that we had nothing left to give, and she gave what she had instead. What was left of her, after, was not a body a physician could have named the wound of."

His voice had gone very flat, the particular flatness of a grief too deep to weep and too large to heal. "She died buying my rings their strength. The fire was meant to unmake an anchor, and whatever guarded it. She was neither. The beauty of the universe is such that when an interference happens that it was never meant to account for, the universe pauses, the way a scholar pauses when he reads what he cannot yet understand. That pause gives just enough room to clarify, or to confuse everything further." He kept pausing, as if he had to gather fresh strength for every few lines.

"The unmaking fire was still deciding what to do with a mortal who had thrown herself into a process that was never meant to touch her at all. In that pause, my rings finished fusing with my anchor, not entirely by their own choice either. They were trying to save me, the one guarding the anchor, and then there was Amudha, arriving from nowhere. Neither of us had any notion of what needed to be done. It has taken me months, years, centuries, unfolding every crumpled fold of that night one after another, and I have still not managed to read it all."

He continued in a voice Vaanathi had never once heard from him before, full of hopelessness and despair. "In the end, Amudha died loving something she should never have been permitted to love at all, and I let her, and I have spent every year since paying for the letting."

Vaanathi became a victim of her own mouth once again, before her mind could steer her toward a more rational kindness. "Did you just let her love you? Or did you love her too?" Even for someone as plain-spoken as her, her own voice landed like a thorned whip across her own heart, and she could not begin to imagine what it had landed on for him. Her eyes glistened with his pain.

"Life and love on this earth, they are meant for you, and for the likes of you, Vaanathi. I am merely a guardian here, passing by. Unfortunately, my passing by takes millions of centuries, so I might look, to your eyes, like a stationed guard rather than a passing one. What I am here is my human manifestation, made possible because of my anchor. I am a being just like this very Bhumi, and thus, in turn, like you. We all come from the same cosmic dust, so I cannot stop caring. But I must stop others caring for me, at all costs, for their own good. Which is what I failed Amudha with." His voice finally broke.

Vaanathi found she could not speak for a long moment. When she did, her voice came out gentler than she had believed herself capable of, given everything the last quarter hour had cost her hand and her nerves both. "The rings guard you," she said slowly, working it through even as she said it. "Against anyone who gets close enough to matter. That is what happened tonight. Not because I meant you harm. Because I meant you closeness, and to whatever is left of them, from that hillside, closeness and harm have never fully separated themselves again. But I also feel you still have more that I do not know yet. So the rings can live with that for now."

"Your feeling is right. But I myself am yet to find the rest of the answers. Almost everything I have known or figured out along the way so far, I have told you tonight. I did not know I could tell all of it so soon," he said quietly, and continued after a pause.

"But the reaction of the rings tonight, the one that almost took your hand, I did not know it would happen at all. Not to you. Not after so many months of your nearness passing without incident. I have told myself for weeks that whatever wariness lived in my rings had settled around you specifically. That you were somehow known to them now, safe in a way even Amudha was never permitted to be. I was wrong." He kept going.

"Or the debt Anaadi has been collecting from me has made them less certain of anyone at all, even you. And I did not think to warn you, because I did not believe there was, any longer, anything left in me for them to guard so violently." He looked at her hand, still cradled against her chest, and something in his face went past grief into something closer to horror.

"Until tonight I was not even aware they still held the unmaking fire inside them. They have never manifested like this before. Because of that, I very nearly let the same fire that killed Amudha reach you, across the width of an archive table, over a border dispute two generations settled. I have spent three centuries certain I had learned my lesson. Tonight I learned I have hardly been careful at all. Being honest and being careful are not, I am beginning to understand, the same discipline. And sometimes they are entirely opposite ones."

## IV

The silence that followed held differently than any silence that had passed between them before it. Vaanathi, sitting on the cold floor of the archive with her hand slowly forgetting its own fright, found she no longer had any wish to fill it with an accusation.

Suddenly the pining, the longing, or whatever it was she had been feeling for the being in front of her, seemed to change into something larger. She had not yet felt it fully enough to name it. That, she decided, could wait.

"I have something to give you as well," she said instead, quietly. "I have been carrying it since the night you were gone, and I find I no longer wish to carry it alone either."

She told him of the threshold. The wedge, the hollow, the smell of neem oil that had nearly undone her before she had read a single leaf. She told him of the eight sites and the ninth entry with no place at all, the mark drawn once in careful ink, the three lines that had sent her, in the dark of a dead man's chamber, straight to a question she had not dared voice aloud until this very hour. She watched his face as she spoke of it. She watched him receive each piece the way a man receives news he had already half known and had simply never been permitted to hear said in another voice.

"Eight anchors cast shadows in the record," she finished. "Smaran wrote that the ninth has none. I understand now why. It is not because the ninth was never found, or never sited. It is because the ninth is not a place at all any longer. It is you."

"Yes," he said again, and this time the word carried the whole weight of the confession rather than merely confirming a piece of it.

"Then that is what the debt has been," she said slowly, thinking it through even as she spoke it. The old familiar pleasure of a puzzle's pieces settling returned to her despite everything, despite the ache still faint in her palm. "The bench. The fountain. The stolen days. It is not Anaadi punishing you for some old crime. It is the anchor itself, unsettled, restless in a body that was never built to hold it, spending small disturbances the way clouds weep before they finally, properly burst."

"I believe so. I do not yet know what it means that the debt is growing larger rather than settling, as I had always assumed such things eventually settled. I do not know what it will cost, the next time it decides to collect." He looked at her then, fully, the careful arrangement he had worn like a second set of robes for six days finally, entirely, gone from his face. "I am telling you this because I owe you the truth of what nearly happened to you tonight. And because Iruli has told me, more than once and more plainly than I deserved, that a wall built to spare someone grief is still, in the end, a wall. I am not telling you this because I believe myself safe to be close to. I would ask you to understand that distinction, Vaanathi, even as I break the very silence I built to protect it."

![Sancharan Vaanathi](15-04-sancharan-vaanathi.jpg)

"I understand it," she said. "I do not agree with it. But I understand it." She reached, slowly this time, deliberately, giving him every chance to stop her that he had not given himself the chance to take earlier. She closed her uninjured hand around his. The rings did not stir. They sat quiet, dull, exhausted, the way a guard dog sits quiet once it has finally, exhaustedly, recognised a face it ought to have known all along. "They are learning me," she said softly. "Perhaps that is worth something. Perhaps it is the only thing, tonight, that is worth anything at all."

"Perhaps," he said. For the first time in six days, in eleven days before that, in a season longer than either of them had yet found the courage to name properly, he did not look away from her while he said it.

## V

They sat like that a long while, the lamps burning low, the rain beginning again softly against the high windows. Two people who had each spent a season keeping a private ledger of the other's silences had, in the space of one frightened evening, finally opened both books to the light.

Neither of them said the larger thing. It sat between them still, unspoken. It was no longer the old question, whether what moved in his chest when he looked at her deserved a name at all. He had answered that, however imperfectly, not an hour past, on the very floor where she still sat. What remained unspoken now was crueller than that question, and neither of them was yet ready to ask it aloud. Whether a loneliness chosen out of love could honestly be called protection, or whether it was only the old silence wearing a nobler coat. And whether Vaanathi, now that she had been given the whole shape of what he feared, intended to accept the distance he had just resolved to keep between them, or meant to chase him through it anyway. Vaanathi did not press any of it. Not tonight. She had learned, this night if nothing else, that some doors opened wider for patience than they ever did for force.

But she kept his hand a while longer than good sense recommended, and he let her, and neither of them, for once, troubled to pretend it was an ordinary courtesy.

## VI

Far across the city, at the top of a crumbling old tower the palace had long since stopped remembering it once paid for, Viyamban was not asleep either.

He had taken to sitting up later than sense recommended, these past weeks, palm resting against the parapet's worn stone out of habit more than expectation, the mark beneath it as dormant as it had been for months. He had almost stopped waiting for anything to change. Almost.

The stone woke under his hand without warning, a single low shudder that ran through the old wheel and its wedge the way a struck bell runs through the hand that struck it. He went still, the way he always went still, and did not move again for the better part of a minute, letting the shape of it settle into memory beside the two he already carried.

*There,* he thought, and did not trouble, this time, to say it aloud. He no longer needed the sound of his own voice to make a thing real. Three tremors now, in less than a year, after decades in which the mark had done nothing but sleep. The stepwell at Amudhakulam. The fever that was never truly a fever. And now this, sharper than either, close enough that he could almost have named the direction it came from if the night had allowed it.

He did not yet know what had happened, or to whom, or why. He knew only that the pattern he had spent a lifetime waiting to be proven right about had just added its third point, and a pattern with three points was no longer a suspicion. It was a shape.

He sat a long while with his palm still against the cooling stone, and did not reach for his ledger. Some entries, he had learned, were better made in daylight, once the hand had stopped wanting to shake.

## VII

Some distance beyond the city's last lit lamp, where the road gave up its stones and became simple beaten earth, a hut stood alone in a fold of the land that the tax-collectors had long ago stopped bothering to walk to.

The man who approached it that night moved with the ease of a mountain breeze curving around a peak and falling forcefully onto the flatlands below. He moved without sound and without any wasted motion, a bow unstrung and slung across his back and a short blade at his hip worn smooth from a discipline that had nothing to do with ceremony. He was of the south by his build and his walk both, trained in the old way, the way that taught a man to read the ground with his feet before he ever trusted it with his weight. He paused once outside the hut, listening to the night the way a man listens who has learned that silence has its own particular textures, and finding nothing in it that troubled him, went in.

A single low lamp burned inside, its light kept small and shielded, throwing more shadow than flame. The man who waited beside it wore a plain dark cloth wound close and a mask that covered everything from the bridge of the nose downward, so that only a pair of eyes remained to be read, calm, patient, and entirely unhurried.

"You are later than I expected," the masked man said.

"The roads were watched more closely than usual, after the dinner." The spy inclined his head, not quite a bow. "The whole city is still talking of it."

"So I am told." Something in the masked man's voice carried the faintest edge, there and gone before it could be named. "Tell me what went wrong."

"Nothing went wrong, master. The prince disarmed the man exactly as we rehearsed it. The court believes what it was meant to believe." The spy hesitated, choosing his next words with the particular care of a man who has learned that bad news delivered carelessly is punished twice. "But it was closer than it should have been. The vial was meant to be swallowed the instant the guards laid hands on him, not a full breath after. Had one of them been quicker, or luckier, we would have had a man alive to be questioned, and a man alive to be questioned is a thread that can be pulled."

"And instead?"

"Instead we have a prince the whole court now believes disarmed a real assassin with his bare hands. His name is already being set beside the princess's own, in the same breath, by kitchen girls placing wagers on a wedding." The spy could not quite keep something that was almost admiration out of his voice. "Whatever the cost of the closeness, master, I do not think it could have gone better. Veeran fought like a man who has killed before and did not flinch from doing it again. The emperor himself helped him to his feet. I have never seen a court fall so completely for one evening's work."

The masked man was silent long enough that the spy began, despite himself, to wonder if he had said too much.

"Closeness is not success," the masked man said at last, quiet and even. "A thread pulled too soon unravels a great deal more than one evening's careful work. You will tell whoever manages the next of these that precision matters a great deal more to me than spectacle. I did not ask for a story the kitchens would sing. I asked for trust, built cleanly, with nothing left behind for a patient man to find. See that the next is handled properly."

"Yes, master."

The masked man rose, unhurried, and crossed to the hut's single unshuttered gap, where the night showed itself in a narrow strip of stars. He was quiet a while, looking out at them the way a man looks at something he has been patient with for a very long time and does not intend to remain patient with much longer.

"You know only the shape of what we are building," he said, not quite to the spy anymore, "and that is by design, and it will remain so a while yet. But I will tell you this much, since you have served well enough tonight to have earned it. There are nine old debts owed to this world, nine places where something was bound down and told to stay. Bhumi has worn those nine bindings like a crown it never asked for, and called it balance, and called it peace, and it has never once asked whether the ones doing the binding had any right to ask it of us." His voice did not rise. It did not need to. "One of the nine has already gone missing from the record entirely. No site. No guardian anyone can name. A wound in the pattern that no one but I have thought to go looking for. When I find where it has hidden itself, I will begin with that one, because a debt no one can locate is a debt no one will think to defend." He turned back from the window, and the small lamp caught his eyes for a moment, dark and entirely without doubt. "And when that one is gone, we will not stop there. My clan has waited a very long time for a patient enough hour. We intend to spend the whole of it. Every last binding, until Bhumi answers to no one's hand but our own."

The spy said nothing. There was, he had learned over several such nights, nothing useful to say to that particular tone of voice.

"Go," the masked man said, gentler now, the ordinary courtesy returning to it as easily as it had left. "Rest. There will be more roads to watch before this is finished."

The spy bowed properly this time, and let himself out into the dark, and did not look back at the hut, or at the narrow strip of stars, or at the small steady lamp still burning behind the mask of a man the whole of Anantapuram had, only nights before, thanked with open hands and open hearts for saving nothing more than his own carefully staged life.
