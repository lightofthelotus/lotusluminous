## I

He came at dusk, as he had promised. He knocked once on the open door and waited.

Vaanathi had lit two lamps and set them at either end of the low table. She had thought about where he would sit. In the end she had put a mat on the floor near the door, a good three paces from her bed, and sat on the bed herself with her feet drawn up. It looked, she thought, like the arrangement of two people at a very formal negotiation.

"Come in."

He came in. Iruli stayed on the lintel. He sat on the mat with his hands on his knees and looked at the lamp flame. He kept his eyes elsewhere.

In the next room, behind a half-drawn curtain, she could hear a small domestic noise. It was Nila, moving about, humming a few notes under her breath. It was an ordinary sound, and Vaanathi was oddly grateful.

"I have never told it all in order," said Sancharan. "I have told pieces. To myself, mostly. I may get it wrong."

"Tell it the way it comes."

"It does not come in order." He turned his hands palm up on his knees and looked at them. "It comes as a smell. A voice. The feel of a particular hot afternoon."

"Then start with that."

He was silent for a time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the palace a gong was struck, softly, twice.

"Her name was Amudha," he said.

## II

It was two hundred and fourteen years ago, in the market of a farming town called Maruthur.

He had gone there for no reason. That was the first thing he told her. It was his habit, once in a century, to walk among mortals for a season and see what had changed. He did it on foot. He did it alone, because Iruli had grown tired of the way children threw things at her. He wore a wanderer's cloth and carried nothing. He kept to the edges of the roads.

Maruthur lay on a wide green plain, ringed with paddy. Its market was held in a square of beaten earth, under thatched awnings, on every fourth day. There were heaps of red chillies and mounds of tamarind. There were women selling cloth and men selling oil and a boy with a basket of live crabs. The noise was tremendous. It rose like heat.

He had walked through the middle of it before he noticed the effect.

It was a small thing. The tamarind seller's heap had begun to droop. A cluster of marigolds, fresh that morning, hung their heads as he went by. A dog that had been sleeping in the dust got up, looked at him once, and went away stiffly, with its tail down. Mothers drew their children against their legs. A man turned from his bargaining and stared, and forgot what he had meant to say.

*I have been careless,* he thought. *I had not noticed how long it was since I walked among crowds.*

He was turning to leave when someone laughed.

It was a woman's laugh, loud and unembarrassed, and it went on and on. It came from a low table at the edge of the square, where a stout young woman sat behind a tray of sweets. She had put one hand flat on the table to steady herself. Her shoulders were shaking. She was laughing, not at a joke, but at him.

"I am sorry," she said, when she could speak. "I am so sorry, sir. I cannot help it."

"At what?"

"The way you walk." She wiped her eye. "You walk as though you were carrying a house on your back. And the house is full of bad news. And you are trying very hard not to let anyone see."

He stood in the dust of the market square and stared at her.

He had been called many things. A storm. A reckoning. The slow one. A shadow that comes at the end. No one had ever told him that he walked badly.

She was perhaps twenty-three. She was not tall and she was not thin. She had a round face, brown-gold in the sun, with a wide mouth and a dimple on one side. Her hair was long, dark and wavy, and it hung loose down her back, with a small white flower pinned behind one ear. She wore a mustard-gold band across her chest, tied with a twist at the front, and a deep red wrap about her waist. There were green glass bangles on her wrists. Her feet were bare. A pair of small gold hoops swung at her ears. She looked at him with bright, shameless, amused eyes, with one hand on her hip.

"Have you eaten?" she said.

"I do not eat."

"Everyone eats. It is only that some people pretend." She picked a sweet from the tray. It was a golden ball, sticky with palm sugar and sesame. "Here. It will do you good. You look as though no one has given you anything for a hundred years."

He took it. Their fingers did not touch. He was very careful of that. He had been careful for a thousand years.

"Thank you," said the Judge, to a woman who sold sweets in a market.

"Now," said Amudha, "tell me what is in the house."

## III

He did not tell her that day. He did not tell her the next. He went away on the evening of the first, meaning never to return, and came back on the morning of the fourth because he had found he could not remember what the sweet had tasted of.

She had been waiting. She said so. She said it with her hands on her hips.

"Four days," said Amudha. "I was beginning to think the house had fallen on you."

"I was busy."

"Doing what?"

"Looking at the river."

"Every day?"

"Rivers are slow."

"So are you." She laughed. It was the same laugh, loud and warm. It went through the market like a bell. "Sit. Eat. You look thinner."

He sat. It became a habit. For ten days he sat on a low stool at the end of her table and watched her sell sweets. He watched her bargain with a miser, and cheat a cheat, and give a handful to a hungry child without letting its mother see. He learned that she had been orphaned at six and raised by an aunt who beat her. He learned that she could not abide a closed door. He learned that when she was angry she went quiet, and when she was happy she sang off key.

He learned that she was not afraid of him.

It took him a long time to understand why. At first he thought it was ignorance. Then he thought it was courage. By the second month he thought it was something else, a simple unexamined certainty that a person who sat quietly at the end of a table and listened could not be dangerous.

Iruli arrived on the eleventh day.

She came down out of the sky like a thrown stone, and landed on Amudha's tray, and took three sweets in her beak. Amudha screamed. She snatched up a ladle. The crow flew to a rafter, ruffled out her feathers, and stared down with an expression of lofty disdain.

"That is a thief!" said Amudha.

"She is mine."

"Then you have bad taste in birds."

"I have been told."

"Does she bite?"

"Only when provoked. And on market days."

Amudha looked at the crow. The crow looked at Amudha. A long, careful silence fell. And then, with great deliberation, the bird dipped her head. It was a small bow, grudging and sidelong. It was the first one Iruli had made to any living thing in four hundred years.

"Well," said Amudha. "She has manners, at least." She took a sweet from the tray and held it up on the flat of her palm. "Come on, then. But you will ask next time."

The crow came down, and took it, and did not say a word.

## IV

On the fortieth day, at the river steps, he told her.

It was evening. The market had emptied. The plain lay quiet under a sky the colour of ripe mango, and the river ran slowly past, brown and wide. She sat on the lowest step with her feet in the water. He sat three steps above her, with his hands folded.

"I have something to say," he said, "and I would like you not to speak until I have finished."

"I always speak."

"I know. I would like you to try."

She shut her mouth. She looked at him sideways, with bright, curious eyes.

He told her. He told it badly. He told her what he was, and what the work was, and what happened to a living thing when he laid his hand on it. He told her about the dogs and the marigolds. He told her that he had come to the market out of a sort of tired curiosity, and that he had meant to go away. He said he was sorry. He said it three times.

She heard him out. When he had finished, she sat for a long time with her face turned to the river. A fish jumped. The ripples spread slowly, and met the bank, and were gone.

"So you are the one who comes at the end," she said.

"Yes."

"Do you come for everyone?"

"No. I only count."

"That is a terrible thing to do."

"It is."

"Alone."

"Yes."

"For how long?"

"Since before there were counting words."

She was quiet again. Then she turned her head and looked at him. Her face had lost its laughter. It was strange to see. It looked older, and kinder, and more tired.

"I think I understand," she said. "I do not think you want me to. But I think I do."

"What do you understand?"

"That you are very lonely."

It was not a question. It was said very plainly, as one says that rain is wet. He felt it go into him like a cold coin dropped in a well. It fell and fell and he heard no sound at the bottom.

"I am the Judge," he said. "I am not permitted to be anything else."

"Who says?"

"No one. It is the nature of the work."

"Then it is a very bad nature." She turned back to the river. "I think someone should say it is permitted."

"Amudha."

"I will say it. I give you leave." She laughed, a short quiet laugh, not her usual one. "I have nothing else to give. I am a seller of sweets."

"You do not understand what you are saying."

"I understand perfectly. I will grow old, and you will not. I will go grey, and you will watch. I will die, and you will keep the count." She did not look at him. "I have been told since I was six years old that I was not worth waiting for. You are the first person to turn up on the fourth day and apologise."

"That is no reason."

"It is the only one anyone has needed." She drew her feet out of the water and rose. Her wrap was wet to the knee. She looked down at him, and her bright eyes were steady. "You may not touch me. I know. You have said. I will not ask it. I will only sit here, and you will sit there, and I will talk too much, and you will pretend you do not mind."

"I do not mind."

"I know," said Amudha. "That is how I can tell you do."

## V

The grey came in the third month.

He saw it first. It was a single strand, bright as a thread of silver, threaded through her long black hair at the temple. She was bent over the tray, laughing at something, and the light caught it, and his heart stopped.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing."

"You went white as rice flour."

"It is nothing."

"It is a hair, is it not?" She put up a hand and found it. She held it out in front of her eyes, squinting. "A grey one. Well, look at that."

"I am sorry."

"For what?"

"You are twenty-three."

"I am twenty-three with a little wisdom. It suits me." She dropped the strand and wiped her fingers on her wrap. "Do not be so solemn. A grey hair is only a hair. It does not hurt."

"It is the beginning."

"Everything is the beginning of something." She planted her hands on her hips. "I know what it is. I am not stupid. I have been waking up with my hands a little more tired each morning for two weeks. It is the cost. You told me the cost."

"I told you it might be slow."

"It is slow. It is as slow as the river. I could be eighty before the end of the year, or I could be eighty at the end of fifty." She gave him a long, level look. "I would like to be the one to choose."

He had meant to go. That was the thing he told Vaanathi afterwards, in a flat even voice that cost him a great deal. He had packed nothing. He had gone out of the market one morning at first light and walked west, towards the river. He had walked all that day and all that night. And on the second morning he had stopped on the road, quite suddenly, with his hand against a tree.

The tree was dead. It had withered in the night, in the short time he had leaned against it. He took his hand away and looked at it.

"I cannot do this," he said, to the empty road.

A voice behind him said, "You are walking like a man with a house on his back again."

She had followed him on foot. She had walked all night, barefoot, in the dark, with her sandals in her hand. Her hair was loose and her feet were bleeding. There was a second grey thread at the other temple. She was grinning. She was shaking with tiredness, and she was grinning.

"Do not," she said, before he could speak. "Do not say it. I know. You are going to explain that this is a mistake. You are going to say that it is for my own good. You are going to be very noble." She dropped her sandals in the dust. "I would like to say, before you start, that I have decided."

"Amudha..."

"I have *decided.*" She crossed the road in four quick steps. She stopped, one pace from him, and stood with her arms folded and her chin up. "I am not brave. I am only stubborn. You have spent a thousand years doing what is right, and what has it got you? A crow and a house of bad news." She sniffed. "I would like to see what happens if somebody just stays."

He looked at her. For a very long time he could not speak.

And then something rose in his chest, quite unexpected, and burst out of him before he could stop it. It was not a word. It was a laugh. It was rough, startled and cracked from long disuse. It rang down the empty road like a dropped pot.

Iruli, perched in the dead tree, fell off her branch.

Amudha stared. And then she was laughing too, with her head thrown back and her hand pressed to her side, in the middle of a dusty road at dawn, with her feet bleeding and her hair going grey, and the whole wide green plain turning gold in the first of the sun.

## VI

The lamp had burned low. Vaanathi had not noticed.

She sat on the bed with her feet tucked under her and her arms wrapped round them. Her face was wet. She had not decided to cry. It had simply happened, somewhere around the road and the dead tree, quietly, in the way that water finds the lowest place.

Sancharan sat on the mat with his hands resting on his knees. He had not looked at her once. He was looking at the flame.

"I had not laughed since," he said. "That is the count. Two hundred and fourteen years."

"Since her."

"Since the last time."

"And it was on that road."

"It was on that road." A faint trace of the old dry humour touched his voice. "I will tell you the oddest thing. For almost a year afterwards, I could not stop. It would come on me at the most absurd times. I laughed at a pot that fell in the market. I laughed at the crow being sick on a priest. People began to avoid me. I did not mind."

"What changed?"

"She did." He drew a breath. "I am telling you this part first, so that you will know how it was. I am not telling it to be kind to myself. I want you to see what it was, before I tell you how it ended. It was good. It was the best thing that had ever happened in my life. And it was a mistake."

"It was not."

"It was the greatest mistake I have made. I was told so. By the nine. I was told what would come of it. I did not listen." He closed his eyes. "I would not undo it. That is the shame of it. If I were given the chance, I would walk down that road again."

Vaanathi said nothing. In the next room the humming had stopped. There was a stillness behind the curtain, a very deep and careful one, as of someone who has stopped in the middle of a movement and is pretending she has not.

"Tell me the rest," said Vaanathi.

"It is not a short story."

"I have the night."

"You will not like what comes next."

"I did not like the first part either." She wiped her face on the heel of her hand. "I would still like to know."

He opened his eyes. He looked at her at last. Something in his face had come loose, and it was terrible, and very gentle.

"On the night the wheels broke," said the Judge, "I was not at the council. I was sitting on a doorstep in Maruthur, with a woman's head in my lap, telling her a story about a crow who learned to bow. She had a fever. It was only a small one. And then the sky went wrong."
