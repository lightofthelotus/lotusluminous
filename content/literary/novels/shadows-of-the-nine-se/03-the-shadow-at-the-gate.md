## I

The petitioner had been talking for almost an hour. Vaanathi had stopped listening long ago. She lost interest around the part where he compared his flooded fields to a man buried alive.

She sat where she always sat. Her seat was to the left of the throne, one careful step below it. She sat close enough that the court could never forget her birth. She sat far enough that no one could mistake her for holding real power. Sunlight fell through the high windows in long gold bars. It lit up the dust that never quite settled in this old, well-used hall. Her father listened to the petitioner with the same stillness he brought to everything. That stillness made ministers lower their voices without being asked. When the man finally fell silent, Anantaraja tilted his head, just slightly.

"Send an official to the eastern embankments before the month turns," he said. "And tell the district revenue office I will want to know why this was reported to me before it was reported to them."

It was, Vaanathi thought, a perfectly good answer. But it was not the answer she had been about to give. She had wanted to say something different, to release grain from the crown's emergency reserves straight to the affected families. That way they would not have to wait on an official's slow schedule. She opened her mouth to say so.

"Your Grace need not trouble the princess with matters of embankments," said Minister Charudatta smoothly. He spoke before she had made more than a breath of sound. The court's attention slid past her, like water sliding around a small stone.

Vaanathi closed her mouth. She had learned, over many years, exactly how much a closed mouth could say. She let this one say a great deal.

Her father did not look at her. He rarely did, during court. She understood why, even though he had never explained it. To look at her would invite the whole hall to look at her too. It would make people wonder if the throne's daughter thought herself fit to advise the throne. Anantaraja protected her in a simple, maddening way. He pretended, in public, that she needed no protecting at all. She had decided years ago that she preferred his blindness to Charudatta's sharp attention. She had not yet decided if that preference made her wise, or simply tired.

The next petitioner was announced. He did not come.

Instead, Ratnan came, the under-steward of the royal archive. He moved through the side doors faster than any under-steward was allowed to move in the emperor's hall. His face had gone the colour of old rice. He did not wait for the herald to announce him. He did not even remember to bow until he was three steps past the point where bowing would have mattered.

"Your Grace." His voice cracked on the second word. "Your Grace, the keeper, Smaran, he has not opened the archive since yesterday's bell. When we broke the door,"

He stopped. The hall went very quiet, the way air goes quiet just before thunder. Everyone's skin seemed to sense that something had changed, before their minds caught up.

"He is dead," Ratnan said. "In the reading chamber. Alone."

***

Later, Vaanathi could remember that quarter hour two different ways. One version she could recite without feeling anything at all: the court physician sent for, the great doors closed, the low murmur that her father let run just long enough before he raised a hand for silence. The other version she felt in her body for days afterward, a cold at the back of her neck she could not explain.

She had known Smaran her whole life. When she was nine, he had shown her how the old ledgers were wrapped in cloth soaked in neem oil, to keep the silverfish away. He had let her sit on the archive floor for whole afternoons, reading old dynastic records the way other children read adventure stories. Now he was dead. And the court's first breath, before grief, before even shock, was something closer to a single word.

She heard it move through the hall in pieces too quiet to trace to one mouth. *Shadow.* *Ill-timed.* Someone, she thought it was old Bhargava the treasurer, whose fear of the sky had only grown worse with age, said clearly enough for her to hear: "The season turns wrong this year." He did not need to finish the thought. Three other ministers nodded as if he had.

Vaanathi did not believe in seasons turning wrong. She had said so more than once, to her mother, who smiled the smile she used for opinions she found charming but unconvincing. She had said it to Nila too, who agreed so quickly that Vaanathi sometimes suspected her friend just wanted to end the conversation. But she watched the unease ripple through grown men, men who commanded armies, who taxed whole provinces, who an hour ago had only been arguing about embankments. And she understood something, not for the first time, but freshly, the way an old scar aches again when the weather turns: belief was never really the point. Fear does not need belief. Fear only needs a story old enough that no one remembers who first told it.

"A quiet inquiry," her father said, once the hall had settled enough to hear him. "The keeper served this house faithfully for thirty years. He will be honoured as such. There will be no talk of omens in my court." He paused, just long enough for the words to reach every corner of the hall. "Is that understood."

It was not a question. The hall understood that. The murmuring did not stop entirely, Vaanathi doubted anything short of an actual eclipse could have stopped it, but it dropped low enough that no one could be punished for it. That, she supposed, was the most any emperor could ask of a rumour.

An hour later, she was given leave to see the chamber herself. The official reason was thin: the keeper's records touched on her own study of the border tributaries, and someone competent should confirm nothing important had been disturbed. She suspected her father had arranged this excuse himself, in his usual quiet, sideways way, a door left unlocked, rather than a door held open, so no one could accuse him of choosing to open it.

The reading chamber smelled of neem oil and old palm-leaf. Underneath that smell was something colder, which she made herself not think about too closely. He laid there as if he were sleeping but his body said otherwise. An oil lamp burned down to a hard black wick. Bound leaves of preserved palm, stacked with the same obsessive tidiness that had been Smaran's whole personality, turned into a filing system.

![Vaanathi visits Smaran's room](01_02_SmaranDeath.jpg)

And the door.

Two guards still stood beside it, talking in low voices about how strange it all was. The bolt had been thrown from the inside, the old iron bar dropped into its cradle, the way it was dropped every night when the keeper wanted no interruption. Yet somehow, in a room with one door and no window wider than a man's forearm, whatever had taken Smaran had left no mark of ever forcing its way to him. Vaanathi listened without seeming to listen. It was a skill Charudatta had taught her, without meaning to, through years of practice being ignored.

She found nothing so dramatic as a clue that hour. What she found was smaller and stranger, and it lodged itself somewhere behind her ribs and stayed there the rest of the day. On the shelf nearest the keeper's writing table sat the great genealogical ledger of the royal line. She knew that shelf as well as she knew her own room. She had spent a hundred idle afternoons tracing the ink of ancestors eight generations gone. Now there was a gap.

Not a missing book. A missing *page*, torn close enough to the spine that only someone who had run a finger down that exact binding a hundred times would notice. The binding sat wrong, a hair too thin, where the tear had been smoothed flat and the book reshelved as if nothing were amiss.

Someone had taken something. And whoever it was had wanted very badly for no one to notice.

Vaanathi said nothing about it to the guards. She was not yet sure why.

## II

"You have the face," said Nila, from the low couch by the window, "of someone about to tell me something they have already half-decided not to tell me."

Vaanathi's chambers looked over the western gardens, where the last light of day turned the tamarind leaves the colour of old brass. She had changed out of her court silks into something looser and was pacing in it. She knew this made her look exactly as unsettled as she felt. She had decided she did not have the discipline to care.

"A page is missing from the royal ledger," she said. "The genealogical volume. Torn out, and the binding smoothed over to hide it. On the same night the only man in this palace who would have noticed a stray thread on that shelf turned up dead behind a door bolted from the inside."

Nila sat up. "You told your father this?"

"I told my father nothing. My father has already decided this is grief, and coincidence, and an old man's tired heart. The moment I mention a missing page, Charudatta will find some clever reason it doesn't matter. And I will have wasted my one useful observation of the week for nothing." Vaanathi stopped pacing and dropped into the chair opposite Nila, less gracefully than a princess of the Anantha line was supposed to arrange herself. "For once, I would like to know something before I am required to prove I am allowed to know it."

"That," Nila said, "sounds like a fine ambition for a woman with no actual authority to investigate anything."

"I have authority over my own feet. I intend to use them."

Nila had been at her side since they were children. She was the daughter of a minor court astronomer, and she had been folded so completely into the palace's inner rooms that Vaanathi sometimes forgot there had been a time before her, the way one forgets there was ever a time before knowing how to walk. Nila had a gift for saying the unhelpful true thing at exactly the moment Vaanathi least wanted to hear it. Vaanathi had long ago decided that was the whole purpose of a good friend.

"You understand," Nila said, more gently now, "that half this court would say the missing page, the death, and the season are all the same story. That there's a name for what they'd say did this."

Vaanathi made a small, unimpressed sound in her throat. "Don't."

"I'm not saying I believe it."

"You're saying it like you half do."

"I'm saying," Nila said, with the patience of long practice, "that you grew up hearing that name the way the rest of us grew up hearing about the tiger that took the herdsman's boy near the north road. It's a story your grandmother's generation still flinches at, even the ones who've never seen a tiger in their lives. You can decide you don't believe it, and still feel your skin go cold when someone says it in a dim room. That isn't superstition, Vaanathi. That's just what it means to be raised by people who were afraid before you were even born."

Vaanathi didn't answer right away, because it was, infuriatingly, true. She had been six years old the first time she heard a servant blame a bad harvest on the shadow that walks when the wheel of fortune turns cruel. She had watched an old woman spit three times over her shoulder at the mention of a single word. She had heard that word invoked over spoiled milk, broken betrothals, a lame horse, a king's untimely death two dynasties past. *Ganda, a misfortune arriving at the worst possible hour.* It worked less like a name and more like a held breath, the sound a room makes right before something in it goes wrong. She did not believe some wandering planet-lord walked the earth taking archivists in the night. And yet some small, old, unreasoning part of her still flinched at the sound of it, the way a hand flinches from a flame it has never touched, simply because enough other hands had already told it what burning feels like.

"There's something else," Nila added, watching her face with the careful look of someone about to hand over unwelcome news. "A scholar is expected at court within the week. Sent from the southern collegium, or so the stewards say, come to study the archive's older records, to cross-check something with the border treaties. Apparently the request went out a month ago."

Vaanathi went very still. "A month ago."

"Before Smaran died. I know what you're thinking, and I'd think it too. But a month is a very long time to plan a murder around a man you had no way of knowing would still be alive to murder."

"Or it's a very long time to plan something that only needed a dead archivist and a missing page to finish," Vaanathi said. She did not enjoy how steady her own voice sounded saying it. "When does he arrive?"

"Tonight, I'm told. Perhaps he's here already." Nila's mouth curved, not unkindly. "You have that look again."

"What look."

"The one where you've already decided to dislike someone you haven't met yet."

"I haven't decided anything," Vaanathi said. It was, strictly speaking, a lie, and she suspected Nila knew that exactly as well as she did.

## III

He had been standing at the edge of the eastern courtyard for nearly a full watch before anyone noticed him. This did not surprise him nearly as much as it perhaps should have. People rarely look at stillness along a wall when there is movement to watch instead. Grooms were leading tired horses to the stables. Two junior clerks argued amiably over a ledger that would not balance. The long gold light slid down toward the rooftops, the way it did at this hour in every court he had ever stood in, on every stretch of a world that kept turning, whether or not the people on it noticed.

He had chosen the name Sancharan for this errand the way he chose most things, without much attachment, only the practical sense that it would let him move unremarked through rooms that were, on the whole, easier to move through unremarked. It suited him well enough. He had been called worse things in more languages than this court's scribes even had words for.

The crow on his shoulder had opinions, as she generally did, about the wisdom of standing still in plain sight for a full watch.

*You could at least pretend to be tired,* she said, in the private register only he could hear, half caw, half something closer to a woman's dry amusement filtered through a bird's throat. *Travel-worn scholars do not usually stand like fence posts, admiring the architecture.*

He did not honour this with more than a passing thought, which she correctly took as agreement to consider it, and she settled more comfortably against his collar. Iruli had flown ahead of him for three days, across country he had already walked once before. That had been centuries ago, under a different name. In that season, this same palace had been half its present size, and the tamarind trees along the western wall had not yet been planted. He did not mention this to her. She would only have found some way to call it nostalgia, and he was not, strictly, nostalgic. He simply noticed the things that stayed, and the things that didn't, the way anyone does, when they have had a very long time to watch.

By the time he crossed into the courtyard, he had already heard that the archivist was dead. Servants talk in doorways the way water finds the lowest ground; it took no real effort to catch the current. He heard, too, the word that followed the death through the halls like a second, quieter death of its own, *shadow*, *ill season*, *ganda*, the old reflexive flinch a palace makes whenever misfortune arrives without an obvious author. He was, through long practice, unmoved by it. It had happened before. It would happen again, in some other court, under some other roof, for as long as people needed a name for the shape of what frightened them, and had the good sense not to use their own names instead.

Still, if he were honest with the part of himself that kept its own counsel even from Iruli, hearing it again brought a small, specific kind of tiredness.

He was still turning that thought over, lightly, without much weight, the way a man turns over a stone he has turned over a thousand times and does not expect to find anything new beneath it, when the crow left his shoulder without warning. She wheeled once through the low light with a cry sharp enough to startle two grooms and a fine grey mare into a fit of skittish stamping. Then she landed, with what he privately suspected was deliberate theatre, right on top of the carved stone finial above the courtyard's central archway. There she sat, wings half-spread, watching the scrambling grooms with something that could only be called satisfaction.

Heads turned. His, among them, though for a different reason than everyone else's.

*Subtle,* he thought at her.

*You were being ignored,* Iruli returned, entirely unrepentant. *I find that dull.*

It was, in this fashion, a startled mare, a badly behaved crow, and the small chaos of two grooms trying at once to calm a horse and find whatever madman had loosed a bird into the courtyard, that Sancharan stopped being unremarked, after a full watch of careful stillness.

The woman who crossed the courtyard toward the commotion moved like someone used to being watched, and thoroughly tired of it. He clocked this in the space of two strides, the same way he clocked most things about most people before they had spoken a single word to him, a habit older than caring, and considerably harder to break. She wore no obvious mark of rank, only plain court silk and hair bound simply against the evening. And yet every servant in that courtyard adjusted their posture slightly as she passed, the unconscious deference people pay to blood before they've had time to think about paying it.

![Sancharan entering the palace and the drama follows](01_04_SancharanEntry.jpg)

"That bird," she said, arriving in front of him with the directness of someone who had decided, somewhere between the archway and this exact spot, that directness would save time, "belongs to you."

"She belongs to no one," Sancharan said. "She merely tolerates my company more than most."

"How fortunate for you both." Her gaze moved over him, quick, assessing, the particular sharpness of someone cataloguing a stranger for later use, not for present pleasure. "You're the scholar. From the southern collegium."

"I am a scholar," he agreed. It was not, strictly, a lie, and it had the added virtue of answering nothing at all.

"Whose request to examine this court's archive arrived a month ago," she said, "and whose arrival happens to fall on the same evening the archivist who guarded that archive was found dead behind a door no one could explain." She said it lightly. It did not land lightly. "Forgive me if I find the timing remarkable."

*Ah,* he thought, with the weary clarity of a man who has seen this shape before, in many faces, across many centuries. *Here it is, then.*

He had lived through this exact moment many times. It was the fast, frightened intelligence of someone reaching for suspicion, because suspicion at least gives a story edges, something to hold onto. The alternative was admitting a good man simply died, and the universe offers no account for it. In this body, under this name, he did not carry the usual weight of being on the receiving end of that fear. And yet something in him, old, tired, worn smooth by countless versions of this same conversation under countless other names, braced for it anyway. It was like a hand bracing for cold water, even after being told, correctly, that this water is warm.

"Forgive me instead," he said, and heard his own voice come out flatter than he intended, "if I decline to apologise for a dead man's timing, which was, I imagine, entirely his own affair and none of mine."

Even as he said it, he understood it was not the right thing to say to a grieving court, to a woman who had, he could see now beneath the sharpness, the particular tightness around her eyes of someone holding a single afternoon's shock very carefully in both hands. She had plainly cared for whoever had died. His words were cruel in the specific, economical way true things can sometimes be cruel. He watched them land. He watched her decide, in the space of one indrawn breath, to be angrier rather than hurt. He recognised the choice with old and unwelcome familiarity, it belonged to someone who learned young that anger holds its shape better than grief does, in rooms full of people waiting to see you fail.

*That was unkind,* he told himself, in the same private register he usually kept for Iruli. *You did not need to say it that way.*

He had not meant, beneath the old reflexive armour, to be unkind at all. He was not entirely sure, even turning it over as her expression closed against him, what exactly he had meant. Only that he was tired of being met with suspicion before he had done anything to earn it, and tiredness, worn long enough, has a way of coming out of a mouth sharper than the heart behind it intends.

"How reassuring," Vaanathi said, each word set down like a stone, "to know the southern collegium teaches its scholars such fine manners alongside their letters." She turned to go, then turned back, she was not yet finished, and there was something almost admirable, he thought distantly, in how little effort it cost her to keep finding more to say. "You'll forgive me if I have you watched, scholar. This court has had quite enough this week of things arriving in the dark, with no explanation for how they got here."

She left before he could answer, which was, he suspected, exactly the point.

He stood a moment in the settling dusk. Iruli drifted back down to his shoulder with the unbearable smugness of a creature who had watched the whole exchange, and clearly intended to have opinions about it at length.

*Well,* she said. *That went extremely well.*

He did not honour this either. He was thinking instead, against every instinct centuries had trained into him, about the exact colour her eyes had gone in the last of the light. Not dark, as he'd expected from across the courtyard, but brown with something warmer underneath, like a honey drop held up to a lamp. And he was thinking about how thoroughly he intended never to mention having noticed this, to anyone, ever, least of all to the very interested crow currently watching him, head cocked at an angle that suggested she had noticed him noticing.

*Not one word,* he told her.

*I said nothing,* said Iruli, with the particular innocence of a creature who had said, in fact, nothing at all, and fully intended to make up for the omission later.

***

Vaanathi did not look back as she crossed the courtyard, which cost her more than she would have liked to admit.

She had meant to be cold. She had not meant, she turned the thought over with something like irritation at herself, to notice, in the middle of being cold, his particular stillness. The way he had stood through the whole exchange like a man who had absorbed far worse insults than hers, and found them, in the end, unremarkable. That should have made him easier to dismiss. Instead it did something closer to the opposite. A small, unwelcome curiosity lodged itself beside the anger, the way a splinter lodges beside a bruise, and makes the whole area ache in a way you cannot quite locate.

*He all but admitted he doesn't care that a man died,* she reminded herself, climbing the north stair two steps at a time, in a manner entirely unbecoming a princess of the line. *That is not a mystery. That is simply an unpleasant man.*

She almost believed it. She had gotten quite good, over the years, at almost believing the things that made a day easier to finish.

## IV

The stables at that hour were mostly empty. The grooms had gone to their supper, and the last light drained slowly from the sky in the orange way it always did over the western wall this time of year. Sancharan found the quiet more restful than he'd like to admit, and sat a while on an overturned feed bucket, his back against sun-warmed stone, letting the day settle.

![Iruli and Sancharan talk](01_05_Sancharan_Iruli.jpg)

Iruli preened one wing with theatrical unconcern for a good while before she spoke again.

*You liked her,* she said. *You may deny it. I will simply continue to be correct.*

"I insulted her within the first minute of speaking to her."

*Yes,* Iruli agreed. *Very effective courtship strategy. I look forward to seeing what you attempt next, perhaps you could set something of hers on fire.*

"I did not choose to insult her. It simply happened."

*Things you do not choose to happen, and yet which happen with such remarkable frequency around you.* She hopped closer along the low stable wall, dropping her voice, not that anyone was near enough to overhear, but old habits held regardless. *For what it's worth, old friend, she is not wrong to be suspicious. Someone in this palace wanted that man dead, and wanted it to look like nothing at all. I flew the east wing roofline before you'd even reached the gate. There's a service door behind the archive, bricked mostly shut, the kind no one thinks to watch, and the mortar around it has been disturbed recently. Fresh scrape marks on the stone sill. Someone used it within the last several days.*

Sancharan sat up slightly. "You didn't lead with this."

*I was building to it. You interrupted with your feelings.*

"I have no feelings."

*You have an entire courtyard's worth of feelings, currently walking back toward the west wing with her jaw set very tightly, and you are welcome to keep insisting otherwise to a bird who has known you considerably longer than you have known yourself.* Iruli clicked her beak, as close as she came, in her own vocabulary, to gentleness. *The door, Sancharan. Someone came and went from that archive without ever needing the bolt at all. Whoever locked it from the inside afterward wanted very badly for everyone to believe the room had been sealed. That is not the work of any shadow the court's frightened ministers are muttering about. That is the work of a hand. A careful one.*

He said nothing for a moment, watching the last colour drain from the sky, turning the thought over the way he turned over most things, slowly, without urgency, because urgency had rarely served him, and he saw no reason it would start now.

"Then someone in this court," he said finally, "is content to let an old fear do the work a careful hand doesn't want credit for."

*That,* said Iruli, "is usually how it begins."

## V

By the time the sun had fully set, the court's official story had already begun its quiet spread through the palace's lower halls, the way such stories always did, passed steward to steward, softened at each retelling, until by morning it had worn down to something almost gentle: the keeper Smaran, beloved of the archive these thirty years, had died of a failing heart, alone, at his desk, as old men sometimes do. A season of ill fortune, some still murmured over supper, out of the emperor's hearing. Nothing more official than that. Nothing anyone could be punished for believing, so long as they believed it quietly.

Vaanathi heard the official story delivered to her directly by her mother, with the particular gentleness Manasa reserved for news she suspected her daughter would not accept gracefully. She did not argue. She had learned, in this same long week that had taught her the exact weight of a closed mouth, that arguing with an explanation the whole court had already agreed to be relieved by served no purpose beyond tiring herself out, for an audience of no one.

Instead she waited until the corridors had emptied for the night, until the last lamp-boys had made their rounds and gone. Then she walked back down toward the archive alone, telling no one, carrying nothing but a small oil lamp and the particular stubbornness that Nila liked to say, not entirely fondly, was the one part of her that could never be talked out of anything.

The reading chamber's door stood closed again, resealed by the palace's own steward in the old, formal way. A fresh disc of wax was pressed over the join of door and frame. It was meant to stay unbroken until someone with the standing to authorise it chose to break it. She had watched this done a hundred times in her life, on storerooms, on treasuries, and once on a minister's chambers under a cloud of suspicion that turned out, disappointingly, to be nothing at all. She knew exactly what the royal seal looked like pressed into wax, without needing to think about it. She had seen it stamped on a thousand documents since she was old enough to read them.

This was not that.

She held the lamp closer, close enough that the wax caught the light and threw its small shadow up the door's dark wood. The mark pressed into it was careful and precise, clearly made with real skill and no haste at all. It was not the sunburst-and-crown of the Anantha line. It was a narrow ring, broken at one point along its edge by a thin dark wedge, like a circle with a single bite taken cleanly out of it.

![Vaanathi sees the symbol](01_06_Vaanathi_Sees_Seal.jpg)

She had never, in all her years growing up inside every roogitm this palace had, seen that mark before in her life.

Vaanathi stood a long moment in the empty corridor, lamp held steady, though her hand, she noticed distantly, was not quite as steady as she would have liked. She understood, with the cold clarity that only arrives once true fear has finished being loud and settled into something quieter and far more lasting, that whatever the court had agreed, that morning, to call an ending, was not one at all.

It was, she thought, closing her fingers around the small clay lamp until its warmth reached her skin, only the first mark of something that had not yet finished being written.
