## I

Vaanathi woke before dawn with brick dust in her mind. She dressed in five breaths and went straight back to the reading chamber.

She was too late. Three masons were already at work.

They had set up a plank frame in front of the shelves. A boy stirred a bucket of wet lime. One man laid a fresh course of brick across the old dry stack, tapping each one into place with the handle of his trowel. The wall behind the shelves was already two hands thicker than it had been the night before.

"Stop," she said.

The men looked round. The one with the trowel gave her a half bow and went on tapping.

"I said stop."

"Highness." He sounded patient, as people do with a child who has wandered into a workshop. "We have orders from the minister. The room is to be made safe."

"Safe from what?"

"A wall that was never closed, Highness. It is a danger. Someone might fall."

"Someone might fall into what?"

He had no answer. He looked at his boy, who looked at the bucket.

Vaanathi walked up to the new brickwork and put her hand on it. The lime was wet and cold. It would harden by noon. After that, to reach what lay behind the wall, you would need a hammer and a day.

"Put down the trowel," she said. "Please do it now. If you want to argue with the minister, argue with him. I will argue with you if you lay one more brick."

She did not raise her voice. She had learned long ago that a loud princess is only noisy. A quiet one is worth listening to. The mason put down the trowel.

"Thank you. Now. Do you know what is behind this wall?"

"No, Highness. Only that there is an old stair. We were told to cover it."

"An old stair."

"It goes down, Highness. I looked, when we came in. Twenty steps and then a fall of earth. It is filled up. Nobody could pass."

"Show me."

It took the three of them a quarter of an hour to lift out the new bricks and then the old. Vaanathi stood with her arms folded and watched her own hands shake. She told herself it was the early cold.

When the hole was wide enough, she took a lamp and stepped through.

The stair was narrow. It was cut into the rock and worn at the centre of each step into a shallow dip. She counted as she went down. At fourteen the air changed. It became cool and damp, with the faint mineral smell of deep water. At twenty the passage ended in a slope of packed earth, floor to roof.

The mason had been right. Nobody could pass.

But the earth was not dry. It shone in the lamplight. A thread of water ran down the near wall and gathered on the lowest step in a small clear pool. It was the same dripping she had heard the night before. She stood and listened to it. It fell slowly, and with perfect regularity, and she had the unpleasant thought that it was counting.

She climbed back up. Sancharan was standing in the chamber.

"I did not hear you come," she said.

"People rarely do." He glanced at the masons, who had gone very still, and then at the hole. "You have been down."

"It ends in a fall of earth."

"Does it?"

"What do you mean?"

"Nothing yet." He looked past her into the dark of the stair. Then he turned to the masons. "Please do not lay any more bricks. Leave the opening as it is. Post a guard from the princess's own household. Not the minister's."

"That is not yours to order," said Vaanathi.

"No. It is yours." He met her eyes. "I was only hoping you would give it."

She was angry for about as long as it takes to draw a breath. Then she gave the order.

## II

They burned Smaran at noon, on the river bank below the city, where the ground was bare of grass and the wind came off the water.

The pyre was made of sandalwood and mango. Kings were burned on sandalwood. A record-keeper was not, and Vaanathi knew who had paid for it. She saw her father at the front, in plain white, with his hands joined. He did not look at her. He looked at the wood.

A boy of about ten stood beside the pyre with a torch. He was Smaran's sister's grandson, the only family the old man had left. He held the torch in both hands. The flame shook. When the priest nodded, he put it to the wood and then stepped backward, very fast, and bumped into the back of a courtier's knees.

The courtier did not mind. Nobody did. Vaanathi felt her throat close.

Her mother stood on the other side of her, a small woman in a plain white wrap with a coil of dark hair tied low at the neck. Empress Manasa had been crying quietly for some time. She was not a loud person. She cried the way she did everything, without fuss, and then she took Vaanathi's hand without looking and held it.

"You went back last night," she said. It was barely a whisper.

"Who told you?"

"A guard. Several guards. Child, you went to a dead man's chamber in the dark."

"I went with a scholar."

"That is worse." She squeezed Vaanathi's fingers. "Be careful, my dear. You were always the one who walked into rooms. Your father and I stood outside and held our breath."

"I know, Amma."

"I do not think you do." Her mother's eyes went to the pyre and stayed there. "Not yet."

The flames climbed. The smoke went straight up and then bent east, towards the flooded fields.

She looked for Sancharan. He had stayed at the back of the crowd, by himself, on a patch of ground a little higher than the rest. He stood with his hands at his sides and his face turned to the fire. Iruli was on a thorn bush beside him, the one dark thing in a pale crowd.

She saw him do something odd.

It was nothing much. The fire had burned down enough that the wood began to settle. In that moment, when the logs shifted and sparks flew, the flames did not quite move as flames should. They hung in the air a heartbeat longer. The sparks rose, and rose, and fell very slowly.

Then a cough from the crowd broke the stillness, and the fire went back to its ordinary quick business.

She told herself it was the smoke. She told herself that she had been up all night. She told herself a great many sensible things, and watched him all the while.

When the pyre had burned to red ash, the priests carried water from the river to put it out. The crowd began to scatter. She turned, and found him standing close behind her.

"He was loved," Sancharan said.

"He was." She wiped her eyes with the back of her hand. It was a gesture she would have been ashamed of in court. "Do you do that to every funeral?"

"Do what?"

"Stand at the back and look as though you are judging it."

"I am not judging it." He spoke very quietly. "I am only noticing how many people came. It is a good count for a quiet man. It is a better one than most kings get."

She had no answer for that. It was, she realised, the first kind thing she had heard him say.

## III

Her father's summons came before evening. He received her in the small garden behind the council hall, on a bench beneath a champak tree. He had sent his attendants away. That in itself was a warning.

"Sit," said Anantaraja.

She sat. The champak scent was heavy in the warm air. Somewhere a bird gave a single long note.

"You went to the chamber again."

"Yes."

"You broke the seal."

"I had your leave."

"You had my leave to see it. You did not have leave to break it." He said it mildly. "I have not decided how angry to be."

"You may decide when you like."

A very small crease appeared between his brows. In another man it might have been amusement.

"The scholar," he said. "What do you make of him?"

She considered the question honestly.

"He tells the truth and gives nothing away," she said. "I think he is the cleverest man I have met. I do not think he is a scholar."

"Why not?"

"He does not carry anything. A scholar carries a bag of leaves and an ink pot and a list of complaints."

"That is a very reasonable observation." Her father looked at the champak, not at her. "He is a guest of the throne. He will have the freedom of the palace. I wish him to see what he needs to see."

"Yes, Father."

"I also wish to know what he does."

She turned her head.

"You will be his guide," Anantaraja said. "You will take him where he asks to go. You will sit when he sits and walk when he walks. And you will tell me, each night, where he went and what he said."

"You want me to watch him."

"I want you to accompany him."

"That is the same thing with kinder words."

"It is," said the emperor. "Kind words cost very little. I advise you to use them."

She could not tell whether this was a punishment or a gift. Her father had never made it easy to tell. For years he had kept her one step below the throne and one step away from the work. Now he was sending her out into the palace at the elbow of a man he did not trust, and he did not meet her eyes while he did it.

"Why me?" she asked. "You have two hundred men who watch people for a living."

"Because he will not mind you." He did look at her then, briefly. It was the first time in a month. "He minded the gate officer. He minded the masons. He minded Charudatta. I saw it. He does not mind you."

She had nothing to say. The bird sang its single note again.

"Last thing," said her father. "You asked yesterday to feed the eastern families from the crown's stores. I heard it. You did not say it. You did not need to."

"I was stopped."

"You were. I let you be stopped, because the court was watching. I will not do it twice." He rose. His shadow fell across her knees. "Send an officer east with grain. Send him tonight. Take it from the reserve at Kolur, the part that did not fall. Do not tell Charudatta until the carts have gone."

She stared at him.

"Father."

"Go and watch your scholar," said Anantaraja. He did not smile. But he laid one broad hand briefly on the top of her head, as he had when she was six. Then he walked off down the path and did not look back.

## IV

The first thing Sancharan asked to see was the river.

She took him to the embankment below the east wall, where a flight of broad steps went down to the water. The light was gold and low. Fishermen had pulled their boats high up the bank and sat mending nets in silence. Nobody sang.

On the way they passed the water-clock in the lower court. It was a copper bowl with a hole in the bottom, floating in a larger jar, and it had been used to mark the hours since her great-grandfather's day. A boy sat beside it on a stool, ready to strike the gong when the bowl sank.

Sancharan stopped. He watched the bowl for a while.

"It is slow," he said.

"It was set at dawn."

"Then it was set well. But it is slow."

The boy stood up quickly. "It is not, sir. It is exactly right."

"I am sure it is. It will correct itself when I go." He walked on. "I am sorry for the trouble."

Vaanathi looked back. The bowl had started to sink again, quicker now, as if to make up lost time. She decided not to ask.

At the river, he went down the steps to the very last one and crouched, and put his fingers into the current. He stayed that way for a long time. She stood three steps above him and said nothing. The water slid past his hand without a ripple.

"It is not meant to move like this," he said at last.

"How is it meant to move?"

"Like something that has somewhere to go."

When he straightened and turned, his foot slipped on the wet stone. She was beside him before she thought. She caught his forearm in both her hands. It was warm. It was solid. It was, she realised, much heavier than any arm had a right to be.

He went still. Then he took his arm away. He did it gently, but at once, as though the touch had cost him something.

"Do not take my hand, Princess," he said. "Take the rail."

"I was only trying to help."

"I know." He climbed past her up the steps. "I forget my own weight. It is a failing."

She stood on the step with her hands still half raised. She had touched a great many people in her life. Ministers, servants, her mother, Nila. Not one of them had ever made her feel that she had come too close to a fire.

She did not ask why. She was, she found, afraid of the answer.

## V

The archive had been put back in order by midafternoon. The guards had gone. Ratnan had swept the floors himself, with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, as though it were a pious duty.

"I did not know where else to put my hands," he said, when Vaanathi came in. "Highness. Sir."

Sancharan inclined his head. Ratnan seemed unable to look at him. Iruli had settled on the top of the door frame, and each time the young man passed beneath her he ducked.

"I need the old ledgers," Vaanathi said. "The ones for the treasury. As far back as you have them."

"All of them?"

"All of them."

It took an hour to haul them out. They were wrapped in oiled cloth and tied with cord. Ratnan laid them on the long table in careful rows, thirty bundles, each one thick as a man's wrist. The room smelled of old oil and old time.

Vaanathi untied the first. Sancharan took the second. For a long while neither of them spoke. The leaves were cramped with columns of figures, in a script that grew steadily more formal the further back they went. Grain in. Grain out. Salaries. Tribute from the hill chiefs. A fee to a bridge builder. A fine on a drunk elephant keeper.

She saw it first.

It was a tick mark. That was all. It sat in the margin beside a single line, and she nearly passed it over. But the tick was not a tick. It was a small round shape, drawn very neatly, with spokes inside it and a wedge cut out at the bottom.

"Here," she said.

Sancharan crossed the table and looked over her shoulder. He did not touch the leaf. He did not need to. His face went quiet.

"What does the line say?"

She read it aloud. "Paid to the House of the Tower. Oil, nine measures. Rice, nine measures. Cloth, nine lengths. For the keeping of the sky." She frowned. "It is a stipend. For the sky-tower. The old observatory on the east wall."

"Which no one uses."

"Which no one has used for years. The astronomers moved to the new hall in my grandfather's time." She turned the leaf. There was another entry with the same mark, a year later. Then another. She turned faster. "It goes back and back. Every year, the same. It is the same amount."

"And the latest?"

She went to the newest bundle. It was in Smaran's own hand, the beautiful clear letters she had known all her life. She found the current year. She found the entry. It was there, with the mark beside it. *Paid to the House of the Tower.* And it had been paid, she saw, only three months ago.

The tower was not empty. Someone had been living there. Someone had been fed from the crown's own purse for as long as the palace had stood, under a mark that nobody remembered.

"Ratnan," she said. "Who is the warden of the sky-tower?"

"I do not know, Highness. I have never heard of one."

"Who delivers the oil and the grain?"

"I... a boy from the stores, I think. They are listed under general supply. I have never looked." His face had gone paler. "I am sorry. I should have looked."

"You could not have known."

"Smaran knew," Ratnan said slowly. "He must have. He wrote it every year."

"Yes."

She went on reading. Beside the latest entry, in the margin, in the same neat hand, there were three small words. They were not part of the account. They were squeezed into the corner, as though written in haste.

*Ask the bell.*

Sancharan read them over her shoulder. For a moment he said nothing. When he spoke, it was so low that only she could hear.

"There are two kinds of men who leave messages in margins, Princess. Men who wish to be understood. And men who wish only to be understood by one particular person."

"Which was he?"

"I think," he said, "that he was writing to you."

She looked at the words until the letters ran together. Then she shut the ledger with great care and got to her feet.

"Ratnan. Where is the bell?"

"The bell, Highness?"

"The old bell in the treasury," she said. "The one nobody rings."
