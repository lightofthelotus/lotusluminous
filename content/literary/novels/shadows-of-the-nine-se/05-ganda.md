## I

The river gate stood at the bottom of forty stone steps. At night the steps were slick with spray. Vaanathi went down them one at a time, holding the wall with one hand and her lamp with the other.

She had expected a crowd. A scholar sent for by the emperor would surely arrive with servants, a palanquin, a bundle of scrolls the size of an ox. But the gate yard held only a dozen guards, a torch in an iron bracket, and a single man standing in the middle of them.

He was tall. That was the first thing she saw. He was lean and brown, with dark hair that fell past his shoulders and a grey-blue cloth wound at his waist. A second cloth hung over his left shoulder. He wore one plain gold band on his upper arm and nothing else. He carried no bundle at all.

He stood very still. The guards moved round him as water moves round a post.

"I am telling you, you will open it," said the gate officer. He was a thick man with a loud voice. He was holding out one hand. "Every traveller is searched. Even scholars."

"I carry nothing," the tall man said.

"Then you will not mind."

"I do not mind. I only point out that there is nothing to find."

His voice was low and unhurried. It was the voice of someone who had never needed to raise it. The officer's face darkened. He took a step forward.

Something black dropped out of the night.

It was a crow. It came down from the roof of the gate without a sound and landed on the officer's wrist. She was big and glossy, with a blue-violet sheen on her wings and a yellow-gold eye. Before the man could shout, she had closed her beak on the iron key ring at his belt. She pulled it free with a single jerk. Then she was up on the roof again, keys jingling, sitting very upright, looking down at everyone as though they were servants who had been slow with her dinner.

The yard went silent. Then one of the younger guards made a noise that was nearly a laugh and turned it into a cough.

"That," said the officer, "is a bird."

"She is called Iruli," said the tall man. "She does not take orders. I should say that I have also given up."

Vaanathi walked forward into the torchlight.

"Let him through," she said.

The officer spun round and found a princess behind him. His mouth opened. He shut it. He bowed so fast she heard his belt creak.

"Highness. I did not know."

"You do now. He is expected. Please find your keys. I think they are on the roof."

The man in grey-blue looked at her for the first time. His eyes were dark and level. They did not skim her the way most people's did, noting the jewellery and the rank and moving on. They stayed. She had the odd sense of being read, one line at a time.

"Princess Vaanathi," he said. It was not a question.

"And you are the scholar from the south."

"I am called Sancharan."

"That is a name for a wanderer."

"It is a good name for a man who has walked a long way."

"How long?"

"Long enough to forget the start of it." He said it lightly. It did not sound light. "Shall we go in, Princess? The guards have begun to stare."


She could not tell if he was joking. He did not smile. But the corner of his mouth was not quite still.

He started up the stairs. She fell in beside him because she could not think how to avoid it.

"You came on foot," she said.

"The road is long. But the horse would have been alarmed."

"Of you?"

"Of the road." He glanced down at her. "Horses are sensible. They dislike places where something has gone wrong."

"Has something gone wrong on the road?"

"Not on the road. Here." He nodded at the dark bulk of the palace above them. "You have a flood, a drowned ferryman and a fallen roof. You have a dead keeper. You have a minister who stands very close to your father. And you have a princess who comes down forty wet steps at night to meet a stranger herself, rather than send a servant."

"I wanted to see you before anyone told you what to say."

"Yes," he said. "I noticed."

It was not a compliment. It did not feel like an insult. She was not sure what it felt like, and she did not care for that.

## II

Sancharan watched the princess climb back up the stairs ahead of him and thought that she was holding her lamp like a weapon.

It was a small thing. Most people held a lamp like something that might spill. She held hers low and steady, with her elbow tucked in, ready to swing. She had found a dead man's room that morning. He could tell. She walked like someone who had decided not to be afraid and was spending a great deal of effort on it.

Iruli settled on his shoulder. Her claws were light. She had not sat on him for two hundred years, and now she sat there as if she had never left.

*Do not look so pleased,* he thought at her. *We are not here to enjoy ourselves.*

Iruli clicked her beak. She had always been difficult to argue with.

The city lay around them in the dark. Anantapuram. When he had last come this way, it had been a hill with a well on top. Now it had walls and towers and a market that never slept. The people had built it themselves, one brick at a time, and they had done it in the full knowledge that every brick would fall. He had never understood why that made him tender. It did.

He had not meant to come. Few of his kind went among mortals. It had been agreed long ago that they would not. But three nights ago, a name had been spoken in a sealed room. It was the name he had given to a king, in this very place, before the first stone of the palace was laid. *Sancharan. The one who walks.* It had been written in a founder's sealed instructions, to be used only if the line was in danger.

Someone in the Anantha house had broken that seal and said the name aloud. And so he had come.

The weight of the river pressed on him as he climbed. It was wrong. He had felt it from a hundred leagues away, and he felt it more strongly now. The water was moving where it should have been still. It was a very small wrongness, as small as a hair in a bowl of milk.

He knew its cause. He did not like it.

Iruli leaned her head against his ear. She had never been sentimental. She did it now, briefly, and then pulled away as if it had not happened.

*I know,* he told her. *I know what it is. I was there.*

That was the part he did not tell anyone, because there was no one to tell. A long time ago, on a night he did not like to count, something had broken. It had been a quiet thing, and it had been terrible. A woman had died. A door had been left open. Every creature that mattered in the sky had felt it. Most had decided it was over. He had known better. Doors do not close themselves.

Ahead of him the princess paused at the turn of the stair and looked back. Her eyes were large and dark and rimmed with kohl. She looked tired. She looked angry. She looked, he thought, like someone who had never been allowed to finish a sentence.

He had judged kings and kingdoms and the long slow endings of cities. He judged them without hatred and without mercy. It was his work. He had learned to look at a living person and see all of it at once, the beginning and the end laid side by side.

He looked at her and saw very little. The edges of her life were not as clear as they should have been. It troubled him.

He turned his attention to the stairs.

## III

"You will tell me what you know," Vaanathi said.

They had reached the palace's inner court. A single lamp burned above an empty archway. Beyond it a long corridor ran into darkness. She stopped in front of him, and he stopped, and Iruli shifted on his shoulder.

"My friend says tonight is a ganda," she said. She did not know why she said it. "A bad joint between two lunar houses."

"Your friend is right."

"You believe in that?"

"I believe in joints." He looked up at the thin sliver of sky above the court. "A thing that is whole holds together well. A thing that is jointed holds only as long as the joint does. Tonight two houses of the moon meet and part. It is the same as a bridge between two banks. The bridge is the weakest place, and the crossing is when you notice."

"That is a very long way of saying yes."

"It is a long way of saying, be careful where you stand."

"I know very little," said Sancharan. "I was asked to read the signs. I have not read them yet."

"The emperor asked you for a reason."

"The emperor gave me a reason. It was a short one."

"What did he say?"

"That a keeper of records had died in a locked room. That three misfortunes had come in a month. That nobody could find a cause." He tilted his head. "He also said that the court was frightened, and that I should keep my findings quiet."

"Then you will tell me them."

"I will tell the emperor."

"I am his daughter."

"You are." He said it without unkindness. "Which is why you will hear them from him, if he wishes."

She stared at him. In all her years in court, nobody had spoken to her quite like this. They bowed, or they brushed her aside. This man did neither. He answered every question truthfully and gave her nothing at all.

"You are very good at that," she said.

"At what?"

"Refusing without saying no."

"It is a small talent." Something moved at the edge of his mouth again. "I have had a long time to practise."

"You look thirty."

"I look as I choose to look."

It was such a strange answer that she almost laughed. She did not. She did not trust it.

"I am going to the reading chamber," she said. "Tonight. I was going whether you came or not. If you wish to read signs, you may read them there."

"I would like that."

"You may not touch anything."

"I will touch only what I must."

She turned and walked into the corridor. He followed her, and she could not hear his footsteps. Only Iruli's claws, ticking softly against his shoulder.

The chamber door was shut. The royal wax disc sat in the middle of it, dull in the lamplight. Beside it, cut small into the wax, was the mark.

Vaanathi had been working out how to say it. She had decided to say nothing. She wanted to see what he did.

He did not move at first. He simply looked.

The change in him was small. If she had not been watching his face she would have missed it. His eyes stayed on the mark a moment longer than they should have. His breath went out slowly, as if he had set down something heavy. For an instant he looked like a very old man.

Then it was gone.

"You have seen this," she said.

"Yes."

"Where?"

"A long way from here." He did not look at her. "And a very long time ago."

"What is it?"

"A wheel." He touched nothing. His hand stayed at his side. "A wheel with a piece missing."

"I can see that."

"Then you know as much as I do about its shape." His voice was quiet. "I do not know who cut it into your emperor's seal. But whoever it was had a steady hand and a great deal of patience. I would be careful of them."

She felt cold, and annoyed at being cold.

"I have leave to open the room," she said. She put her thumb against the royal disc and pressed. The wax cracked down the middle and fell away in two halves. The mark split with it. She found she was sorry.

She pushed the door open.

The reading chamber was as she had left it. A cold smell hung in the air. The shelves stood in their rows. The writing table sat in its corner, with the blank palm leaf in front of the empty seat. The lamp stood where she had asked them to leave it.

Sancharan stepped inside. Iruli lifted off his shoulder and went to the top of a shelf, where she settled like a small black judge.

Iruli watched him from the shelf. She watched everyone else in the room with the same cold amusement. But when he moved, her eyes followed him alone.

He stood for a time in the middle of the floor. He did not walk about. He did not touch the table. He closed his eyes.

It lasted about as long as it takes to count ten. Vaanathi watched his face and tried to guess what he was doing. She thought of a man listening for a sound in the next room. She thought of someone tasting the air.

When he opened his eyes, he was looking at the wall behind the shelves.

"What is behind that?"

"Nothing. The outer wall of the archive."

"There was a door."

"A service door. It was bricked up before I was born."

"Who told you so?"

She opened her mouth. Then she shut it again. Nobody had told her. She had simply always known. Everybody did. It was the sort of fact that did not need a source.

"Smaran told me once," she said slowly. "When I was a child. He said it was for coolness. He said the room had once been too warm."

"Did he say it was bricked?"

"I do not remember."

Sancharan crossed to the shelves. He ran his eyes along them, top to bottom, the way she had read the genealogy leaves. He stopped at the third shelf from the end. Without a word he put both hands on the wooden frame and drew it away from the wall.

It moved with a soft groan. It was lighter than it should have been. Behind it was a patch of plain brick, floor to ceiling, about the width of a doorway. In the middle of the brick hung a small iron ring.

Vaanathi had stood in this room for a hundred afternoons. She had not known there was a ring.

"This is a bricked door," she said.

"It is," he said. "Look at the floor."

She lowered the lamp. A fan of faint scratches ran across the stone, as if something heavy had been dragged a short distance and set back. They were new. The dust had not yet settled in them.

"And the bricks."

She looked closer. The lines between them were dark and clean. She put out a finger and pressed the nearest edge. It moved a fraction under her touch.

There was no mortar between the bricks. There was only a loose stack of them, laid dry, and a wall that was not a wall at all.

Behind them, somewhere in the dark, something dripped. It was slow and patient, like a clock.

Iruli opened her wings on the top shelf and made a low sound in her throat. It was the first sound Vaanathi had heard the crow make that was not a click.

"Then we open it," said Vaanathi. She had already set the lamp down on the table. She had already put her fingers on the first brick.

"No."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Not tonight." He did not touch her. He did not need to. He stood where he was and the word hung in the room, and she felt it settle on her hands. "Whatever is behind that wall has been waiting a long time. It can wait until you have guards you trust at your back, and daylight to see by."

"The man who used this door may be gone by morning."

"The man who used this door is already gone. He took what he came for. If you pull that wall down now, you will find a passage and a draught and nothing more." His voice softened by a very little. "You will also find that you are alone with a stranger in a room where someone has died. I would not like you to tell your father I made you do it."

She looked at the brick, and at him. She hated that he was right.

"You are very sure for a man who has been here half an hour."

"I am not sure of much," said Sancharan. "I am sure of that."

"Princess," Sancharan said quietly. "You have a door. Someone has been using it."

"The guards said the room was bolted from inside."

"I believe them."

"Then how..." She stopped. She had seen it herself in the moment she said it.

The room had been shut. The door had been barred. Nothing had come in through it. But a door can be shut from within and still have a second door behind it, and she had known this room all her life without ever once seeing it.

She put her palm flat against the cold brick, and felt, very faintly, the air of a long passage breathing against her skin.
