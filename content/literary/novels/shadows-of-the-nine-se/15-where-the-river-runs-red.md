## I

The boat was a flat-bottomed ferry with a patched sail and a boatman who did not want to talk. Sancharan had hired it at the north landing before sunrise. He had paid in silver, and the man had counted the silver twice and then stopped asking questions.

They went upriver with the wind behind them. Vaanathi sat in the bow sitting curled on the mat and her back against a coil of rope. She had slept three hours in two nights. She did not feel tired. She felt thin, like a cloth washed too many times.

The river was wide here and slow. On both banks the land lay flat and green. Palm trees stood in rows. Egrets walked in the shallows with their heads cocked. It was a fine morning. If she had not known what she knew, she would have called it beautiful.

Behind her, Sancharan sat amidships with his back to the mast and his palms open on his knees. Iruli had gone on ahead. She appeared now and then as a black speck against the sky and was gone again.

"Where are we going?" said Vaanathi.

"To a hill called Sivandha Kundru. A day's sail. It stands where the river turns."

"What is on it?"

"A stone." His gaze stayed away from her. "And a friend of mine, who has not been glad to see anyone for some while."

"What sort of friend?"

"A loud one."

He said no more. She let it lie. They had not spoken much since the room beneath the throne. She had gone to bed that night with her father's key under her pillow, and woken twice thinking she heard a bell. Each time she had lain very still and listened, and each time there had been only the night.

By noon, the water began to change.

It happened so slowly that she did not see it at first. The river had been a clear brown, the colour of dark honey-water left to cool. Then it was the colour of rust, and then of a roof tile, a dull, warm, dusty red. The boatman muttered something and spat over the side. His hand made a small quick sign against his chest.

"It is not blood," he said, to nobody. "I tell the children it is not blood."

"What is it?"

"Mud, mistress. From the red hills. It has always been red here, a little, in the rains." His eyes were fixed on the water. "It was never so red as this. And it was never red in the dry months."

She leaned over the side. The water slid by, thick and sluggish. A dead fish drifted past, belly up, grey-eyed. Then another. Then a whole slow raft of them, turning gently in the current, their scales gone dull as old coins.

She sat back. She did not look again.

"The stone is bleeding," said Sancharan quietly.

"Bleeding?"

"It is a way of speaking. It is iron in the rock, going out into the water. It should not. It means the stone is hurt." He drew a long breath. "I would like to be wrong. I do not often get to be."

## II

They put in at a village at the bend, a cluster of mud houses with palm-leaf roofs, huddled on a low bank above the red water. A dozen people stood on the landing. They did not wave. They did not call. They watched the boat come in with the flat patience of people who have run out of ways to be surprised.

An old woman came forward, leaning on a stick. She had a face like a dried fig and one cloudy eye.

"You are not the tax men," she said.

"No."

"You are from the city." Her good eye went over Vaanathi's dress, the gold at her wrist, the bandaged hand. "You are somebody."

"I am the emperor's daughter."

The old woman looked at her for a moment. Then she gave a short dry laugh and spat on the planks. "Then you have come to the right place to see what the emperor has done for us."

"Amma," said a younger woman sharply, and caught her sleeve.

"No, let her hear it. It is true." The old woman jerked her arm free. "The river went red nine days ago. At the dark of the moon. We drew water at dusk and it was clear. We drew it at dawn and it was thick as blood. The cattle will not drink. The children will not wash. We carry water from a pond two hours inland, and the pond is going low."

"Has anyone been sent?"

"Sent?" The old woman spat again. "Who would send? Our headman rode to the district office on the third day. They said it was silt. They said it would clear."

"It is not silt," said Vaanathi.

"We know."

"I mean that I know." She stepped off the boat. The planks gave under her, and for a moment she was afraid she would fall. She did not. "I will send grain. And clean water, in jars, by cart. I do not know how long it will take. I will send it by my own order, with my own seal, and no clerk will touch it."

The old woman stared. The people on the landing had gone very quiet.

"You say that," said the old woman slowly. "Many have said it."

"Then watch for the carts."

She took the bronze disc from her neck and held it out. The old woman did not take it. She only looked at it, at the sunburst and the crown, and then up at the face of the girl who held it.

"A name," she said. "Give me a name. So I will know whom to curse if it does not come."

"Vaanathi."

"Vaanathi," said the old woman. She said it again, as one tries a coin between the teeth. Then she nodded, once, and stepped back.

Sancharan had come up beside her. He did not speak. When she turned, she found him looking at her with an expression she had not seen before. It was not quite pride. It was softer than that, and more troubled, as of a man who has heard a note he had not expected and cannot stop listening for.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing." He looked away. "I was only counting how many people in that village will remember your name in forty years."

"How many?"

"All of them."

## III

Sivandha Kundru was a single bare hill, rising from the plain like a clenched fist. The rock was red. It was a deep, scorched, rusty red, the colour of a smith's forge, with veins of black running through it. No trees grew on it. No grass. The soil at its foot was the colour of dried blood.

They went up on foot in the late afternoon. A path wound among the boulders, steep and loose. Vaanathi climbed with her skirts caught up in one hand and her breath coming hard. Sancharan went ahead of her, never turning, and she noticed that he walked more slowly than he needed to.

The path ended on a broad flat summit. In the middle of it stood a stone, about as tall as her waist, squared and carved. It was the twin of the stone in the stepwell. It had the same wheel cut into its face. The same wedge was gone.

Beside it, with his arms crossed, stood a man.

He was not tall. That was the first strange thing. He was shorter than Sancharan by a head, and much broader, with a chest like a barrel and shoulders that blocked half the sky. His skin was a deep, living red, like laterite stone warmed by the sun. His hair was iron-grey and cropped close to his skull. He wore a rust-brown cloth round his waist and nothing else, no ornament, no cloak. His arms were as thick as her thighs.

He did not turn when they came up. He was looking at the stone.

"You took your time," he said.

His voice was deep and scratchy. It sounded like gravel sliding down a slope.

"Lohitan," said Sancharan.

"Slow One." Now he turned. His eyes were small, black and bright, set deep in a face that looked as if it had been hit many times and had not minded. He looked at Sancharan from head to foot. "You look dreadful."

"Thank you."

"Like something the river dragged out." The red man's gaze moved. "And who is this?"

"This is Vaanathi. Of the house of Anantha."

The black eyes narrowed. "A mortal."

"Yes."

"Anantha."

"Yes."

There was a pause. Lohitan unfolded his arms. He took three slow steps towards her and stopped, so close that she could feel the heat coming off him, like the air near a lit oven. He smelled of hot iron.

"Hm," he said.

"Hm?" said Vaanathi.

"I expected someone bigger." He circled her once, deliberately, looking her up and down. "She is not afraid. Or she is hiding it very well. Which is it?"

"It is both," said Vaanathi. "I have been afraid since the stepwell. I have grown rather used to it."

Lohitan stared. Then, abruptly, he threw back his head and laughed. It was a vast, ringing, delighted bark, and it echoed off the rocks. A pair of vultures lifted from a ledge far below.

"Rather used to it!" He slapped his thigh. "Slow One, I like her. Where did you find her?"

"At a river gate."

"At a river gate." The red man wiped his eye with the back of one thick hand. "She is not what I imagined. I imagined someone pale and quiet and very sad. They are always very sad, the ones who wander in here."

"I am not sad," said Vaanathi. "I am angry. I find it more useful."

"So do I." His grin was fierce and not unfriendly. "So do I, girl. So do I."

A black shape dropped from the sky and landed on the top of the stone. Iruli folded her wings, looked at Lohitan, and made a low contemptuous noise.

"Oh, it is you," said Lohitan. "I wondered when you would show your beak."

Iruli said something short and unpleasant.

"I will not." He pointed one finger at her. "And I will thank you to keep your opinion of my posture to yourself."

## IV

Up close, the stone was in a terrible state.

Vaanathi saw it as soon as she stepped round Lohitan. It had been scored and scraped over and over, the grooves cut deep, some old and dull, some bright and new. A pale scatter lay at its foot. She knelt and took a pinch. It was cold, fine and grey. It was the same.

"The same as the stepwell," she said.

"The same as the man in the corridor," said Sancharan.

"What man?" said Lohitan.

Sancharan told him. He told it plainly and briefly, the knife, the assassin, the ring of ash on the palm, the word *Judge*. Lohitan listened with his head lowered. When it was done, he spat on the rock.

"They called you Judge," he said.

"Yes."

"They know you. They have been waiting."

"Yes."

"Then it is as I feared." The red man turned to the stone. His broad shoulders had begun to shake. "I have stood on this hill for four hundred years. I have watched sea-raiders and plagues and a hundred kings. I have never in all that time been unable to protect what is mine. And now someone comes up here, once a month, and carves my stone, and I cannot stop them."

"Cannot?"

"Do you think I do not try?" He rounded on Sancharan. His voice rose. "I stand here every dark moon. I am awake. I have a staff, and I have a temper, and I have not missed a night. And every time, at the same hour, the ground goes soft under me and the air goes thick and I swing at nothing. They come and go like smoke. I do not see their faces. I do not hear their steps."

"At the dark of the moon," said Vaanathi.

"At the dark of the moon."

"When there is no light."

Lohitan stopped. He turned slowly and looked at her. She felt the weight of those small dark eyes, curious and hard.

"Why do you say it so?"

"Because it is the only hour you cannot see. And the only hour the moon does not watch." She spoke slowly, working it out as she said it. "If the moon were in her place, there would be a little light, even at the dark. But it is not the moon's light that is missing. It is the moon herself."

Silence.

The wind blew over the summit. The red rock ticked as it cooled. Iruli, on the stone, had gone very still.

"Who has sat in the pearl seat?" said Vaanathi. "In the great court. I saw it. It was the only seat that was clean."

"Nobody," said Sancharan quietly.

"Since when?"

"A generation." He had gone pale beneath his brown. "She stopped answering. We thought she had withdrawn. She has done it before. She grows tired of us. We let her be."

"And the dark moons grew darker," said Lohitan, in a low voice. "And I never thought to ask."

"Perhaps someone did," said Vaanathi. "Perhaps someone wished to be sure there was nobody left to see."

Neither of them answered. The sun was going down behind the hill, and the red of the rock was deepening to the colour of a coal.

"The ash," said Sancharan at last. "Lohitan. When your stone is scraped. What happens to the stone?"

"What do you mean?"

"The scrapings. Where do they go?"

"They fall." Lohitan frowned. "They turn grey. All of them, as soon as they leave the face. Good stone, laterite, a thousand years old, and it goes to this." He kicked at the pale pile. "It is not how stone should die."

"No."

"It is how fire dies, when it has nothing left to burn." The red man's voice dropped. "I have seen it once before. A long time ago. I did not like it then."

"When?"

"The night the wheels broke."

Vaanathi did not look at Sancharan. She did not need to. She heard him draw a breath and hold it.

## V

They stayed on the hill for the night. Lohitan made a fire of dry brush and thorn, and the three of them sat round it while the stars came out. The crow roosted on the stone and glared at the dark.

He turned out to be a good host in the way that a large bad-tempered dog is a good host. He gave her a flat grey bread, baked on a hot stone, and dried meat that was tough as leather. He told her stories of battles that had happened before her city had a name. They were funny, and loud, and mostly about him losing his temper. When she laughed, he looked pleased.

"There is a thing I will give you," he said, late in the night.

He put his hand into the fold of his waist cloth. When he opened it, a small red bead lay in his palm. It was round and smooth, about the size of a pea.

"Coral," he said. "From the deep. It is not magic. I do not do magic. But it is warm, and it was mine, and I would like you to hold it."

"What is it for?"

"Courage." He turned the bead over with one thick finger. "It is not a feeling, girl. People think it is. It is a tool, like a spear. You pick it up. You hold it. You do not drop it, even when your hands are shaking. That is all it is."

She took the bead. It was warm. It sat in her palm like a small steady heartbeat.

"Thank you," she said.

"Do not thank me. Break something with it." He grinned. "Preferably a wall."

Later, when the fire had burned low and Sancharan had gone a little way off to sit with his back to a boulder and his eyes on the north, Lohitan leaned over and spoke to her in a rumbling undertone. It was meant to be a whisper. It could have been heard in the next valley.

"He is afraid of you."

"I know."

"No. You do not." The small black eyes glittered. "He has been alone for two hundred years. He is very good at it. He has made a fine art of it. I have never seen him look at anyone the way he looked at you when you gave that old woman your name."

"I did not see."

"You were not meant to." The red man sat back. "I have known him since before the first moon rose. He is the slowest of us. He thinks everything through four times, and then he does nothing, in case it was the wrong thing. It is a great strength. It is also a very lonely way to live."

"He says it is impossible."

"Of course he does." Lohitan snorted. "He is the Judge. He weighs. He sees it all laid out in a row. And a person who sees everything laid out in a row often forgets there is a door." He rubbed his square jaw. "I am not saying he is wrong. I have no idea. I am a soldier. I only say that I have watched him for a very long time, and I have never seen him afraid until this week."

She looked across the fire. Sancharan sat with his head bowed. He seemed to be listening to nothing. But his shoulders were stiff, and she knew that he had heard every word.

"Slow One," said Lohitan, not turning his head.

"I heard," said Sancharan.

"Good."

"I would prefer to be left out of it."

"I know you would."

"It is going to be a long night."

"For you, certainly." The red man poked the fire. "I will go on drinking water from the sky, and you will go on pretending you are not listening. Girl, that is called friendship."

Vaanathi laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, and the sound went spinning off into the dark, and a night bird answered it from the plain.

She did not hear the other sound until it was almost too late.

It was a small scrape, below them on the slope. It was the slither of a loose stone. Lohitan's head came up. He was on his feet before she could move, his great hands closing on a staff she had not seen him pick up.

"Down," he said. "Behind the rock."

She dropped. Sancharan was beside her in a breath.

Below them, at the foot of the red hill, a lantern flame bobbed among the rocks. It moved steadily upward. It was carried by a figure in a dark wrap, a figure that walked very quietly, one careful step after another.

It was not coming for the stone.

It was coming up the path to the fire.
