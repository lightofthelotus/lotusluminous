## I

They reached Anantapuram at dusk on the second day. The ferryman set them down at the north landing and rowed away fast, without looking back. Ratnan kissed the stone steps. Nobody laughed at him.

The city looked different. Vaanathi could not say how. The lamp-sellers were out, and the water-carriers, and the women with baskets of limes. But the voices were low, and people stood in doorways and watched the sky. Twice she heard a mother snap at a child for pointing at the river.

"They know," she said.

"They know something is wrong," said Sancharan. "They do not know what. It is worse."

Her father received her in the small garden behind the council hall, under the champak tree. He sat on the bench with his hands between his knees. A tray of food stood untouched beside him. When she came through the gate, he did not rise. He looked at her for a long moment, from her dusty hem to her tired face, and then he held out one arm.

She sat down and let herself be held. It was not like him. It was brief, and clumsy, and he smelled of old lamp smoke. She did not mind.

"I have sent the grain," he said, into her hair. "Twenty carts. Eight of them with water jars. I sent it under your seal, as you wrote. Charudatta objected. I told him it was my daughter's own affair, and that she had the right."

"Did he accept it?"

"He bowed." Her father released her. "He is very good at bowing."

She told him what she could. She told him of the red river and the stone. She told him there had been a council, and that nine had spoken, and that one of them had proposed to take her out of the world. She did not tell him that Sancharan had refused it. She saw, from the stillness of his face, that he guessed.

"They were wrong," said Anantaraja.

"They may not have been."

"They were." He said it very quietly. "I will not argue it. I will only say that I have known the Judge for a week, and I do not believe he is a creature who lets a thing happen. I think he has been learning how."

She looked up at him.

"You gave him a very hard look in that room," she said.

"He deserved it."

"He did."

"And he took it." The corner of the emperor's mouth moved. "That is the part that interests me."

## II

Charudatta was waiting at the foot of the stair that led up to her rooms. She was no longer surprised. He stood with his hands folded in the lamplight, in forest green, and the smile arrived on his face a breath before she reached him.

"Princess. How good to see you restored."

"I was not ill, Minister."

"No? The household said you were unwell. I was deeply concerned." He bowed. "I am told you have been to the eastern villages. A charitable errand. The people will speak of it for years."

"I hope so."

"I hope so too. Charity is a fine thing in a princess. It is always welcome." He tilted his head. "And a slightly dangerous one, in a season like this. It attracts attention."

"Whose?"

"Everyone's. Particularly that of a prince who has crossed the sea to ask for your hand." He let the sentence hang, and she saw the pleasure in it. "You will have heard. It is a very happy alliance. Samudrapuram has the finest fleet on this coast. Their grain would feed the east for a year."

"I have heard."

"His Grace will announce it formally. In three days." The smile widened a shade. "I had hoped to tell you first."

"You are very kind."

"I am your servant." He bowed again, and made to go. Then he paused, as though it had only just occurred to him. "A small thing. My steward Sundar has gone home. His mother is ill. I mention it only in case you had cause to speak to him."

"I had no cause."

"Of course not."

"I hope she recovers."

"I will tell him so." His eyes rested on hers for exactly one breath longer than was comfortable. "She is, I believe, very near the end. Good night, Princess."

He went away down the passage. She stood looking after him. Sundar's mother had been dead for three years. Ratnan had carried rice to her burning.

Either the minister did not know it. Or he had said it for her to hear.

## III

Nila was in her room when she came up. She was standing at the window with her back to the door. She did not turn.

"You were gone nearly two nights," she said. "You said one."

"I said I would be back soon."

"You said you were going upriver to look at a stone." Nila turned. In the light of the single lamp her face was pale and composed. There was no sign of the teasing girl. "I have lain awake. I have counted the roads. There was a man at the south gate asking for you. Four times. He said he had a gift."

"What kind of gift?"

"He did not say. I told him you were unwell." She came and sat on the bed's edge. "I did not like him. He had a very smooth voice and a grey cloth. I know your guard's names, Vaanathi, and I do not know this man's."

"Did you tell my father?"

"I told Ratnan. He told the emperor." A flicker of humour crossed her face. "He was very brave about it. He only fell over twice."

Vaanathi took off her sandals. Her feet ached. She set the coral bead on the table beside her bed, where it lay like a small warm eye.

"I have something to ask you," she said. "It is odd. Do not laugh."

"I never laugh at odd things. I save it for sensible ones."

"In the council hall there was an empty seat. A pale one. Pearl-white. It was dusty. Nobody had sat in it for a generation." She hesitated. "They did not say whose it was. I think it was the moon's."

Nila's face did not change. But her hands, in her lap, had gone absolutely still.

"I see," she said.

"They said she stopped answering. They thought she was weary of them. And the dark moons have been dark. Truly dark. Lohitan said that when he stands on his hill, he cannot see." She hesitated. "Do you know anything about that?"

"About the moon?"

"About what happens to the world, when she is not there."

For a time, Nila did not speak. A moth had come in at the window and was beating itself softly against the lamp glass. She watched it.

"I know that the tide goes slack," she said at last. "I know that the sea does not rise as high. I know that women who are carrying children sleep badly, and that the jasmine blooms late. I know that the old farmers say the seed does not wake well." She smiled a little. It was a brittle smile. "I am a scribe's niece, Vaanathi. I only know what the farmers say."

"I did not mean to..."

"No. I am glad you asked." Nila rose and went back to the window. "I would only say one thing. If she is not answering, it may be that she has a reason. Even gods do not always leave a room because they are angry."

"Why else?"

"Because they wish to see what the room does without them." She looked out into the dark. "Go to bed. You look like a wrung cloth."

"I cannot sleep."

"Then lie down and look at the ceiling. It is very restful." She did not turn round. "I will sit here for a while. I like the moths."

## IV

She did not lie down. She lay on top of the covers for an hour, staring at the ceiling, and heard the palace go quiet round her. A watchman called the third watch. A dog barked in the lower court. And then she got up, put on a dark wrap, and went out through the women's wing and down the back stair into the garden.

It was an old garden, at the east end of the palace, long unfashionable. A square pond lay in the middle, covered with lotus pads. Jasmine grew along the walls in a tangle of white. An ancient banyan stood in one corner, its aerial roots hanging down in curtains. Under it was a stone bench.

Sancharan was sitting on the bench.

He did not look surprised. He had a way of looking as if he had arrived earlier than you and found the place agreeable.

"You also cannot sleep," she said.

"I do not sleep. It is nothing to do with tonight."

"Do you mind if I sit?"

"It is your garden."

"It is your bench. I think you have been sitting on it a long time." She sat down at the far end. The stone was cool under her hand. "I had a conversation with a friend. It was odd. I am still turning it over."

"About what?"

"About whether you can know a person your whole life and be told, at the end, that you have only seen one side."

"Ah." He did not ask who. "That is a very old thing to learn. I am surprised you have learned it so young."

"I am twenty-four."

"As I said."

She laughed under her breath. It was dark. There was no moon. A few stars showed between the banyan's leaves. The jasmine smelled so sweet it was almost too much.

"I want to ask you again," she said. "Not in the corridor. Not in the fortress. Here. Quietly. You promised to tell me about the night. The night the wheels broke."

"I did."

"Will you?"

He was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, it was with great care, like a man putting his weight on a bridge.

"Tomorrow," he said. "I will tell you after the tower."

"Why the tower?"

"Because I do not trust that old man, and I do not wish to go to him with my head full of the past." He turned his hands over on his knees. "It is not a story I tell well. I would like to be able to stand up straight afterwards."

"After the tower."

"After. I give you my word."

She did not push. She sat with her hands in her lap and breathed in the jasmine. After a while she put out one hand and picked a bud from the vine that grew along the bench's back. It was small and pale and tightly closed, the tip just touched with green.

She held it a moment. Then she did something she could not afterwards explain. She turned and held out her open hand towards him, palm up, with the bud lying in the middle of it.

"Take it," she said.

"I cannot."

"You can. It is only a flower. I am not asking you to take my hand."

He looked at the bud. She watched his face. He did not move for so long that she thought he would refuse. Then, very slowly, as though it cost him something real, he held out his own hand and turned it palm up beneath hers.

She tipped her hand. The bud rolled across the gap and landed in his palm.

It lay there.

Neither of them breathed.

The bud was small and pale on the dark skin of his hand. It did not wilt. It did not brown at the edges. It did not do anything at all. It was only a jasmine bud, lying on a man's palm, in the starlight.

"Four breaths," said Sancharan, very low.

"Is it all right?"

"I do not know." His fingers had curled just a little, the way you hold a thing you are afraid of dropping. "I cannot feel the end of it. I can feel only that it is a flower. It is very small. It is very..." He stopped. He swallowed. "It is very alive."

"Perhaps it is not you."

"Perhaps." He looked at her, and his eyes were dark and strange. "Perhaps it is only a very strong flower."

"Jasmine blooms at night."

"So I am told."

"My friend says it grows late, when there is no moon."

"Does she?"

"She says the farmers say so."

He said nothing. But a small, startled line appeared between his brows, like a man who has begun to hear something at the edge of a sentence, and does not yet know what.

## V

The footstep was so soft that she almost missed it.

It came from the far side of the pond. It was a quick, light step, the step of a man who knew exactly where he was going. It did not shuffle. It did not drag.

She raised her hand. Sancharan was already still.

A figure had come out of the shadow of the east wall. It carried a small shuttered lamp, held low, so that only a thread of light fell on the path. The figure was tall. It walked upright, with long easy strides. It was clad in a dull brown wrap. Its grey hair hung loose to its shoulders, and its short beard glimmered white.

Vaanathi's mouth went dry.

"It is a ghost," she whispered.

"It is a man," said Sancharan.

"It cannot be. He..." She caught her breath. "He walks like a soldier."

"Yes."

"He has not left the tower in nine years."

"So he says."

The figure passed the edge of the pond. It did not look round. It crossed the path in front of the banyan, no more than ten paces from where they sat in the shadow of the roots, and she saw its face in the thin yellow light.

It was Viyamban.

It was the same deep-lined face, the same heavy brows, the same mild large eyes. But the mildness was gone. The face was hard, and watchful, and clear. The mouth was set like a trap. He looked ten years younger. He looked like a man who had spent a lifetime holding something heavy and had put it down for an hour.

He went to the foot of an old jasmine bush, close to the bench, and knelt. She heard the faint scrape of a trowel. He dug for a moment, quickly, and drew out a small clay jar with a stopper of wax. He opened it. He put something inside, a folded cloth, small and neat. He stoppered it again and returned it to the earth, patting the soil flat with the palm of his hand.

Then he rose, brushed off his knees, and glanced once round the garden.

Vaanathi did not breathe.

His eyes went over the banyan, the bench, the roots. They went over her. They went over the man at her side. For one long frozen instant she was sure that he had seen them. Then the shuttered lamp dipped, and the thread of light went out. A faint shuffle of sandals sounded on the path, receding.

She heard his voice, once, from a long way off. It was a dry whisper, and it was nearly gentle.

"Sleep well, little princess."

Then he was gone.

She found that her hands were shaking. She pressed them between her knees.

"He saw us."

"I think so."

"Then why did he not..."

"Because he does not care," said Sancharan. His voice was very level. "That is what troubles me. A man who has been caught does not say *sleep well.*"

"Is he going to kill me?"

"Not tonight." He had stood. He had gone to the bush, already on his knees. "He said it for effect. He wants us to dig."

"Then we should not."

"I think we must."

He dug with his hands. The earth was loose and damp. It took him only a moment. He drew up the jar, wiped it on his sleeve, and cracked the wax seal with his thumbnail. She heard it break.

Inside the jar lay a folded cloth. He lifted it out and held it to the thin starlight.

It was cotton. It was the twin of the one that had been brought to her door. But it was not a letter. It was not even a clean page. It was covered with writing from corner to corner, tiny and close, in dark ink.

She took it from him. Her fingers were stiff. She read the first line.

*My dear girl.*

The second line was the same. And the third. And the fourth. They ran down the cloth in rows. *My dear girl. My dear girl. My dear girl.* Each one was in Smaran's hand. Each was a little better than the one before. She followed them down. The slant grew surer. The loops grew rounder. The final lines were so perfect that she could have wept.

Near the bottom there were two more words. They were written in a different hand, in a looser and a gentler script, as if the writer had set down his pen and spoken half aloud.

*Forgive me.*

She held the cloth in both hands. She did not know that she had begun to cry until she tasted salt.

"It was him," she said. "All of it. The cloth. The poison. The..." She could not finish. "He wept for his friend."

"I think he did."

"How could he?"

"I do not know." Sancharan's voice was gentle. "I have seen it before. A man who kills what he loves, and mourns it honestly, and would do it again."

"I liked him."

"I know."

"He gave me water in a clay cup."

"I know, Vaanathi."

She wiped her face with her wrist. Then she folded the cloth, very small, and put it into her sash. She stood up. She was not shaking any more. Something quite cold and clear had settled in her chest.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"Tomorrow we go to the tower."

"Not tomorrow. Not at dawn, when he expects us."

"At noon, then."

"At noon." She turned and looked at the dark path where the old man had gone. "I would like him to see me in daylight."
