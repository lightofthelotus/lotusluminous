## I

The floor was pale and smooth and faintly bright. It held the light of the sky above, but gave nothing back. When Vaanathi looked down, she saw no reflection of herself. She saw only the stone, going on and on, with a soft glow like the inside of a shell.

She was standing in a courtyard. It was wide enough to hold a village. Behind her the door still stood, a plain dark rectangle in the open air. It was shut.

Iruli shifted on her shoulder. She was very quiet.

The sky was the same violet she had glimpsed from the well. It moved slowly, folding and unfolding. Stars hung in it in clear patterns. They were not scattered the way stars are on earth. They had been placed, one at a time, with a patient hand, and the arrangement felt like a sentence she could nearly read.

Beyond the courtyard rose walls. They were high and plain and the colour of old bone. They did not look built. They looked grown, as a cliff is grown. There were no windows. There were no flags. At the far end stood an arch as tall as a temple tower, and through it she could see another court, and another beyond that, receding into pale light.

There was no sound at all. Not a wind. Not a footstep. Not her own heartbeat, though she could feel it knocking against her ribs.

It was not a silence of emptiness. It was the silence of a very large room in which many people have just stopped talking. She felt it on her skin, a kind of attention. The walls seemed to lean, very slightly, to listen.

"Well," she said. Her voice came out small. "That is a great deal of sky."

Iruli said nothing.

At the centre of the courtyard stood a fountain. It was a wide round basin, carved from the same pale stone, with a slender column rising from its middle. Water was falling from the top of the column. She watched it for some time before she understood what was strange. It fell upward. It leapt from the basin, climbed the column in a thin glittering rope, and vanished at the top without a sound.

Beside the fountain stood a stone bench.

It was plain, long, and low, worn as smooth as a river pebble. It looked as though a great many people had sat on it. She was suddenly, absurdly tired. She had been climbing and descending and hiding in alcoves for three days. She crossed to the bench and sat.

The stone was neither warm nor cold. It was only quiet.

"I should not be here," she said to the crow. "I know that. I have known it since the sixth landing."

Iruli turned her head and looked at her. It was a long, patient, unhurried look. Vaanathi had the sudden strong impression that the bird was waiting for her to finish being foolish.

"I know," she said. "I am going. In a moment."

She looked at her left hand. The cut from the iron ring had stopped bleeding. A dark line ran across the palm, and in the middle of it a single drop had gathered, round and bright. As she watched, it swelled, trembled, and fell.

It struck the bench between her fingers.

Nothing happened. Then something did.

## II

She felt it before she saw it. A low shiver ran through the stone, a vibration she felt in her teeth. She leapt up. The place where the blood had fallen was dark. Around it, fine lines had begun to spread, thin as hairs, branching outwards. The surface of the bench crazed. It went dull. A flake of stone lifted and fell away. Beneath it, the grain was grey and pitted.

It was ageing. She could see it happen. In a dozen breaths the bench had gone from a smooth, quiet seat to a thing that looked as if it had lain out in rain for a thousand years. A crack ran down the middle. One end crumbled and slid to the floor in a soft pile of grit.

Her hand flew to her mouth.

Iruli screamed.

It was a harsh, high cry, nothing like the dry clicks she usually made. She flung herself off Vaanathi's shoulder and beat upward into the violet sky. The cry rang off the pale walls and came back changed.

"What did I..." Vaanathi began.

"Stand still."

The voice was behind her. It was not loud. It was deep and dry and slow, like a stone being moved across stone. She turned.

Three figures stood at the arch.

They were tall. They were taller than any man, and they had the shape of men, but they had been made, not born. Their bodies were of the same pale stone as the floor, smooth and unmarked. Their faces were blank ovals, with no eyes, no mouth, no nose. Each held a staff as long as a spear, topped with a plain round knob. They did not walk. They glided over the floor, and the floor did not echo.

"Stand still," the first said again.

She stood still.

"Name."

"Vaanathi." Her voice shook. She steadied it. "Daughter of Anantaraja. Of Anantapuram."

"Blood."

"I do not understand."

"You have spilled blood on the floor of this place. We ask its line."

"Anantha. The house of Anantha."

The three figures were silent. It was a long silence, and it was not comfortable. She felt as though she were being weighed against something very large.

"The line of Anantha has crossed the threshold," said the second figure. Its voice was higher than the first, and thinner, and had the same slow grind in it. "No Anantha has come here since the Accord was sealed."

"The Accord?"

"The count begins."

"What count? I do not know what you mean. I came by accident. A door opened. I..." She was talking too fast. She made herself stop. "I am a guest. I mean no harm."

"Harm has been done," said the third. It lifted its staff. The round knob caught the violet light and glowed faintly. "The blood has entered. The sleeper stirs."

"What sleeper?"

"That is not yours to ask."

"I am asking."

"You are no longer asked."

The three staffs came down together. They pointed at her chest. She had the sense, quite clear and calm, that she was about to die in a place with no name, for a reason she did not know, and that nobody would ever find the body.

She thought, quite distinctly, *I should have listened to him.*

Then the whole courtyard shook.

## III

It began as a groan, far off, a thin high crack. It grew. A second crack answered it from a different direction, and a third. The pale stone floor shivered and rippled as water does when a heavy object is dropped into it. The sentinels staggered. Their staffs swung wide.

At the door in the open air, the iron bands began to glow. They went from dull grey to red, then white, then a blaze so bright that Vaanathi flung up her arm to shield her face. The wood split down the middle with a sound like a branch breaking under snow. The door burst inward in a spray of splinters.

Sancharan came through it.

He did not walk. He fell, and caught himself, and staggered upright. His face was grey and streaming with sweat. His hands, flung out in front of him, were bleeding at every knuckle. His dark hair was plastered to his temples. He looked like a man who had carried a house on his back up a long hill.

He looked at her. He saw the staffs. His face changed.

It was not anger. It was something colder and much more still. The air in the courtyard went tight, and Vaanathi felt her own breath stop in her chest. The sentinels stopped too, as if a hand had closed around each of them.

"Lower them," said Sancharan.

It was a quiet voice. It was the same low voice she knew. But it carried to every corner of the vast court, and it did not ask.

The three staffs did not move. Then, slowly, one by one, they dipped. The knobs touched the floor.

"Walker," said the first sentinel. Its voice had changed. It had lost its grinding edge. It sounded almost uncertain. "You broke the ward."

"I did."

"It is forbidden."

"It was a poor ward. It let a child through and kept me out." He took a step forward. His legs shook. "If you wish to complain, complain. I will answer to the Council, and to nobody else."

"The Council will be told."

"I should hope so."

"The blood has entered, Walker. You know what follows."

"I know what follows." He did not look at the sentinels. He was looking at Vaanathi. "I also know who opened the door. It was not her. She was only the one who bled."

The first sentinel was silent for a time.

"The count begins," it said at last. "We cannot stop it."

"I am aware."

"Then we will stand down."

The three pale figures drew back. They did not bow. They stepped sideways, one at a time, and the stone under their feet closed over the place where they had been, as if they had never been there.

The courtyard was empty again. The fountain still ran upward. The ruined bench lay in its heap of grit, and the stones were quiet.

Vaanathi took one step towards him. Her legs gave way.

## IV

He caught her before she hit the floor. It was done so quickly that she could not have said how. One moment she was falling. The next she was in his arms, held hard against his chest, with his heartbeat very loud against her ear. It was slow and heavy, like a bell in a deep well.

"I am all right," she said into his shoulder. "I am only a little dizzy."

"You are not all right."

"I am not hurt."

"You are shaking. You went through a door I told you not to enter, and you are standing in the one place in the world you must not be. I would say you are a good deal worse than not all right." His voice was rough. "Do you understand what you did?"

"I did not do anything. I sat on a bench."

"You bled on it."

"It was an accident."

"It always is."

He let her go. It took a visible effort. He held her at arm's length with both hands on her shoulders, and looked at her face. She saw, close up, what the ward had cost him. Fine white lines like cracks ran across the back of both hands. His eyes were red-rimmed. For a moment he looked like a very old man.

"You said you would be back before I finished shouting," he said.

"You had not finished."

"No." A strange sound came out of him. It was half a laugh and half something else. "No, I had not."

"I am sorry."

"Do not be. I should have stopped you." He drew a breath and let it out. "I did not. I stood on a stair and let you walk into a fortress that has not seen a mortal in an age. I let you, because I could not stop you without taking your will away. And I did not want to."

"I would not have forgiven you."

"I know." He looked down at her hand. "That is the whole of it."

The cut on her palm had begun to bleed again. A thin red thread ran down her wrist. He caught her hand in both of his and turned it over. His fingers were warm and rough. They trembled very slightly.

"Hold still."

He tore a strip from the cloth that hung over his shoulder. It was grey-blue and fine, the uttariyam he always wore. He wound it round her palm, firmly, once and twice and three times. She watched his hands. They moved with great care. They moved as though he were handling something that could break, and had broken once before.

He knotted the cloth. He did not let go.

For a long moment they stood like that, in the empty violet light, with her hand held in both of his.

"I should not have done that," he said.

"You did it very well."

"I mean the rest." He released her hand, one finger at a time. "I should not have held you. I should not hold you now. I have told you, Vaanathi. What I touch, ends."

"It did not end."

"Not today."

"And the bench?"

"The bench is not you." He turned away and looked at the heap of grit. His shoulders were rigid. "The bench is what happens when your house's blood meets this place. It is not a thing you did. It is a thing that was always going to happen, once someone of your line walked in."

"Then it is not my fault."

"No. It is mine. I am the one who let the door open." He closed his eyes. "I have not stood here in a long time. I find I do not like it."

## V

Iruli came down out of the violet sky, then, and dropped onto Sancharan's wrist. She was shaking. She pecked once at his bleeding knuckle, hard, and then bent her head and held it against his hand. He stood very still and let her. Vaanathi looked away. It seemed a private thing, the way you look away when someone you respect is crying.

"She was angry with me," he said, after a while. "She told me what she thought on the way in. At length."

"What did she say?"

"That I am a fool. I did not argue."

They did not leave at once. She asked to see the great chamber, and for a moment she thought he would refuse. Then he said, "Quickly," and led her through the arch.

The inner court was smaller. A round hall opened from it. It had no roof. The violet sky stood over it like a bowl. In the middle of the floor lay a wheel inlaid in dark stone, nine spokes wide, each spoke ending in a single great seat.

The seats were thrones, or the nearest thing to them. They were old, as old as the floor. They were made of different stones. One was dull red, rough as laterite. One was pale as a pearl. One was gold, one a clear green, one the warm cream of ivory. They were arranged in a ring, each facing the centre, and every one was worn smooth by long sitting.

She looked at the inlaid wheel under her feet. It was the same wheel, the same one she had seen on the wall of the tower and in the margin of the ledger. But here it was whole, except at the bottom. At the place where the sixth hour would be, the dark stone stopped in a clean wedge-shaped gap. Nothing filled it. It was like looking at the socket of a missing tooth.

A very thin thread of grey was rising out of that gap. It was no thicker than a strand of silk. It climbed a hand's width into the air, wavered, and thinned to nothing. Then another began behind it.

"Do not go near that," said Sancharan.

"What is it?"

"A draught." He said it too quickly. "From a place that should be shut."

She did not believe him. But she stepped back, and she noticed that he stood between her and the gap until she had.

She counted nine. Her breath caught.

One seat was set a little apart. It was grey-blue, plain and dark, with no ornament. Its arms were cracked down the middle, as if something had struck them very hard. A thin fissure ran along the floor from its foot, towards the centre of the wheel.

She looked at it, and then at him. He was not looking at the seat. He was looking at the floor in front of it, at nothing. His face was empty.

"This is where you sit," she said.

"I did once." He did not turn. "I have not sat there in a very long time."

"Who are you?"

It was the second time she had asked. It came out softer than the first.

For a moment she thought he would answer. His mouth opened. His hand, at his side, curled and uncurled. She saw him fight something and lose, and win again.

"Not here," he said. "Not in this place. When we are out. When you have slept."

"You promise?"

"I promise."

She had no right to ask for more. She followed him back through the arches, past the dead bench, to the place where the door had stood. Only the frame was left. It was a ragged arch of splintered wood, with the dark well-shaft showing through.

At the threshold she stopped. She looked back one last time.

The fountain was still running upward. The ruined bench lay where it had fallen. And on the far side of the court, in the shadow of the arch, the three pale sentinels stood motionless, with their staffs planted in the floor. They had no faces. But she was sure, without knowing why, that they were watching her.

"The count has begun," the first said. Its voice came across the court, flat and clear, and seemed to go on ringing long after it had stopped.

She stepped through the broken door. Sancharan came after, and his hand closed on her shoulder.

Far above them, in the world she had left, a drop of water fell from the roof of the stepwell into the black pool. The ripple that followed did not spread outward. It spread inward, and was gone.
