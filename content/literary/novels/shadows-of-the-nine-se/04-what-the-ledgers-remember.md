## I

![Smaran's Memorial](02_01_SmaranMemorial.jpg)

They held the tribute at midmorning, when the light in the great courtyard was clean and unshadowed. It gave nothing away.

There was no pyre lit for show, no procession of grief performed for an audience. Anantaraja had never had patience for mourning as spectacle, and Vaanathi had inherited enough of that impatience to be grateful for it now. What there was instead was simple, and simple enough to nearly undo her anyway. Smaran's writing table had been carried out from the archive and set beneath the old banyan at the courtyard's heart. His brushes lay across it in the order he had always kept them. Above it burned a plain brass lamp, left to burn down on its own. He had once told her, years ago, half joking, that a scholar's proper memorial was not incense but ink that kept being used after he was gone.

The court filed past in the loose, murmuring order such things always took. Ratnan wept without shame, and Vaanathi found she respected that more than she expected to. Charudatta offered exactly as much grief as the occasion required, and not a breath more. He moved on to talk with the treasury steward before the lamp had burned even an inch. Her mother stood beside her for a while, said nothing, and pressed Vaanathi's hand once before letting go. Manasa had never needed words for the things that mattered. Some days that was a great comfort. Other days it was a great frustration. Today, Vaanathi could not decide which.

Sancharan stood near the back, apart from the court proper. He held himself like a man who understood he had not yet earned the right to grieve in the front rows of anyone's funeral. Vaanathi noticed this. She noticed, too, that he did not perform stillness the way courtiers performed it, the studied, watchful blankness of people arranging their faces for whoever might be looking. His stillness looked, if anything, tired. Old. As if grief were a language he had been fluent in for longer than she had been alive, and had simply stopped finding new ways to wear it.

She disliked how much that observation cost her to make.

Ratnan found her near the lamp before the court had fully dispersed. His eyes were still red-rimmed. He kept turning his hands over each other, an anxious habit he had never quite grown out of since his under-steward days. "He would have hated all this fuss, Your Highness," he said, with the ghost of a laugh that didn't reach his eyes. "He always said a proper scholar's memorial was the next reader who opened his books and found them still in order."

"Then we will have to keep them in order," Vaanathi said, "for as long as it takes someone to earn the right to disorder them again."

It was, she thought, the truest thing she had said all week. It cost her more than she let show. She had known Smaran since she was old enough to sit cross-legged without her mother's ladies scolding her posture. She had spent whole humid afternoons in that archive, tracing her own family's ink back through the generations, while he corrected her grammar without ever once making her feel small for needing it. Grief, she was discovering, did not arrive all at once, the way the court's dramatic tellings of it always suggested. It came in pieces, a brass lamp left to burn, a steward's ruined composure, the plain, ordinary cruelty of a room that still smelled like the person who was no longer in it.

She was still turning that thought over, watching the light thin toward midday, when she noticed her father crossing the courtyard toward her. He walked with the unhurried, deliberate gait he used only when he had already decided exactly what he meant to say.

***

![Anantaraja and Vaanathi Meet](02_02_KingVaanathiMeet.jpg)


It was her father who found her afterward, in the shade at the courtyard's edge. She was surprised enough that she nearly missed the first half of what he said.

"The southern scholar has offered to catalogue what remains of the archive," Anantaraja said. He watched the last of the court disperse rather than looking at her directly, she had long since learned this was simply how he delivered anything that mattered to him. "The genealogical volumes, the border correspondence. Smaran kept no second hand trained to that work, and I will not have thirty years of record left to rot because we buried the one man who understood their order."

"A generous offer," Vaanathi said carefully, "from a man who arrived the same evening the last person who understood that order died."

"Yes." Her father's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Which is precisely why you will oversee him."

She turned to look at him properly then. "Father,"

"You noticed the missing page," he said, still not looking at her, in the same flat tone he might have used to note that it looked like rain. "Did you imagine I would not?"

Vaanathi said nothing. It was, she reflected, extraordinarily inconvenient to be caught being clever by the one person in the palace she had never quite managed to be clever in front of.

"I cannot chase a rumour through my own court without handing every frightened minister in it a reason to whisper louder," Anantaraja went on. "But I can set my daughter to watch a stranger work, in the ordinary course of ordinary business, and no one will think it anything but prudence." Now he did look at her. There was something in it, not quite warmth, her father did not deal easily in warmth, but a kind of grave attention. She understood, with a small unexpected ache, that this was the closest thing to trust he knew how to offer aloud. "Watch him. Watch the work. Tell me what you find, and tell me only me."

"And if he asks why the emperor's own daughter has appointed herself his supervisor?"

"Then you will tell him the truth," her father said. "That this house does not hand its dead's secrets to strangers without first learning whether the stranger can be trusted with them. I imagine a scholar of his evident quality will find that a perfectly reasonable policy. And if he does not, that alone will tell us something worth knowing."

"And if what I find is nothing?"

"Then you will have spent a week cataloguing ledgers instead of arguing with Charudatta about embankments," her father said, "and I imagine you will survive the disappointment."

It was, she would think later, the nearest her father had ever come to making a joke expressly for her benefit. Walking back into the palace's cool shadowed halls, she did not know whether to feel grateful for the assignment, or resentful of how neatly it delivered her straight into the company of the one man in Anantapura she had sworn, not two days prior, to keep at the length of a very long spear.

## II

![Vaanathi Enters Archive Room](02_03_ArchiveRoom_Entry.jpg)

The archive, reopened under seal-breaking that Anantaraja himself authorised, smelled the way it always had, neem oil, dry palm-leaf, the particular dry stillness of a room built to outlast the people who used it. Someone had righted whatever ittle furnitures were there. No one had thought to remove the faint discolouration on the floorboards near the writing table. Vaanathi found she could not quite make herself look at it directly, so she didn't, and set to work instead pretending the room contained nothing but books.

Sancharan was already there when she arrived, of course he was, she thought, with more irritation than the fact strictly warranted. He sat cross-legged on the floor, three volumes open around him in a neat fan, and the crow perched with proprietary comfort on the shelf above his head, one dark eye tracking Vaanathi's entrance with what could only be described as interest.

"Your Highness." He did not rise, which she noted, and which she found, against every instinct she had cultivated since childhood to expect deference, almost refreshing. "I was told I would have an overseer. I confess I expected someone,"

"Elder?" Vaanathi interrupted.

"No, I was about to say someone," Sancharan continued.

"Male, and of higher rank?" Vaanathi interrupted once more.

",considerably less inclined to have already accused me of murder," Sancharan finally finished, with the air of a man reclaiming a sentence by force.

"I accused you of convenient timing," Vaanathi said, settling herself onto the floor at a distance she told herself was purely practical. "There's a difference."

"A narrow one."

"Narrow differences are still differences." She reached for the nearest open volume, more to have something to do with her hands than out of any real intention. It was the border correspondence, decades of treaty language in three dead clerks' handwriting, nothing that should have mattered to anyone. "What are you looking for, scholar, that requires this particular room and no other in the empire?"

"Precedent," he said, without looking up. "The southern collegium disputes a boundary marker with one of your father's border lords. I was sent to find whichever century first drew the line in ink, so the argument can be settled with a document instead of a war." He paused, just long enough to be deliberate. "It is, I promise you, a very dull errand. I did not expect to arrive and find a man dead behind a locked door for my trouble."

*He says it so easily*, Vaanathi thought, watching him. *As though the ease costs him nothing.* Then, catching herself mid-thought, she noticed something else. His hands, moving across the page, were not easy at all, steady, yes, but with the specific careful steadiness of a man deliberately not letting them be anything else.

She did not know, yet, what to do with noticing that.

***

They worked in something that was not quite silence and not quite conversation for the better part of an hour. It was the particular rhythm two wary people fall into when neither wants to be the one who speaks first, and both keep finding themselves speaking anyway. He corrected her once, mildly, on the history of a treaty seal she'd misdated by two reigns. She corrected him twice, less mildly, on points of court protocol he seemed to have learned entirely from books, never once from an actual room full of people. Somewhere in the second hour, without either of them quite marking the moment it happened, the corrections stopped sounding like combat. They started sounding, faintly, like something closer to sport.

"You read old Prakrit as though it were your mother tongue," she said, watching him move through a border correspondence three centuries dense, with the ease of a man reading a shopping list. "The southern collegium must keep remarkable teachers."

"They kept adequate ones. I had a great deal of time to practise." Something flickered behind his eyes, there and gone before she could name it. "Old languages are patient. They do not mind being neglected for years and then returned to. People are considerably less forgiving."

"Is that meant to be wisdom, or complaint?"

"With me," he said, "it is rarely easy to tell the difference."

She laughed before she could stop herself, a short, surprised sound, gone as quickly as it came. She saw him hear it. Something in his face eased very slightly at the sound of it, as though he had not expected to earn that from her, and did not quite know what to do with having earned it anyway.

"Tell me something true, then," she said, testing him, the way she tested most things she did not yet trust. "Not scholarship. Something about you."

He was quiet long enough that she thought he might simply decline, the way he had declined so much else since his arrival. "I have buried a great many friends," he said finally, without looking up from the page. "More than any one life should reasonably allow. I find I no longer know how to grieve them properly, in the moment they are lost, only afterward, in pieces, at inconvenient hours, when I am meant to be thinking of something else entirely." He paused. "That is true. I do not offer it often."

Vaanathi did not know what to do with the honesty of it. It sat so strangely against the guardedness of everything else he'd given her. "That is not the answer of a man who has spent his life reading treaties."

"No," Sancharan agreed. "It isn't."

He did not explain further, and she did not press. Some instinct, sharper than her curiosity, told her that pressing now would only close a door she had just felt come open a hand's width. She returned to her own volume instead. If she read the next several pages without absorbing a single word of them, she kept that fact strictly to herself.

It was reaching for the same shelf, some while later, that undid the fragile calm they had built.

The genealogical volumes sat highest, on the shelf Smaran alone had ever needed a stool to reach. Everyone else in the palace simply avoided disturbing it. Vaanathi rose to retrieve the damaged one, the one with its smoothed-over tear, the one she had come back to see with her own eyes a dozen times since the night she'd first found it wanting. At the exact moment, Sancharan, apparently following the same instinct by an entirely separate path, reached for it too.

Their hands did not touch. They came close enough that not touching required a small, deliberate adjustment from both of them, a fraction of a second where each simply stopped. In that fraction, Vaanathi was unhelpfully, thoroughly aware of the warmth radiating off his skin, though nothing had actually met. She noticed, too, that he had gone very still, in a way that was different from his usual stillness, not guarded, for once, just briefly, entirely undefended.

![A Moment Shared in the Archive Room](02_04_Archiveroom_Accident.jpg)

"You first," he said, and withdrew his hand. His voice came out rougher than the moment strictly required.

"Thank you," Vaanathi said, and heard her own voice do something similarly unhelpful. She took the volume down with considerably more concentration than the act deserved.

She opened it to the torn page's absence, the smoothed seam she now knew as well as a scar on her own hand. It was only because she was looking so closely, in that particular light, at that particular angle, that she caught it: pressed faintly into the binding's inner edge, nearly invisible unless the page lay flat and the light fell just so, a small mark. Not ink. Pressed in, the way a seal presses wax, a narrow ring, broken at one point by a thin dark wedge.

The same mark. Smaller here, cruder, as if made in haste rather than with a wax-seal's careful craft. But unmistakably the same shape she had found pressed into the archive door three nights ago.

She went very still. Beside her, close enough that he could not have missed her stillness, Sancharan went stiller still. For one unguarded instant, before he caught himself, something crossed his face that was not surprise. It was recognition.

"You know this mark," Vaanathi said. It was not a question.

"I have seen its shape before," he said carefully, in the voice of a man choosing each word the way one chooses a stone to cross a river, testing its weight before trusting it. "In older records. It is not a mark this court's ledgers should carry."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only one I have that would not be a lie," Sancharan said. There was something in the way he said it, not evasive, exactly, but weighted, as though the truth he was withholding cost him something to withhold. It made her want, with an intensity that alarmed her, to keep pressing until he set it down.

She did not press. Not yet. She filed it instead, the way she had learned to file everything that mattered, in the quiet locked room of her own mind where she kept the things she intended to return to.

"Then I will simply have to find my own answer," she said, closing the volume with more care than her frustration wanted to allow.

![The Symbol on the Manuscript](02_05_SymbolManuscript.jpg)


"I imagine you will," Sancharan said. Beneath the caution was something that might almost have been admiration, and Vaanathi found she did not entirely hate hearing it.

## III

He did not sleep well that night, though sleep had never been a thing his body strictly required. Iruli found him well past the second watch, sitting on the low wall above the stable roofline, looking at nothing in particular with the specific unfocused attention of a mind occupied elsewhere.

*You are thinking about the ledger,* she said, settling beside him.

"I am thinking about the mark in the ledger."

*You are thinking about her hand not touching yours,* Iruli said, with the serene confidence of a creature who had known him across several centuries, and had stopped pretending not to notice things for his comfort sometime during the second one. *I was there. I saw the whole undignified business. You went stiff as a fence post and she went red as a struck bell and neither of you said a single honest word about it.*

"There was nothing to say."

*There was everything to say. You simply chose, as you generally do, to say none of it.* She clicked her beak, not unkindly. *You told her something true today, though. I heard it. About the burying of friends. That is not nothing, for you.*

"It was a small truth. Small enough to be safe."

*Was it safe?* Iruli tilted her head. *You have not told a mortal anything true about yourself in longer than I care to count. Today you handed a princess a piece of it over a border ledger, entirely unprompted. That does not sound like a man being careful to me. That sounds like a man who has simply forgotten, for one unguarded hour, that he was supposed to be.*

He did not answer that directly. "You could tell her, you know," she went on, more gently now. "Not everything. Enough."

"Enough would become everything within the hour. You know how these things unravel, once the first thread is pulled." He rubbed a hand across his face, an old human gesture he had picked up so many centuries ago he no longer remembered whose it had originally been. "And even if it did not, what precisely would I be offering her, Iruli? A name that has made grown men cross themselves in the street. A history longer than her family's whole dynasty, most of it spent watching people flinch. She looked at me today like I might, for once, simply be a man. I am not eager to watch that look leave her face."

*You are afraid,* Iruli said, with the particular gentleness she reserved for the rare occasions she meant to actually wound him, *that she will be no different from all the others. And you are more afraid, I think, that she will be entirely different, and you will have to decide what to do with that.*

He did not answer this, because there was no answer to it that did not concede the point. He had spent a very long time being a creature who did not concede points, even to himself, even in the dark, even to a bird who had earned, several times over, the right to make him.

"The mark," he said instead, redirecting them both toward safer ground. "You know what it means as well as I do."

*I know what it usually means,* Iruli said, allowing the redirection, though her tone made clear she was only lending it, not forgetting the rest. *A seal, broken or hidden, come loose from wherever it was meant to rest. Someone is looking for it. Someone else may already have found it. And an old man who spent thirty years guarding the one shelf in this palace where such a thing might have been recorded is dead behind a door that should not have let anyone in or out.*

"Then it has begun again."

*It never entirely stops,* Iruli said. *It only waits for someone patient enough to start it moving.* She turned her head, studying him with an expression that, on a human face, might have been read as sympathy. *You will have to tell her something, eventually. Not everything. But something. She is not the sort who will be satisfied by silence for very much longer, and I do not think, old friend, that you particularly want her to be.*

He said nothing to that either, which she took, correctly, as agreement he was not yet ready to speak aloud.

## IV

"You're doing the thing again," Nila said, from her customary position curled into the window seat, "where you stare at a wall as though it has personally wronged you."

"The wall has done nothing." Vaanathi did not look away from it. "I am simply thinking."

"About the scholar."

"About the *mark*," Vaanathi corrected, with more force than the correction required. That told Nila, and, if she were honest with herself, told Vaanathi too, precisely how much the correction was worth.

"Of course." Nila's mouth curved, entirely unconvinced. "Tell me about the mark, then. Tell me everything about the mark. Spare no detail. I am riveted."

"You are insufferable."

"I am your oldest friend, which in this palace amounts to nearly the same office." Nila drew her knees up beneath her, settling in with evident enjoyment. "You spent a full hour, by your own account, sitting on an archive floor with a strange and evidently well-read man. And you have said perhaps four words about the actual work you were meant to be doing, and no fewer than forty about the precise quality of his silences. I am merely following the evidence, as you are so fond of doing yourself."

Vaanathi opened her mouth to argue, found she had no argument ready, and closed it again. She suspected that told Nila everything the silence itself hadn't already.

"There is a symbol carved into Smaran's ledger that has no business being there," she said instead, steering them back with more determination than grace, "and the only person in this palace who recognised it refused to tell me what it means."

"And that is the whole of what's troubling you tonight." Nila's voice carried the particular mildness she used when she already knew the answer, and wanted the pleasure of hearing Vaanathi arrive at it herself.

Vaanathi did not rise to it, not immediately. She sat instead on the edge of her own bed, turning over the day in the unhurried, methodical way she turned over most things once the day's noise had finally quieted. Smaran's brushes laid out in their careful order. Her father's rare, grave *watch him, and tell only me*. The warmth she had felt without being touched, standing in a dead man's archive with her hand a finger's width from a stranger's.

"He stood at the back of the tribute," she said finally, "like a man who did not think he'd earned the right to be nearer the front. I keep thinking about that. Most men who arrive somewhere new and find a body waiting for them do not stand at funerals like it is a debt they owe."

"Perhaps he simply has manners."

"Perhaps." Vaanathi did not sound convinced, even to herself. "Or perhaps he has buried more people than manners could account for, and has simply forgotten how to stand anywhere but the back."

Nila was quiet for a moment, watching her with the particular careful attention of someone deciding how much honesty a friend can bear at the end of a long day. "You cared for Smaran," she said gently. "It is allowed, you know, to simply grieve a man you loved, without turning the grief into a puzzle to solve. Not everything you feel tonight has to be about the scholar."

Something in Vaanathi's chest loosened without warning, something she had kept carefully closed since the moment Ratnan came stumbling through the court doors with his face the colour of old rice. To her own considerable irritation, she found her eyes stinging.

"He taught me to read dynastic ledgers like other children read adventure stories," she said, very quietly. "He never once made me feel foolish for asking questions no one else thought worth asking. I keep expecting to walk into that archive and find him there, complaining about the light."

Nila crossed the room without being asked and sat beside her, saying nothing, offering only the particular steady presence that had been, for as long as Vaanathi could remember, worth more than any amount of well-chosen words. They stayed like that a long while, the lamp burning low, the palace settling into its night sounds around them. If Vaanathi's thoughts drifted once or twice back toward a stranger's careful hands and carefully withheld truths, she allowed herself, this once, not to examine why.

## V

It was Iruli, three days later, who found the second mark. She would insist afterward, with some justice, that she had not been looking for it at all, that she had merely been investigating a promising kitchen scrap left unattended near the treasury stores, and had happened, in the course of that investigation, to notice something the treasury's own stewards had walked past a hundred times without seeing.

The old bell that hung above the treasury's inner door was struck once a year, at the closing of the accounts, and otherwise left to gather dust and pigeon interest. At the base of its collar, beneath a rim of verdigris no one had scraped clean in decades, was a mark pressed into the bronze itself, old enough that its edges had softened with a century of weather. A narrow ring. Broken, at one point along its circumference, by a thin dark wedge.

The treasury stewards had gone for their evening meal by the time Vaanathi and Sancharan stood before it. The great vaulted room was lit only by the last grey light through its high, narrow windows, coin-sacks stacked in orderly rows along the walls like a congregation gone very still. Vaanathi held the lamp up until the mark caught the light properly, and felt the same cold settle along the back of her neck that had found her three nights ago in the archive corridor. This time it was not fresh shock. It was something worse, the particular dread of a fear confirming itself.

"This bell has hung in this room since before my grandfather's grandfather held the throne," she said, quietly enough that only Sancharan could hear it. "It is struck once a year, by the same steward's family, in the same manner, for longer than anyone living can account for. No one has touched its base in decades. And yet here it is." She turned to look at him fully, and found, for once, that he did not look away. "Two nights ago it was a door. Now it is a bell older than the dynasty that rules beneath it. This was never about one dead archivist, was it."

Sancharan was silent long enough that she thought, for a moment, he did not intend to answer at all. He studied the mark instead, his expression unreadable in the lamp's uncertain light. When he finally spoke, his voice had lost the careful lightness he had worn all week like a second garment.

"No," he said. "I do not believe it was."

"Then what was it about?"

"I do not yet know enough to tell you truly," he said, "and I would rather give you nothing than give you a guess dressed up as certainty. But I will tell you this much, because you have earned more honesty than I have given you, and because I do not think silence serves either of us any longer." He nodded toward the bronze, toward the small stubborn wound in its collar that had waited a century to be noticed. "That mark is old. Older than this treasury, likely older than this empire. Whoever cut it into that bell did so long before your family's line ever sat a throne. And whoever pressed it into wax on your archive door three nights ago either knew that history, or was made to believe they were continuing it."

"Continuing what?"

"A search," Sancharan said. "Or a concealment. I confess I do not yet know which, only that both would leave exactly this kind of trail. And a dead man who guarded your family's oldest records is a very convenient casualty for either purpose."

Vaanathi lowered the lamp slowly, watching the mark fall back into shadow, watching the ordinary bronze become, once again, an ordinary bell that no one would look at twice. It did not feel ordinary any longer. She was not certain it ever would again.

"You will tell my father none of this," she said, "until I have decided how much of it he should hear, and in what order. This is my house, scholar, whatever else it may also be."

"I would not dream of taking that from you," Sancharan said. There was nothing careful in it at all, only something that, in a gentler world, in a different week, she might almost have called relief, at being, for once, permitted to simply tell the truth to someone who had asked for it plainly.

They stood a while longer in the darkening treasury, the two marks, one fresh in memory, one worn smooth by a century of quiet, pulling, she was beginning to understand, toward the same unfinished question. For once, neither of them was in any hurry to be the first to leave the room.
