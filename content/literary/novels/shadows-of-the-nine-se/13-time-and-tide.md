## I

For the first three days, Vaanathi did not count.

There was no reason to count. He had gone before, in the quiet way he went, and the going had never once outlasted her patience. A night. A morning at the very worst. She had learned the rhythm of his absences the way one learns the rhythm of a household, without ever deciding to learn it, and the rhythm had never yet broken its word.

On the fourth day she began to count, and told herself the counting meant nothing.

On the seventh, she stopped pretending.

She had covered for him by then. It felt more of a reflex now than it felt like a decision. The same reflex that makes a hand close around a falling lamp before the mind has weighed the cost of the burn. Charudatta had asked, in the courteous sideways manner he asked everything, whether the southern scholar had completed his cataloguing, since no one had seen him at the archive in some days. And Vaanathi had heard herself answer, smooth as poured ghee, that she had sent him south herself, to verify a set of border records against their originals. A tedious errand. Weeks, possibly. The kind of work one gave a scholar precisely because no one else could be made to endure it.

Charudatta had bowed and withdrawn, satisfied or pretending to be, and Vaanathi had stood in the emptied corridor afterwards with her own lie ringing in her ears like a struck vessel.

She had lied for him. He had not even asked her to. He had simply gone, trusting or not troubling to think about it at all. He had left her holding a story she would now have to feed and water like a tended plant. A lie in this court did not survive on one telling; it needed tending, the way a planted thing needs tending. Her mother would ask, eventually, in the way her mother asked things, without appearing to ask anything. Bhargava would count the days on his fingers and find them ominous. The court had spent a season learning to read misfortune into shadows. A stranger who arrived with a death and then vanished without one was exactly the kind of shadow it had been waiting for.

*You could simply tell me,* she thought at the empty air, more than once, in the black hours when the palace had gone to its beds and left her alone with the lamp. *Wherever you are. You could have simply told me you were going, and I would have guarded the door of it gladly. It is the keeping me in the dark I cannot forgive. It has always been the keeping in the dark.*

Even the crow was gone. That was the detail that unsettled her most, when she let herself examine it. Iruli had a way of remaining behind when he travelled, a dark shape on a parapet, one unimpressed eye keeping its own account of the household. Her absence meant he had gone somewhere the crow was needed more than the princess was. Vaanathi discovered she could construct several explanations for that, and that she disliked every one of them.

"You are not sleeping," Nila observed, on the ninth evening. She said it the way she said most true things, mildly. She was working a comb through Vaanathi's hair as though the conversation were incidental to the task.

"I am sleeping enough."

"You are sleeping the way a sentry sleeps." The comb moved, unhurried. "Waking at every sound in the corridor. Lying down dressed, or near enough to dressed that it amounts to the same intention." Nila set the neemwood comb aside and came around to look at her properly, and her face held the particular gentleness she saved for the questions she already knew the answers to. "He will come back, Vaanathi."

"You cannot know that."

"No," Nila agreed. "But I know you. And I know that you have spent nine days waiting at a door, and that waiting has never once agreed with you. You are not built for it. You are built for the other thing."

"The other thing."

"The thing you do instead of grieving." Nila said it plainly, without cruelty, which was somehow worse than cruelty would have been. "You did it when Smaran died. You turned the grief into a trail and followed it. For you, following was bearable and weeping was not. I watched you do it. I am watching you decide to do it again now, and I find I would rather help you than pretend I cannot see it."

Vaanathi looked at her friend for a long moment. Outside, the first true rain of the season was arriving over the gardens, that first heavy smell of wet earth rising to the windows, the smell of mud that always reached the palace a full breath before the rain itself did.

"Then help me," she said. "Because I have run out of doors, Nila. Every trail I know how to follow runs through him, and he is not here to be followed. He spoke to me once of an anchor. His own anchor, as though a man might carry such a thing the way he carries a name. I have been through every leaf in every manuscript in the library... twice. The old accord is mentioned and never explained. Nine anchors, entrusted, guarded, and not one word of anchors of what, or where, or how a thing like that is kept." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I am at a dead end. I have been at a dead end for four days now. I have not wanted to say it aloud, because saying it aloud makes it true."

And it was there, in the saying of it, that the old man's voice finally reached her.

It came the way remembered voices come, without warning and without announcement, in the exact grain and slowness of the original. She was eleven years old again, cross-legged on the archive floor, fighting a leaf whose faded hand had defeated her for the better part of an afternoon. Smaran had told her twice to rest her eyes. She had argued instead, the way she argued everything at eleven, insisting that a history no one could read might as well never have happened. And somewhere in the middle of losing that argument gracefully, bent over his own ledger and not even looking up, he had said it. *When every door in this palace is shut to you, child, come and argue with me. My door does not count as a door.*

He had said it twenty or thirty times across her childhood. She had taken it for the fond nonsense of an old man who preferred her company to his solitude and would not say so plainly. At least till now.

Vaanathi sat very still.

He had said it so many times. Not a word changed or altered. Smaran did not repeat anything these many times. He was the most economical man with words she had ever known. He repeated nothing, except the things he was afraid would otherwise be forgotten.

"Vaanathi?" Nila was watching her face. "You have gone somewhere."

"His door," Vaanathi said slowly. "He always said his door does not count as a door." She was already rising, already reaching for the lamp. "Nila. When they searched his chambers, after. They searched the room. Every shelf, every chest, the floor, the walls. I read the steward's accounting of it myself."

"And found nothing. You told me."

"They searched the room," Vaanathi said again. "Who searches the door?"

## II

Smaran's official chamber stood off the archive's eastern gallery, and it had stood locked and unentered since the inquiry closed, not out of any order but out of the plainer thing that keeps such rooms shut. No one wished to be the first to treat a dead man's room as an ordinary room again.

Vaanathi went alone. Nila had offered, and Vaanathi had refused, and neither of them had needed the reason spoken. Some doors are only meant to open for one person. And this one, definitely, was Vaanathi's.

The corridor at that hour held nothing but her own lamp and the sound of the rain, steady now, working at the roof tiles with the patience of something that intended to stay the season. She stood before the chamber door longer than she meant to. It was an ordinary door. Old teak gone dark with decades of oiled hands, a carved border of lotus-buds along its frame that a hundred years of palace doors shared, the same border her own chamber door carried. She had walked through it as a child more times than any accounting could hold. She had never once looked at it.

The door itself gave her nothing. She went over the inner face by lamplight, finger's width by finger's width, pressing each carved bud in turn, feeling for a seam, a give, a hollowness under the knuckle. Solid teak, honest all the way through, exactly as a door ought to be. She sat back on her heels, and the disappointment that moved through her was so much larger than the moment deserved that she understood, with some embarrassment, how much hope she had allowed herself on the walk here.

Then her eyes, falling, found the threshold.

Every doorway in the old wing carried one. A raised beam of wood set into the floor beneath the door, the height of two stacked fingers, worn glossy in the middle where generations of feet had crossed it and left the ends untouched. Her grandmother's generation had held it ill-mannered to tread on a threshold at all. One stepped over, always over, and Smaran, she remembered now with a clarity that hurt, had scolded her for it exactly once, at nine years old, when she had jumped his threshold with both feet like the child she was. *The threshold is the house's own spine, child. You would not step on your grandmother's spine.*

The house's own spine. The one strip of the room that belonged to neither the room nor the corridor. The one place a searcher stands over, and never on, and never once looks down.

My door does not count as a door.

She knelt. The threshold beam ran the width of the frame. She ran her hands along it, and almost smiled at her own reasoning as she did. *It is only the feet that must not touch the threshold. These are my hands, after all.* At each end of the beam, where it met the jambs, sat a wedge of the kind carpenters set to strengthen a threshold's hold. The left one did not budge. The right one seemed suspiciously flexible. She pressed it a little.

Not freely. It gave with a small creak of protest, against some resistance that had waited years to be asked, and the vertical plank below shifted and swung open like the lid of a jewellery box. Inside, in a hollow bored the length of her forearm into honest teak, lay a bundle wrapped in oil-cloth, and the smell that rose from it when she drew it out into the lamplight nearly undid her on the spot. Neem oil. The same bitter, green, careful smell of every ledger he had ever taught her to wrap, the smell that kept silverfish at bay, the smell of an old man's hands. He had wrapped this the way he wrapped the things he meant to outlast him.

She sat down on the floor of the dead man's chamber, rain working overhead, and unwrapped it where she was, because carrying it even as far as her own rooms felt suddenly like a risk the world could not be trusted with.

Palm leaves. A single narrow sheaf, bound in cord, written in the small fast private hand she knew from the letters, the hand of Smaran hurrying, Smaran afraid. But these were not letters. These were workings. Page upon page of a careful man reconstructing something from fragments, the way she had watched him rebuild a ruined genealogy from three surviving lines, testing, crossing out, testing again.

The first leaf stopped her breath.

*If you have found this, then you have run out of doors. I always hoped it would be you, child, and I always hoped it would be never.*

The rest she read the way one drinks after a long dry road, too fast, going back, forcing herself slow.

He had gone further than the letters ever admitted. The nine anchors of the old accord were not a poet's figure, he wrote, not a manner of speaking. They were things. Set things, in set places, each one seated in the world's own ground the way a foundation stone is seated, and each one given, in an age before the dynasty's own founding, into the keeping of a line entrusted to guard it. *Entrusted, and in at least one case that I can prove, erased,* he had written, and underlined the word once, and she could feel through the ink how much it had cost him not to write more. *The guarding was made hereditary because the thing guarded does not move. That is the nature of an anchor. A thing that holds must have a place to hold to. Every text agrees on this even when they agree on nothing else. An anchor has a site the way a name has a bearer.*

And then, across the last leaves, the part that he had plainly assembled over months, the part that read less like scholarship than like a man picking a lock in the dark. A list.

Not of anchors. He was too careful to claim that. A list of *places that behave wrongly in the record*, he called it. Sites where the old correspondence went quiet in the same shaped way, where land was marked unclaimed that no empire leaves unclaimed, where a river's course or a hill's name had been written and unwritten too many times for honesty. Eight entries: a district, a watershed, an empty yard. Eight trails a patient man spent a life walking down.

And a ninth entry that was not an entry at all.

No district. No watershed. No question word, even. Even a *what* or a *where* would have promised more certainty than he owned. Only the mark, drawn once in careful ink, the narrow ring broken by its thin dark wedge, the same mark from the ledger, from the bell, from a stranger's unguarded recognition on an archive floor a lifetime ago. And beneath it, in the small fast hand, three lines that she read four times over before she allowed herself to believe she had read them correctly.

*The ninth I cannot place. Every trail I follow toward it ends the same way, not hidden, ended. Ended as though the ground it stood on was taken up out of the world itself. Eight anchors cast shadows in the record. This one has none. A thing that holds must have a place to hold to, and the ninth has no place, and I do not know what that means, and I have begun to fear that finding out is precisely what someone in this palace intends.*

Vaanathi sat with the leaves in her lap for a long time.

The rain went on overhead, patient, ancient, entirely indifferent. The lamp burned low. And in the quiet of the dead man's chamber, the pieces she had been carrying for a season arranged themselves at last into a single shape, and the shape was a question, and the question had a face.

Nine anchors. Eight with places. One with none, ended, taken out of the world.

And a man who had once said to her, on a moonlit night, in a voice too tired to guard itself, *my own anchor*. A man who carried his going and his coming the way other men carry a bundle for the road. A man who had been gone, now, for eleven days, into a somewhere that no map of hers had ever held a road to.

*Where is yours, Sancharan?* she thought, and the thought did not feel like curiosity any longer. It felt like the first tread on a stair with no visible ending. *Eight anchors have a place in the world. Why does the ninth have none? And why do I already know, the way one knows a fever coming, that when you finally answer me, the answer is going to hurt us both?*

She wrapped the leaves back in their oil-cloth with the care he had taught her, nine years old, cross-legged on an archive floor. She returned the bundle to its hollow, shut the plank, and pressed the wedge back until nothing seemed to have moved. Some instinct she did not examine told her the dead man's hiding place was still the safest room in this palace, and that the day she moved these leaves would be the day she had finally decided who was allowed to know she possessed them.

As she rose to leave, she nearly set her foot on the threshold, and then saw the ant. A single ant, crossing the worn wood on its own small errand, and some instinct, faster than her own thought, lifted her foot before her mind had finished noticing it. So she hopped, stepping over the threshold just the way her grandmother had always insisted, and went out into the rain-loud corridor, carrying nothing in her hands and everything behind her eyes.

She had come missing one man. She left missing two of the men she had cared for more than herself.

## III

The passage home gave no warning. It never did. The dark opened, unhurried, and set him down in the same narrow lane behind the scribes' quarter it had taken him from, and the seam closed behind him with the soundless finality of a page turned. To Sancharan, one day had passed. A single day, spent on the floor of his own fortress, being made to remember what he had spent centuries arranging not to.

He knew before his second breath that the world disagreed with him.

He had grown, across centuries, to carry his own count of the world's time whenever he stood inside Anaadi. He could feel how far forward he had travelled, the way a man who has walked one passage a thousand times knows, even in the dark, exactly how far along it he stands. That inner count told him one day. One day, and no more. But the air told him otherwise. It carried the green, mineral weight of a land that had been rained on, and rained on again, the smell of laterite drinking deep, of stone gone dark and moss remembering itself in the courtyard joints. When he had left, the gardens were still holding their breath for the monsoon. Now the monsoon was simply here, settled in, familiar, an occupation already past its ceremonies.

He looked up. It was habit older than any of his names, the first thing he did on arriving anywhere, the way other men check their bundle or their footing. And the sky, his oldest and most literal text, the one thing in any age he had never once needed to question, told him plainly that it was eleven nights later than it had any right to be. The stars that shift only a finger's width in ten nights had shifted their finger's width. And the wanderers among them, the ones that move far enough in a single night, had moved ten nights' worth and more.

Sancharan stood very still in the wet lane and read it twice.

And then there was the moon. The moon does not lie. It only hides, on cloud-heavy nights like this one, and Sancharan had always known how to look past clouds. Whatever else in creation bends, dissembles, forgets itself, the moon keeps its ledger to the earth in full view of anyone patient enough to read it. He read it now, the phase, the height, the company it kept, and the accounting came back the same both times. Eleven days. He had spent one day inside Anaadikottai and the world had aged eleven days without him.

*That is not possible,* Iruli said, from his shoulder. Her voice had gone flat in the particular way it went when she was refusing, on principle, to be frightened. *Say it is not possible, so that I can argue with you about something.*

"It is not possible," he said quietly. "Anaadi keeps time the way a miser keeps coin. It spends slowly and it never, in all the ages I have held it, spent faster than the world outside. Days within for hours outside. That is the shape of it. That has always been the shape of it."

*And yet.*

"And yet," he agreed.

Neither of them said the next thing. Neither of them needed to. The bench aged two centuries in a breath. The fountain running backwards. The tea that forgot to stay warm. Small wrongnesses, he had called them, symptoms of a debt he did not fully understand, and he had treated them the way a strong man treats a cough, as a thing that would pass because things had always passed. Now the debt had stopped collecting in furniture. It had reached into the one bundle he had believed only he could open, and taken eleven days of the world out of it, and it had not asked.

The rings sat quiet at his hands, dull as river stones. Spent, or sulking, or grieving. He no longer entirely trusted himself to tell the difference, which was itself a new and unwelcome piece of knowledge.

*Stay here,* Iruli said, lifting off before he could answer. *Do not walk into that palace until I know what the palace believes happened to you. Eleven days is long enough for a court to bury a stranger twice over, and I would rather we knew which grave they dug you before you contradict it.*

She was gone the better part of two hours. He waited in the lane's deep shadow, listening to the rain begin again on the tiles above. He found, with a kind of distant clinical interest, that he could not make himself feel the urgency the situation plainly deserved. The feeling was there, somewhere, the way a fevered man hears his own child calling from the next room and cannot make his limbs rise to answer. He could observe it. He could not reach it. And the knowing of that was its own slow ache, deepening with every hour. Anaadi had emptied him the way a fever empties a man, and what was left was moving him around out of long practice.

Iruli returned wet and thoughtful, and delivered her accounting from the lintel above his head, which he had learned across centuries meant the news required her to look down at him while giving it.

*You have been in the south,* she said. *Verifying border records against their originals. A tedious errand, weeks in the doing, the kind of work one gives a scholar precisely because no one else could be made to endure it. The whole palace has it word-perfect, which means it came from one mouth and was repeated carefully. You may thank the princess for your alibi. She built it seven days ago, unasked, and she has been feeding it ever since, to the chamberlain, to the treasurer, to her own mother across a supper table, which I am given to understand was the costliest telling of the lot.*

Sancharan closed his eyes.

"She lied for me."

*She lied for you eleven days running, in a court that has spent a season sharpening itself on strangers. She is, by every servant's account, thinner, sleepless, and short with everyone except the people she is lying to.* Iruli let that sit precisely as long as it needed to. *You will also want to know that you cannot walk in through the front gate tonight looking rested and clean. You have been on southern roads for a fortnight. Arrive like it. You are wearing a story another person wrote for you, old friend. And the least of what you owe her is to wear it convincingly.*

He looked down at his own hands, clean, unweathered, one day tired. Then he knelt, without a word, and pressed his palms into the lane's wet red mud, and began, with the methodical care of a man performing a penance he had assigned himself, to make his borrowed name look as though it had earned its journey.

## IV

He did not go to her that night.

He told himself the hour was to blame, and the mud, and the need to let the gate-keepers see him arrive properly at dawn like a man returning off a southern road. All of it was true. None of it was a real reason, and he had been honest with himself for too many centuries to pretend otherwise. Past the second hour of sitting alone in his own dark quarters, he was still watching the lamp burn and not lighting it.

The reason was that he did not know what he would be bringing her.

Before Anaadi, he could have named what he carried toward Vaanathi, or believed he could, which had amounted for a while to the same comfort. A regard. A warmth. A thing that had been growing all season in the space between an archive floor and a walled garden, and that he had begun, cautiously, to trust the weight of. He had carried the like of it once before, towards a laugh in a market, and he had spent eleven centuries not looking at what became of that, and now he had been made to look. All of it, whole, at once. The palm jaggery pressed into his hand. The silk cloth of a dancer uncoiling its bladed thread. The hollow on the hillside where the searchers had passed twice.

And somewhere in that long forced remembering, a question had opened under everything he thought he stood on, the way a stepwell opens under grass.

He could not name the question yet. That was the worst of it. He, who had names for the ages of stars, could stand in the ruin of his own certainty and not find the word for what was missing from it. He only knew that when he reached now for the warmth he had been trusting, his hand closed on the shape of it and came back reporting weight, and texture, and could not swear to the substance. As though he had spent a season admiring a flame through thick glass and had only now been made to ask whether he had ever once felt its heat, or only remembered, very exactly, what heat was supposed to feel like.

*You are doing that thing,* Iruli observed from the window ledge, *where you sit in the dark and conduct a trial no one summoned, with yourself as accused, judge, and the entire gallery besides.*

"I am tired, Iruli."

*You are frightened,* she said, not unkindly. *I have seen you tired. Tired makes you dull. This is making you quiet in the wrong key.* She turned her head, one eye catching the faint light from the window. *She waited eleven days for you. Whatever it is you think you lost on that floor, I would not make her wait an eleventh night to learn you are alive. The rest of it, whatever the rest of it is, can be carried later. Announcing you are alive cannot.*

"And when she asks me where I was?" He said it evenly, to the dark. "When she asks me, and she will ask me before she asks me anything else, what am I to give her? The truth is a story I have never told anyone in a mortal age. And tonight I do not trust myself to tell it in a way that is," he paused, and the pause went on too long, "true enough. She deserves it whole. I am not whole. Those two facts have never sat in the same room before, and I find I do not know how to seat them."

Iruli was silent for a while. When she spoke again, the sarcasm had been set down entirely, which happened seldom enough that he turned to look at her.

*Then give her the smallest true thing,* she said. *You have a habit, my old friend, of believing the only honest gift is the entire treasury. It is not. One coin, freely given, buys more than a locked vault ever has. I watched you learn that lesson once at a very high price. I would rather not watch the tuition paid twice.*

He had no answer for that. She had not expected one. She tucked her head beneath her wing, formally ending the argument by declaring it beneath her further attention, and left him alone with a lamp he had still not lit and a dawn he was no longer certain how to meet.

## V

Word of the scholar's return moved through the palace by mid-morning, travelling the way all news travelled in that house, through the kitchens first.

Vaanathi received it from Nila, who delivered it quietly, watching her face, and had the grace to leave the room afterwards without offering a single word of advice. She stood at her window for a long moment, and was interested to discover that what arrived first was not relief. Relief came second, enormous and unsteady, buckling something behind her ribs. First, and faster, came anger, clean and specific, already composing sentences.

She made herself wait until evening. It was the hardest discipline she had kept all season, and she kept it imperfectly, arriving at the walled garden a full quarter hour before the time her note had named.

He was already there.

He rose when he saw her. The lamplight found him and her prepared sentences scattered like startled birds, because he looked wrong. Not travel-worn, though he wore that too, the road dust and the weathering, all of it correct and all of it, she understood with the strange double sight she had been developing all season, arranged. Underneath the arrangement he looked the way the archive had looked the week after Smaran died. A room with everything in its place and the person missing from it. And still, beneath all of it, he remained unfairly himself to look at, composed even in ruin, and for one unguarded breath her anger forgot its own argument. Then he spoke, and she remembered it.

"You are angry," he said. "You have every right to be. I would like to say what I owe before you spend it, if you will allow me that."

"You were gone eleven days." Her voice came out lower than she intended, which she had learned long ago meant she was angrier than shouting. "Eleven days, Sancharan. No word. No warning. I lied to Charudatta. I lied to my own mother, at her own table, and she knew, I am almost certain she knew, and she let me do it, which somehow makes it worse. I built you a road south and paved it fresh every single day, and the whole time I did not know," her breath caught, and she forced it level, "I did not know if I was maintaining an alibi or tending a memorial."

The tears came then, at last. Tears that had not come for childhood wounds, that had not come even for Smaran. She felt them and did not fight them. And she watched him take the sight of them like a blow, watched everything in him move toward her, and watched him hold himself, by some terrible discipline, exactly where he stood.

"I know," he said softly.

"You know." She stared at him. "That is what you have to say to me? *You know?*"

"Iruli told me everything. What you did. What it cost." He held her eyes, and did not defend himself, which was so far from what her prepared sentences had been built to breach that she found herself momentarily without a wall to aim them at. "I did not intend eleven days. I intended one. Something," he stopped, and she watched him choose, and she watched the choosing fail, "went wrong. With time itself. In the place I go. I have never seen its like before, and it should not be possible, and that is the truth, and I am aware of how little of it I am giving you."

"Then give me the rest."

"I cannot tonight."

"You cannot tonight." She said it flatly. She had heard those words from him before, in a fortress under a wrong sky, and they had been a promise then, warm at the centre, a door closed gently with the light left burning behind it. These were not those words. These were a wall with the light gone out. "No. This is different. Do not think I cannot hear the difference, Sancharan, I have spent a season learning your silences like a script. Before, when you refused me, you refused me like a man protecting something. Tonight you are refusing me like a man who has lost something and will not say what."

He said nothing. And the nothing confirmed it, and she felt the confirmation land somewhere it would bruise deepest.

She had come armed. Eight anchors with places, a ninth with none, a dead man's careful ink burning a hole in a threshold across the palace. She had rehearsed the question all afternoon, *where is yours*, just those three words, a key she had bled for. She looked at him now, hollowed and arranged and holding his wall, and understood, with a clarity that gave her no satisfaction at all, that this was not the night to spend it. Not because he did not deserve the asking. Because tonight the asking would win her nothing but the sight of him breaking, and she found, to her own fury, that she still could not want that.

"I found something," she said instead, quietly. "While you were gone. While I was running out of doors to open. I am telling you that I found it, and I am telling you that I understand more than you think I do, and I am telling you nothing else. Do you hear me? That is what it feels like, Sancharan. From the other side. I have carried it for eleven days. You may carry it for a while now."

"Vaanathi." He reached for her then, one half-step, as though against every counsel he had left.

"No." She stepped back from the reach, and the step cost her, and she paid it. "You came back alive. I am glad of it. I am more glad of it than I have been of anything in my life, and tomorrow I will probably forgive you, because I have apparently become the kind of fool who does. But tonight you have given me a wall, and I find I have one of my own, and mine has eleven days of masonry in it."

She turned at the garden gate. He had not moved. The lamplight held him where she had left him, road-dusted, hollow, watching her go, and for one treacherous moment the sight of it nearly turned her around.

"When you are ready to tell me what you lost," she said, "I will be ready to tell you what I found."

Then she walked out of the garden, and for the first time in the whole long season of the two of them, it was Vaanathi who left first, and Sancharan who stood alone in the lamplight, learning what it was to watch a door close from the outside.
