## I

"I will tell it as it happened," said Sancharan. "I will not tell it as I would like it to have happened. I have tried that. It does not work."

He had moved. He sat now with his back against the doorframe and his knees drawn up, and his hands hung loose between them. He still did not look at her. The lamp had been trimmed twice. A thin line of grey had begun to show along the lattice, though it was still hours to dawn.

"We had been together a year and two months," he said. "It was the monsoon. She had a cold that turned into a fever. It was not a bad one. I sat with her on the doorstep of her aunt's house, because inside it was too hot, and she said she wanted to see the rain."

"And the story?"

"I was making up a story about Iruli." The faintest line of humour touched his mouth and went. "She had asked me to. She said a crow who bowed to a sweet-seller had to have a past. I was telling her that Iruli had once been a very proud queen who was turned into a bird for refusing to say thank you."

"Was she?"

"No. She was a crow. She was always a crow. But it made Amudha laugh."

## II

The rain had stopped by midnight. Water dripped from the thatch. The street was a long dark strip of puddles and the smell of wet earth.

Amudha lay with her head in his lap and her feet on the step, wrapped in a blanket to the chin. Her hair, loosened and tangled, spread across his knee. He had learned not to look at the grey in it. There was a lot, now. It ran through the black like salt across a dark field. Her face had thinned. The dimple was deeper, and there were lines at her eyes. She had said it was the best her face had ever looked.

He held his hands in the air above her. It was how he had held them for a year, a finger's width off her, always. She had stopped remarking on it.

"And then," he said, "the queen looked at the sparrow and said..."

"You have stopped."

"I am thinking."

"You are listening to something." She opened her eyes. They were large and clouded with fever. "You have your head on one side like Iruli."

He had not noticed.

It was a very small sound. It was not in the air. It was a hum, deep and slow, somewhere under his ribs, the feel of a distant bell that has been struck once. He sat quite still. The hum went on. It changed pitch. It deepened.

Then the stars moved.

He saw it over her shoulder. Above the thatched roof across the road, where the sky was clearing, a thousand points of light had been hanging in the same patterns they had held for ten thousand years. They shivered. One at a time, then all together, they slid sideways, a hand's width, like beads on a loosened thread. They stopped. And in the gap they had left behind, a pale grey light began to spread.

"Oh," said Amudha softly. "That is not right."

"No."

"It is going to be bad, is it not?"

"I think so."

"Go."

"I will not leave you with a fever."

"It is a small fever." She put a hot hand out from under the blanket. It hung in the air a finger's width from his wrist. She had learned to do that too. "I have had worse. I have had the pox. Go. You are hearing them, are you not? The nine. They are calling."

He was. He had been hearing it for nine breaths, and each breath it grew louder. It was not a voice. It was the sound of eight other presences, all reaching out for him at once, the way a drowning swimmer reaches.

"I will come back," he said.

"I know."

"I will be as quick as I can."

"You are never quick." She smiled. It was the old smile, wide and rueful. "Go on, house-on-your-back. I will keep the step warm."

He went. He ran down the street, a thing he had not done in a thousand years. Iruli flew above him, screaming. And behind him, on the doorstep of a low thatched house, a woman sat wrapped in a blanket under a sky that had gone wrong, and watched him go.

## III

The courtyard of the fortress was burning.

He saw it as he came through the door in the world. The pale floor of the great court was covered in a drifting grey flame. It was not a hot fire. It gave off no light. It was a cold fire, like wet ash, and where it passed it left a fine grey dust that fell like ash. The fountain had stopped. The sentinels lay in broken heaps of stone. Overhead the violet sky was veined with dark.

A door stood open in the far wall. It was the door from the stepwell, the plain dark door of the ninth tier, the one that was never meant to be opened. It had been thrown wide. Through it, in a thin cold stream, the grey fire poured.

Seven of the nine were already in the court.

Surya stood in the centre with his arms out. A light poured from him like the sun at noon, hard and gold, and where it fell the grey fire shrank. But it came on, always. Behind him, Guru was on his knees with both hands pressed to the floor. Shukra was chanting, without sound. Lohitan had a spear, a fire-red one, and he was driving it at the stream with a roar. Budha was shouting directions that no one could hear.

"Where have you been?" cried Surya.

"I came as I could."

"Hold the east wall!"

He went. He had never moved so fast. He crossed the court through the drifting flame. It brushed his arms. He felt it, a dull burning cold that ate at the edges of his time. He reached the east wall and put both hands against the stone, and he did the thing that was his office. He held.

He held the moment still.

It was a vast, sick effort. All the time in the court gathered itself around him and slowed. The drifting flame froze in the air. The sound went out. He felt something tear in his chest, and knew that it was the great anchor, the one he had carried since the first dawn, the weight that was his share of the world.

The grey fire pressed against him. It had no face. It had no voice. But it was alive, in the way that a fall of rock is alive. It meant nothing. It wanted nothing. It was simply the going-out, and it was coming in.

"I cannot hold it," he said aloud.

"You must!" called Surya.

"I cannot."

"Then break it!" Guru's voice, hoarse, from the floor. "Fuse it! Give it your anchor! Do not stand there, Judge!"

"It will not be mine again."

"It will be nobody's, if you do not."

He understood. He had always understood. Somewhere in the heart of him he had known what the cost would be since the first moment he heard the hum under his ribs.

He took the anchor in both hands and drove it into the stone.

It did not hurt. That was what he remembered. It was a vast, clean, silent thing. Nine rings of black iron slid down over his fingers. They were warm. They fitted exactly. They closed round the place where his anchor lay, as a hand closes round a flame, and held it down. A clean heavy silence fell. The grey fire stopped.

It did not fade. It stopped, like a river meeting a dam.

And everywhere in the world, on every wall and gate and plate and leaf, the nine-spoked wheel cracked. A wedge broke clean out of every circle, at the sixth hour, all at the same instant. It fell away as a flake of dry clay falls from a wall. In the great court, on the inlaid floor, a wedge of dark stone dropped into nothing, and a thread of grey rose out of the gap. It was only a single thread. Nobody saw it but him.

The sound came back. Lohitan was shouting. Surya was on his knees.

And in his chest, a long way off, a small steady note that he had been listening to without knowing it went quiet.

## IV

"I came back in an hour," said Sancharan.

His voice was perfectly flat. He had put his head back against the doorframe and closed his eyes. Vaanathi sat on the bed with her hands clasped so tight that the knuckles ached.

"It was an hour in the fortress. I did not know what it was outside. I ran. I was so afraid. I had a thing I had not felt before, a pressure behind the eyes. I went through the door and down the road. It was still dark."

"And she..."

"She was on the step."

He opened his eyes. He was looking at nothing.

"She had not moved. The blanket was round her. Her head was against the doorframe. I thought she was asleep. I was so glad. I thought, *it was only a small fever.* I came up the step and I said her name."

He stopped. His hands had closed on his knees.

"There was a mark on her chest," he said. "It was grey. It was the shape of a splinter. It was no longer than my little finger. It had gone through the blanket and the cloth and into her, and it was burning with that cold fire. She was awake. She said, *you took your time.*"

Vaanathi made a small sound. She did not know she had.

"It had come through the stepwell door. A fragment. A bit of the fire that had flown loose in the confusion and gone out through the open way, down the road, through a thatched window. It had been looking for something warm." His voice cracked, once, and steadied. "It found the only warm thing on that street who was not afraid of the dark."

"She did not..."

"She did not feel it, at first. She said it was like a drop of cold water. And then it spread."

He told her the rest in short flat sentences, as one lays down stones. He had knelt. He had put his hands out. And for the first time in a year and two months he had not stopped them. They had closed on her shoulders, hard, as hard as he could. He had held on. She had said, *oh,* in a small surprised voice. And he had felt her time run through his fingers like water through a sieve.

"I made it worse," he said. "I know that. I could not help it. I held her and I saw all of it, the whole of the road. Her life came up in my hands in one piece. It was very short. It was very bright. It was already nearly over."

"What did she say?"

"She said, *you are holding me.*"

"Yes."

"She said, *at last.* She was laughing. It was a small laugh, but it was hers. She said, *I told you it would not kill me. I was almost right.*" He drew a breath that shook. "She told me I was walking like a house on my back. She said I was to stop. She said I was to put it down for an hour."

"And did you?"

"I did not know how."

Vaanathi covered her mouth with both hands.

"She died about the time the sky went pink. I do not remember the moment. I remember the colour. It was the exact colour of the sweets she used to sell." He turned his hands over in the lamplight. "I held her for a long time. Iruli sat on the roof and did not make a sound."

## V

The splinter had not gone out with her.

He found it afterwards, in the grey light, when he could see again. It lay on her breast, a thing no larger than a thorn. It burned with a pale cold fire. It was not a stone, nor metal, nor bone. It was a piece of the thing that comes at the end, and it had been in her heart.

He put out his hand. He meant to take it.

The rings woke.

They flashed once, nine hard black flares on his fingers, and a pain such as he had never known went up his arm. It was not for him. It was for the splinter. The rings were guarding the thing they held. They struck at anything that could harm it. The splinter was such a thing. It burned him from the wrist to the shoulder. He cried out and fell back.

"So I could not touch it," he said. "I sat in the dust for a long time with my arm on fire. Then I remembered what she had said."

"What?"

"Do not be so solemn." He almost smiled. "I thought, she would laugh at me. I thought, *you are the Judge. Judge this.*"

He took off his cloth. He wrapped the splinter in it, with his hands wrapped too, so that the rings would not wake. He lifted it from her. It weighed nothing.

He carried it back to the fortress, through the door in the world, across the broken floor. The fountain was running, as it had always run. The three sentinels were already at work on the broken stone. They did not speak to him. He walked to the centre of the court and knelt, and dug with his fingers in the pale stone, and it opened for him as water opens. He laid the splinter in the hollow. He closed the stone over it.

"I did not think," he said. "I did not think what it would mean. I only wished to put it where nobody could find it. And I thought, if it lies in the one place that is mine, I will know when it moves."

"It has moved."

"Since your blood."

"And the door?"

"Yes." He opened his eyes. "The door."

She waited. He took a long time.

"The stepwell door was never to be opened," he said. "It was not a door that a being could open from within. It had no handle on our side. It could be opened only from the world, by one who bore a certain blood. It was built that way, deliberately, as a last defence. If the fortress were ever overrun, the keepers could throw it wide and let the enemy through. It was meant to be a mercy. It was meant to be the end."

"Someone opened it."

"Someone did."

"Who?"

"We looked for the answer for three nights. We found the ring." He lifted his right hand a little, and let it fall. "There was a smear of blood on the ring of the door. It was dry. It had been there some hours. The sentinels, who do not lie, said that it was the blood of Anantha."

The room was very quiet.

"Surya said that it was proof," said Sancharan. "He said that the house of Anantha had betrayed the Accord. He said that one of them had come in the night and opened the way, whether out of folly or malice. The others agreed. I did not. I said that we did not know the hand. I said that there was no evidence of what was in the heart that did it."

"But you did not stop them."

"I could not. They were afraid. They had been burned." He shook his head slowly. "I did not stop them blaming. I only said that I would not weigh a house on a smear of blood. I think that is why they let me keep her story to myself."

"Did my family know?"

"I do not think so. Your ancestors did not know that the stepwell door existed. They believed it was a story. They kept the bell and the stones and were never told what for." His voice dropped. "It is possible that the one who opened the door was not of the court at all. A cast-off cousin. A bastard son. Someone with a few drops of the old blood and a great deal of grief."

"Or someone with a reason."

"Or that."

She thought of the ring on the door at the stepwell, worn bright by hands. She thought of the way it had drunk her blood. She thought of the dry brown smear that had waited a hundred years to be read.

"They think it was us," she said. "That is why Surya wanted me put away. It is not only the shard. It is that he thinks we did it."

"He is afraid of you. It is not the same as hatred."

"It is very close."

"It is closer than I like."

## VI

She did not know when she moved. She found that she had left the bed. She had crossed the three paces of floor that she had so carefully set between them. She knelt on the mat in front of him, in the lamplight, and he looked at her, and she saw that his face was wet.

"Show me the rings," she said.

"They do not show."

"Show me."

He hesitated. Then, slowly, he lifted his hands and turned them palm down in the light.

For a moment there was nothing. Then, like salt rising through wet cloth, they appeared. They were black iron, plain and dull, a ring on every finger and thumb, nine in all. They were so close-fitting that they seemed to grow out of the flesh. They did not gleam. They drank the light.

"They hum, sometimes," he said. "When I am near danger. It is why I stood still in the alcove. I did not wish them to wake."

"May I?"

"No."

"Please."

"Vaanathi. They will strike."

"They did not strike when I took your arm on the riverbank."

"I was not holding your blood then."

"Is it only that?" She was very close. She could feel the cold coming off the iron. "Is it blood? Is it the line? Or is it that you are afraid of what will happen if they do not?"

He did not answer. His hands shook slightly in the air between them.

She put out her own hand. She did it slowly. She laid it flat, palm down, over the back of both his.

It was cold. The iron was cold, dull and heavy and strange. She felt it hum, very faintly, under her skin. A faint high note rose in the room, and the lamp flame quivered. Her heart gave one hard knock.

And nothing happened.

Nothing at all. The rings did not flare. The note thinned and sank. Beneath her palm, the skin of his hand was warm. She could feel the long bones of it, the tension, the faint pulse. It was only a hand. It was a man's hand, that had shaken, and held a dying woman, and carried a splinter of the end of the world.

![Sancharan and Vaanathi](15-04-sancharan-vaanathi.jpg)

"Ten breaths," said Sancharan. His voice was barely a thread.

"I know."

"I cannot see your end."

"I know."

"I do not know what you are."

"Neither do I." She did not take her hand away. "I think we are going to have to find out."

A small clatter came from the next room, the sound of a cup set down too hard on a table. A breath was drawn, quick and silent. Then the curtain moved.

Nila stood in the gap. She was holding a lamp, and her face was pale and quite calm. She looked at the two of them, kneeling in the light, with their hands together on the floor. Her eyes went to the iron. They stayed there a moment too long. There was something in them that Vaanathi had never seen, a look of enormous, careful grief.

"Forgive me," said Nila. "I thought I heard someone call. I see I was mistaken."

"Nila..."

"I will go to bed. It is very late." She did not look at Sancharan. "I will only say one thing. I think it is time that you both slept."

She let the curtain fall.

Neither of them moved. After a moment, Sancharan drew back his hands. The rings faded, and were gone. He rose, stiffly, as an old man rises. He stood for a moment looking down at her.

"Thank you," he said.

"For what?"

"For not being afraid of it."

He went out. She heard his step in the passage, and then no step at all. She sat on the floor a long time. At last she lay down on the mat where he had been, and drew her knees up, and slept.

She woke to a sound that she did not at first understand. It was coming from the lower court. It was the great gong, striking the hour. It struck three times, quickly. It stopped. It struck once. It stopped. Then it began again, very slowly, as though someone were turning the hour over in their hands.
