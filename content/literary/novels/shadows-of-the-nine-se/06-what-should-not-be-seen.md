## I

"We should have gone to him already," Vaanathi said, pacing the narrow width of her chamber. Nila had learned, across years of friendship, to simply wait this energy out rather than interrupt it. "Ratnan helped break down that door. He stood close enough to the writing table to have seen whatever Smaran was reading in his final hour. If Smaran suspected someone close to the crown, then Ratnan may be the last person alive who glimpsed what that suspicion was built on."

"You cannot summon an under-steward to your private chambers at midnight without every servant in this wing knowing of it by morning," Nila said, with the patience of someone who had already had this exact argument several times that week, and expected to have it again before the night was through. "Whatever you intend to ask him, you will have to ask it somewhere the walls do not also happen to be listening."

"Then I will go to him."

"Alone?"

Vaanathi did not answer immediately. Nila correctly read that as *no, not alone* — and Vaanathi herself was only beginning to admit it meant precisely what it sounded like.

![Vaanathi Meets Nila](04-01-vaanathi-nila.jpg)

"You are going to take the scholar," Nila said. It was not quite a question. She watched Vaanathi's face with the knowing patience of someone who had known her too long to be fooled by a carefully neutral expression.

"He knows how to ask a question without frightening the answer out of a man before it can be given," Vaanathi said. "That is a rarer skill than you might think."

"That is not what I asked."

"I know precisely what you asked." Vaanathi paused at the window, looking out at the dark gardens. She did not turn around when she spoke again, which she suspected told Nila a great deal more than her face would have. "Yes. I am going to take him. I find I no longer wish to do this particular kind of thinking alone, Nila. I am not entirely certain when that changed, or what to do with the knowing of it."

Nila said nothing for a moment. She only crossed the room and pressed her hand briefly, warmly, against Vaanathi's arm — not quite approval, not quite worry, something gentler and more complicated than either. "Then go carefully," she said. "And come back before the palace wakes, or I will be the one explaining your absence to your mother. I have never once been convincing when I lie to that woman."

"You lie to her beautifully. You simply dislike doing it."

"There is a difference," Nila agreed, "and I would rather not test it tonight on your behalf."

***

Sancharan was waiting at the edge of the scribes' courtyard when she found him, Iruli a dark, watchful shape on the low wall beside him. If he found it strange that the princess of Anantha had sent word asking him to meet her past the second watch, he gave no sign of it. Only the faint, careful stillness he wore whenever he had decided a thing mattered more than he wished to show.

"You came," she said, which was not what she had meant to say first, and betrayed rather more relief than she had intended it to.

"You asked," he said simply, as though the matter required no further explanation than that. Something in the plainness of it settled a knot in her chest she had not quite realised was there.

"Ratnan," she said, without further preamble, because preamble felt like a luxury neither of them could presently afford. "He is the only living person who might know what Smaran saw. I want to ask him before anyone else thinks to silence that knowing."

Something crossed Sancharan's face at the word *silence* — quick, unreadable, gone before she could name it. He did not ask her what had put it there, and she did not offer.

"Then we should not delay," he said simply, and fell into step beside her.

*You have both decided this is a reasonable use of a night,* Iruli observed, lifting from the wall to glide ahead of them, her voice pitched for Sancharan alone. *I want it noted, for whoever eventually writes the history of this particular foolishness, that I raised an objection.*

*Your objection is noted,* Sancharan told her. *Scout the passage ahead regardless.*

*I always do,* Iruli said, with some dignity, and was gone into the dark.

## II

The under-stewards' quarters sat in the palace's older wing — a warren of narrow corridors that had not been built with any particular grandeur in mind. They had therefore never received the attention the grander halls received from the palace's night patrols. This was, Vaanathi reasoned, both the reason it was the sensible place to go unnoticed, and the reason her pulse climbed steadily as they moved deeper into it, lamp shuttered to little more than a glow, footsteps carefully soft against old stone.

"You have done this before," she observed quietly, watching how naturally he moved through unfamiliar corridors, reading the darkness the way she read a familiar room. "Moved through a strange palace at night without being seen."

"More often than I would like to admit to a princess whose good opinion I have only recently begun to earn."

"That is not an answer."

"No," Sancharan agreed, with the faint ghost of something that was almost humour, "it is not. I find I have grown rather practised at those, of late."

"You are certain of the route," he said, after a while, close enough behind her that she felt rather than heard it.

"I have known this palace since before I could walk it upright. I am certain of every route in it." She paused at a junction, listening. She did not fully believe her own confidence, not tonight, not with the particular charge in the air that came from doing a thing she knew perfectly well her father would forbid if asked directly. "I am considerably less certain of the patrol schedule. Charudatta altered it last month, ostensibly for the treasury's benefit."

"Ostensibly."

"Charudatta does very little that serves only one purpose at a time," Vaanathi said, and found, even now, even here, that the observation made him almost smile.

They passed a narrow window that let in a thin wash of starlight. For a moment neither of them spoke. The old palace settled around them with the particular hush of a building that had watched a great many secrets move through its halls long before these two, and would, in all likelihood, watch a great many more after.

"May I ask you something," Vaanathi said quietly, into that hush, "that you are under no obligation to answer honestly?"

"You may ask anything you like. I cannot promise the answer will satisfy you."

"Are you afraid? Of whatever this is we are walking toward?"

Sancharan was quiet long enough that she thought, not for the first time, that he might simply let the silence stand as its own answer. "I have been afraid of very little, for longer than I would like to admit," he said finally. "I find that troubling, some nights, rather more than the fear itself would be. But tonight—" He paused. Something in his voice, low and careful in the dark beside her, made her chest tighten in a way she did not examine too closely. "Tonight I find myself considerably more afraid of what might happen to you, walking beside me, than of anything waiting at the end of this corridor."

Vaanathi did not have a ready answer for that. She was still searching for one when Iruli dropped from the shadows above with none of her usual theatre, landing hard on Sancharan's shoulder, wings still half-spread with the urgency of the warning she carried.

*Lamplight. Two corridors over, coming this way. Unhurried, which means a patrol, not a chase — but unhurried does not mean it will pass you by if you are standing in the open when it turns this corner.*

Vaanathi went very still. There was no time to retreat the way they had come. She could already hear it herself now — the soft, unhurried rhythm of a guard's measured tread, close enough that counting the seconds until it rounded the corner had become a luxury they no longer had.

"There." Sancharan's voice dropped to almost nothing. His hand found her arm with a gentleness that belied the speed of the movement, steering her toward a narrow servant's alcove set into the corridor wall. It was barely more than a recess for storing lamp-oil and old linens — shallow enough that she understood, even as he moved her into it, that it would not hide two people standing side by side.

It would hide one shape, pressed close against the stone, broad enough to fill the recess entirely, if the second person stood close enough behind the first to disappear into their shadow.

She understood what he intended a half-second before he did it. There was no time left to be anything but grateful for his understanding of geometry.

He turned, put his back to the corridor, and drew her in against him. Nothing about it was rough — but it had the swift, economical certainty of a man who had done difficult things quickly before, and trusted his own hands to do them correctly. Vaanathi found herself with her back against the cold stone of the alcove's rear wall, Sancharan's chest a bare handspan from her own. His shoulders filled the recess's narrow mouth entirely. His height and the breadth of him built a wall between her and the corridor that no passing lamplight would think to look behind.

![Vaanathi Sancharan at the Alcove](04-02-vaanathi-alcove.jpg)

She had not, in all her years of careful court distance, ever stood quite this close to anyone who was not family.

"Do not move," he breathed, barely more than shaped air against her ear, "and do not, whatever you hear, laugh."

"I was not planning to laugh."

"You are already fighting it. I can feel it in your shoulders."

"That is an unforgivable accusation," Vaanathi whispered. Despite everything — despite the very real danger not four strides distant, despite the cold stone at her back and the guard's footsteps growing steadily louder — the corner of her own mouth betrayed her anyway.

The lamplight swelled at the corridor's mouth. Vaanathi held her breath. She felt Sancharan go utterly, deliberately still above her, his gaze fixed somewhere over her head. His whole attention bent toward the sound of the approaching patrol, a focus so complete she could feel it in the air between them — a held tension that had nothing to do with fear, and everything to do with the particular controlled stillness of a man who had, she suspected, been keeping people safe in the dark for a very long time before tonight.

She was close enough to feel the steady, unhurried rhythm of his heartbeat, where her palm had come to rest, without her quite deciding to put it there, against his chest. Close enough to see, in the thin wash of lamplight sliding past the alcove's edge, the exact shade his eyes went when he was concentrating on something other than her. Closer still — close enough that when his gaze dropped, briefly, involuntarily, to find hers in the dark, she felt the whole of her careful composure waver. Three days of archive banter and one midnight tower had not managed that.

It occurred to her, with the strange clarity that arrives only in moments too odd for ordinary thought, that she had spent a great deal of her life being watched — by ministers, by court, by servants trained to notice every small thing a princess did and did not do. She had never once felt watched the way she felt watched now, in the dark, by a man whose entire attention seemed to have narrowed to the small space of breath between them. There was nothing in it of the court's assessing appraisal, the cataloguing gaze she had grown so practised at enduring. There was only a kind of quiet, helpless attention — the look of a man who had meant to keep his guard raised, and had simply, somewhere in the last several days, forgotten how.

His hand had not left her arm since he'd drawn her into the alcove, she realised. Not gripping, only resting, warm even through the fabric of her sleeve. She found herself, absurdly, cataloguing the fact rather than objecting to it — the way she catalogued everything now, filing it away in the same locked room where she kept the mark and the letters and every other truth this strange week had handed her.

The guard's footsteps reached their loudest just beyond the alcove's mouth. A pause. The small domestic sound of a lamp being adjusted. A soft grumble about the lateness of the hour and the thanklessness of a night patrol's wage. Then, mercifully, the footsteps moved on, fading back the way they had come, until the corridor's ordinary darkness reasserted itself around them.

Neither of them moved at once.

"He is gone," Sancharan said, very quietly, and did not immediately step back.

"I am aware."

"I ought to release you."

"You ought," Vaanathi agreed, and neither of them did anything about it for one long, suspended moment more. Long enough that she felt, with a clarity that startled her, every point where he was near her — the warmth of him against the cold stone at her back, the particular unbearable nearness of a face she had spent a week deciding she did not especially care to look at too closely. Now it was close enough that looking away had become the more difficult task by far.

It was Iruli, dropping back down to the alcove's edge with a small, unimpressed click of her beak, who broke it.

*The corridor is clear,* she said, with the specific dryness of a creature who had witnessed considerably more than she strictly needed to. *Whenever the two of you have finished not moving.*

Sancharan stepped back first. Vaanathi told herself, straightening her sleeve with rather more attention than the sleeve required, that she was glad of it. She did not entirely believe herself, and decided, on the whole, not to examine the matter further tonight.

## III

Ratnan answered his door on the second knock — bleary and startled, and considerably more awake within the space of a single breath once he registered who exactly stood at his threshold at that hour.

"Your Highness—" He caught himself before the exclamation could properly form, glancing past her down the corridor with the anxious swiftness of a man calculating, in real time, every possible way this visit could go wrong for him personally. "Is something amiss?"

"Nothing that cannot wait for you to let us in," Vaanathi said gently, "and everything that has waited quite long enough already."

He ushered them into his small, tidy quarters — a single room, a narrow cot, a shelf of modest belongings arranged with the careful order of a man who had learned his tidiness from thirty years spent alongside Smaran — and lit a second lamp with hands that had not quite stopped trembling since the door.

"Forgive the state of things," he said, gesturing vaguely at a room that held nothing whatsoever out of place. "I do not often receive visitors of any standing, let alone at this hour, let alone—" He stopped himself, colour rising, plainly aware he had been about to say something about the company she kept that was not his place to remark on.

"You may speak plainly, Ratnan," Vaanathi said, settling herself on the room's single stool with as much easy grace as though it were a throne. "I did not come here for ceremony."

![At Ratnan's Room](04-03-ratnan-room.jpg)

"I have thought of little else, these past days, than that night," he admitted, once they were seated, once Vaanathi had explained, as gently as the truth allowed, what had brought them to his door at this hour. "I keep turning it over. The lamp still lit when we broke the door — he had not gone to sleep, though it was well past his usual hour. Ledgers stacked as though he had been reading through the night. I have served this palace nine years, Your Highness, and I have never once seen a man look so entirely at peace and so entirely wrong in the same moment. I did not understand, then, what unsettled me about it. I believe I understand a little better now."

"What unsettled you?"

"He looked as though he had simply fallen asleep at his desk," Ratnan said slowly, as though testing the memory for the first time rather than reciting something long since settled. "No struggle. No mark. And yet a man does not lock his door from the inside, against interruption, and then simply forget to wake. Smaran was careful in all things. Even his dying looked careful, and that is what I could not sit easily with, though I lacked the words for it until you asked."

"Do you remember what he was reading?" Sancharan asked. "Specifically. Not the shelf it came from — the leaf itself, if you saw it."

Ratnan was quiet a long moment, his brow furrowed with the effort of a man trying to summon a memory he had spent considerable energy, in the days since, trying not to look at too closely.

"Old correspondence," he said finally. "Border treaties, I believe — the hand was old, formal, nothing I recognised as recent record. And there was a leaf apart from the rest, on the corner of the table nearest his hand, that I only glimpsed for a moment before—" He stopped, swallowed. "Before I understood what I was looking at, in the room besides."

"What was on it?"

"A mark," Ratnan said. "I did not think much of it at the time — my mind was elsewhere, as you can imagine. A ring, I think. Broken at one edge." He looked between them, uncertain, clearly reading something in their stillness that told him the detail mattered rather more than he had assumed it would. "Is that of use to you?"

"More than you know," Vaanathi said quietly.

"There is one thing more," Ratnan added, hesitant now, as though only just deciding whether it was worth the telling. "A small thing. Foolish, perhaps, to mention it at all."

"Nothing is too small," Vaanathi said. "Not this week."

"The lamp itself. It had burned down further than it should have, for the hour we found him — burned to the very last of the oil, as though it had been lit rather earlier than his usual habit, or left untended rather longer than a careful man would allow. I noticed it because Smaran was never careless with his lamps. He used to scold the younger stewards for wasting oil, myself included, more times than I can count." He shook his head, faintly embarrassed by his own attention to so small a detail. "It is likely nothing. An old man's habits slipping, at the end. I only mention it because you asked for everything, and it is the one thing that has never quite sat right with me since."

Sancharan went very still at that. Vaanathi had learned to recognise the look as a man filing something away with rather more weight than his face let show, though he said nothing of it aloud, and she did not press him to, not yet, not in front of Ratnan.

They spoke a while longer — of small things, of the archive's usual rhythms, of nothing that yielded another thread worth pulling. Vaanathi was nearly ready to thank him and go when Ratnan, almost as an afterthought, added the detail that would sit with her the rest of the night far longer than anything else he had said.

"An old family friend of my father's called on me, two evenings past," he said, with the mild, faintly pleased confusion of a man still turning over an unexpected kindness. "A retired court official — I confess I did not know him well myself, though he claimed acquaintance with my father from many years back. Asked after my health, my sleep, whether the night of Smaran's death had troubled me overmuch. Kind of him to think of me, given how little anyone else at court has thought to."

"Did he give his name?" Sancharan asked, with a carefulness in his voice that Vaanathi noticed and Ratnan, still pleased by the memory of unexpected kindness, did not.

"He did," Ratnan said, and named him. In that moment, it meant nothing at all to either of the two people listening — an old, unremarkable name belonging to an old, unremarkable man the court had long since stopped troubling itself to remember. So the warning it carried passed by them both entirely unheard, the way the truest warnings, Vaanathi would later think, so often do.

## IV

They walked back through the sleeping palace in a silence that had changed shape since the alcove, though neither of them named how.

"He is being watched," Sancharan said finally, once they were far enough from the under-stewards' wing that quiet speech no longer risked anything. "Whoever visited him did not do so out of simple kindness. A man who helped break down a dead archivist's door does not receive a stranger's concern for his sleep by coincidence."

"You think he is in danger."

"I think," Sancharan said carefully, "that whoever wanted Smaran's silence secured may not yet be satisfied it is complete."

Vaanathi absorbed that in the particular still, cold way she had learned to absorb bad news since the week began — filing it, the way she filed everything now, in the locked room of her own mind reserved for things she intended to act on rather than simply feel. "Then we watch him in turn. Quietly. Without drawing the same attention to ourselves that nearly found us tonight."

"That would require," Sancharan said, "considerably more caution than we exercised in an alcove not an hour past."

"That," Vaanathi said, "was an entirely separate category of recklessness, and I would ask you not to conflate the two."

"Was it," Sancharan said — not quite a question, something gentler than a challenge. In the dim corridor light she saw him watching her with an expression she was beginning to recognise now, the same one from the tower, the same one from the shelf — want and restraint sitting so close together in him that she was no longer entirely certain where one ended and the other began.

"I have not decided," Vaanathi said honestly, "which category it belongs to. I find I am in no particular hurry to decide tonight."

Something in his face eased at that — not quite a smile, but its quieter cousin — and he did not press her further. They walked a while in the easier silence that followed, past sleeping galleries and shuttered windows, the palace around them holding its breath the way old buildings do in the hour before dawn, until they reached the small side door that would return her, unremarked, to the wing where Nila was undoubtedly still awake and waiting.

"Vaanathi," Sancharan said, before she could slip through it. Something in the way he said her name — plainly, without the armour of *Your Highness* or *princess*, the way she had said his without *scholar* wrapped around it in the tower — made her pause with her hand already on the door.

"Yes?"

He seemed, for a moment, to be weighing something, turning a decision over the way he turned over most things, slowly, with the particular care of a man who did not trust himself with haste. "Thank you," he said finally, "for trusting me enough to bring me tonight. I am aware it cost you something to decide that. I do not intend to make you regret it."

"I have not yet decided whether I regret it," Vaanathi said, "though I confess the evidence is not presently in regret's favour." She held his gaze a moment longer than was strictly wise, in a corridor this quiet, at an hour this late, with a door this close to the safety of her own rooms and no particular urgency pulling her through it. "Goodnight, Sancharan."

"Goodnight, Vaanathi."

She slipped through the door before either of them could turn that lingering moment into something neither was yet ready to name. And if her heart kept a considerably less measured pace than usual on the walk back to her own chambers, she allowed herself, this once, in the privacy of the dark, not to examine why.
