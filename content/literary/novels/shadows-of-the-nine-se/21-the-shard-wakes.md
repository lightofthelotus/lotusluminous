## I

The gong did not stop at dawn.

It struck the hour, and struck it again, and struck it a third time out of order. Then it went silent for so long that she thought it was over. Then it began once more, one slow stroke at a time. Each stroke sounded a little further away, as though the bronze were being carried down a long corridor.

Vaanathi was on her feet before she was awake. She threw on a wrap and ran.

The lower court was full of people. Servants stood in a ring round the water-clock. The copper bowl, which should have sunk steadily and ended with a clang of the gong, was rising. It floated up out of the jar on a thin stream of water, as if the water were climbing a ladder. The boy who tended it sat on his stool with both hands over his mouth.

"It started at the second watch, Highness," said a cook, in a hushed voice. "It was running as it should. Then it stopped. Then it ran the wrong way. It has been doing it ever since."

"How long?"

"I do not know. The sun has risen twice by my count. But it is only dawn."

Vaanathi looked up. The sun was low in the east, red and flat. The light on the stones was the light of morning. A single sparrow hopped along the wall.

"Twice," she said.

"I counted," said the cook defensively. "I counted when I put the rice on."

She crossed the court. At the far side, near the kitchens, stood a wooden bench where the water-carriers rested. It had been there for years. It was grey and splintered, and the carriers had worn a dip into the middle of the seat. This morning it was white with a fine soft mould. The legs had gone black. One had cracked through. It leaned like a drunk man against the wall.

"It was new last month," said a girl behind her. "I helped to carry it."

A little way off, in the herb garden, someone cried out. She ran. Kesavan was standing in the middle of his beds with his round face stricken. The row of ginger he had planted five days ago had come up, flowered, and gone to seed in the space of a night. A rank of tulsi plants, which should have been knee-high, stood as tall as her waist, black and dry. A snail, large and old, crawled slowly across the path. Its shell was cracked down the middle.

"They are all gone," said Kesavan. "Everything. The ones in the jars. Even the *dry* ones. They are *older.* The ginger has gone to dust. I opened a jar this morning and it had been in there a hundred years."

"It is the shard."

"It is the what?"

"I will tell you later." She took him by the shoulders. "Is anyone hurt?"

"Three old servants died in the night. In their beds. They were not ill." He swallowed. "I looked at them. They were so old, Highness. They had been old for years. But they had grown older. As if they had slept for a decade."

She let go of him. She had a very strange feeling in the pit of her stomach. It was cold and sinking, like the moment before a fall.

"Where is my father?" she said.

## II

He was in the small garden, on the bench under the champak tree. It was early. The first light lay along the paths. A single bird sang.

She knew from the way he sat.

He had always sat upright. It was a thing she had known all her life, as she knew the shape of the throne or the sound of his step. He was a broad man, and he carried himself as a broad man does when he has been told that he looks like a king. Now he sat folded, with his shoulders hunched and his hands spread on his knees like a man holding something heavy.

"Father?"

He turned his head. The movement was slow.

His beard was white.

It had been grey. It had been mostly black, flecked with silver, as long as she could remember. Overnight it had gone white as bleached cloth. The lines in his face had deepened. His eyes, in their nets of wrinkles, looked at her mildly, with a tired affection. He seemed to take a moment to place her.

"There you are," he said. His voice was thinner. "I thought you had gone out."

"I was in my room."

"Of course. I forgot." He tried to rise and did not. "I have been sitting here for some time. I think I have been here since yesterday. Is that possible?"

"Yes."

"I thought so." He smiled faintly. "Everything is a little far away. I keep reaching for a cup and finding it has moved."

She dropped on the bench and took his hands. They were cold. The skin lay over the bones like old palm leaf.

"It is all right," she said. "It will be all right."

"Will it?" The pale eyes searched her face. "I do not think I mind it so very much. It is only that I have so much left to do." He drew a breath. "You must not be afraid. Is your mother well?"

"I have not seen her."

"She is in her room. She will not come out." Anantaraja closed his eyes. "She does not want you to see. I told her it was all right. She said, *I have a new face this morning, and I do not like it.*"

Vaanathi's breath caught.

"I will go to her."

"Yes." He opened his eyes. "Go. And child."

"Yes?"

"It is the blood." He said it so quietly that she could scarcely hear. "It is the blood of our house. It is in the walls of the palace, and the stones, and every servant who has eaten at our table. But it is most of all in us. It is the Anantha who feel it first."

"I know."

"I would like you to tell the Judge that I have a little time. Not a great deal. A month, perhaps. Perhaps less." He looked at her very clearly, and for a moment he was his old self, the broad quiet king of the closed face. "I would like it used well."

## III

The empress sat by the window in her chamber, with her back to the door. She had drawn a cloth over her head. Vaanathi knelt beside her and did not speak.

"I did not want you to see," said Manasa.

"I know."

"I am quite all right. I only..." Her voice faltered. "I looked in the water this morning. I did not know the woman. She was somebody's grandmother."

"Amma. Please."

The empress turned. She put back the cloth.

She had been a small, neat woman with a coil of dark hair. The hair was grey. Not silver, not streaked. It was thin and fine and entirely grey. Her face had fallen in at the cheeks. Her eyes were the same. They were large and dark and clear, and they looked at her daughter out of a stranger's face with such gentleness that Vaanathi could not breathe.

"I was thirty-nine on the night before," said Manasa. "Now I am something else." She put out a hand, and it shook. "It is not so very bad. I think I will be rather a good grandmother. I wish I had more time to practise."

Vaanathi took the hand and held it to her face.

"I will fix this."

"Do not promise."

"I will."

"Please do not promise." The thin fingers moved against her cheek. "You have always been a child who promised. It frightens me. I think you will keep it, and I do not know what it will cost."

"It will cost what it costs."

"That is what I mean." Manasa drew her close. She smelled of jasmine oil and old linen. "Listen. I am not afraid for myself. I have had a long life, and it has been kind, and I was told when I was young that I would never see my children grown. And here you are." She laughed shakily, a little breath. "I only ask that you do not go to the cold place. Whatever it is. The place your father looks at when he thinks nobody is watching."

"I have to."

"I know." Her mother kissed her forehead. It was a dry, light kiss, like the touch of a leaf. "Go on. And take the crow. She is the only one of your people who has any sense."

## IV

Sancharan was waiting at the foot of the stair. He had not slept. His face was grey, and the line of his jaw was very hard. Iruli sat on his shoulder with every feather on end.

"You have seen," he said.

"My mother is an old woman."

"I know. I am sorry."

"She said she had a new face." Vaanathi heard how steady her own voice was. It sounded like someone else's. "Is it going to get worse?"

"It will. It will spread. It will go out from the palace, in rings." He spoke flatly, as if reading a list. "It is the shard. It has woken fully now. It is counting, and it counts in the blood. It will take the Anantha first, and the people nearest, and then everything that has ever eaten from this land."

"How long?"

"I do not know. Budha could say. A month. Less. The dark of the moon, perhaps, if nothing is done." He met her eyes. "Something must be done."

"What?"

"I must go in." He took a long breath. "Alone. I must find the stone where I laid it, and see what has changed. I have never gone to look. I could not bear to. If I can hold it, even a little, if I can slow it..."

"No."

"Vaanathi."

"No. I am coming."

"You are forbidden. The Council..."

"The Council can put me to sleep when it has found a way to catch me." She turned and looked at him. "It is my blood. It was my blood that woke it. If there is anything in the whole of that fortress that I can do, I will do it. I would like to see it, and not be told afterwards."

"It is dangerous."

"My mother is an old woman. I am not afraid of dangerous."

"It is not about danger." His voice cracked. "Every minute inside costs. You have seen it. An hour became six. It will be worse now. If we are in there a day, we may come out and find a week gone. If we are in there a week..."

"Then we will not be in there a week."

"It is not that simple."

"It never is." She took a step closer. She did not touch him. "Will you hold the door, or shall I break it?"

He looked at her for a very long time. Something went across his face, a flicker of despair and love and a kind of fierce reluctant pride. It was gone in a breath.

"One hour," said Sancharan. "No more. I will count it."

"Agreed."

"If I say come, you come."

"If you say it kindly."

"I will say it as kindly as I can." He closed his eyes. "I am going to regret this."

"You regret everything. You may as well regret something useful."

Behind them, the stair creaked. Nila stood on the last step with a small bundle in her hands.

She looked as though she had not slept either. But her face was composed and her voice was light. "I have rice," she said. "And a flask of water. And a little salt. You will want it, whatever you are about to do."

"Nila..."

"I do not ask where." She put the bundle in Vaanathi's hands and closed her fingers round it. She did not look at Sancharan. "I only say that time is not kind to those who linger. Come back before you are tired of it."

"How do you know about time?"

"I am a scribe's niece." The corner of her mouth lifted. "I know about deadlines."

## V

They went down at dusk, by the west road, without a lantern. Pazhani at the gate let them through without a word. He looked as though he had aged ten years. The thorn hedges along the road had flowered and withered in the same week, and the white petals lay on the ground like scattered rice.

The stepwell mouth was dark. The nine landings went down into the cold. At the sixth, where the ward had been, there was a rim of white glass, melted into the stone. Sancharan's hand passed through the place without resistance. At the ninth, the door was a ragged arch of splinters, and beyond it, the violet.

The fortress had changed.

She saw it at once. The courtyard was greyer. The pale floor had lost its glow. The sky was the same deep violet, but the stars had begun to slide, one by one, out of their patterns, so that the sentence they made was breaking into pieces. The fountain at the centre ran upward, as before, but the thread of water was thin, and it jerked. Round its base the stone was cracked in a spreading star, as if a great weight had been dropped on it from above.

From the cracks came a cold, grey light.

"There," said Sancharan.

He knelt at the edge of the star. He laid both hands flat on the stone, and she saw the rings appear on his fingers, black and dull. He bent his head.

"I laid it here," he said. "I laid it under the court. It is a long way down. I did not think it could reach."

"Can you hear it?"

"I do not hear. I feel." He turned his head slowly. "It is counting."

She knelt beside him. At first there was nothing. Then, very faintly, she began to hear it too, a dry whisper, very low, coming up through the cracks. It was not a voice. It was a rhythm. A soft, insistent ticking, like a water-clock dripping in an empty house.

*One. And one. And one.*

It was counting breaths. She knew it as plainly as if it had spoken. It was counting the breaths of every living thing in the palace. It was counting the lines in her mother's face. It was counting the days.

"What does it want?" she whispered.

"Nothing. It wants nothing. It is not a thing that wants." His voice was strained. "It only does what it is. It is a splinter of the end. It was meant to be dead. And your blood has told it to wake."

"Then I will tell it to sleep."

"You do not know how."

"No." She drew the bandage off her left hand. The cut across her palm had healed to a thin pink line. She looked at it. "But I know what I mean."

"Vaanathi..."

"I will not spill any. I am only going to lay my hand on it." She put her palm flat on the cracked stone, over the star, in the cold grey glow. "Just my hand."

It was colder than ice. It drew the warmth out of her arm in one long pull. She gasped. The ticking rose, loud in the empty court, quick and urgent, *one, and one, and one,* as though it had found something it had been waiting for.

*Be still,* she thought. She did not know where the words came from. *Be still. I am here. I am not afraid. You have nothing to count.*

The ticking faltered.

She felt it check, like a runner hit by a gust of wind. It caught. It slowed. A long shuddering breath went through the stone, and the whispering thinned. In the centre of the court the fountain steadied, and the thin silver thread of water rose straight and clear.

She did not take her hand away. She did not dare.

"It is quieting," said Sancharan, very softly. "It is... I have not seen it do that." He looked at her, and his eyes were wide in the grey light. "It knows you."

"I only asked it to be still."

"You asked it as one of its own."

"It is only an ending," said Vaanathi, through her teeth. She was shaking with cold. "It is only the last thing. I think it is tired."

She did not know how long she knelt there. Time had stopped being a measure. She held her hand on the stone, and the stone held its breath, and the hour went by, and another, and she did not count. Sancharan sat beside her, and did not say *come.*

At some point he laid his shoulder-cloth round her. It was plain grey cotton, and it smelled of rain.

At some point she slept, upright, with her palm still flat on the floor.

## VI

When she woke, the sky was the same. It had not changed. It was a deep slow violet, with stars that were nearly in place.

"What time is it?" she said.

"I do not know."

"You said you would count."

"I did." He was sitting a little way off, with his arms round his knees. His face was drawn. "I counted to four hours. And then I stopped, because I did not wish to know the sum."

She took her hand from the stone. It was white to the wrist and numb. She flexed it, slowly, as feeling returned. The star of cracks had narrowed. It was only a thin web now. The grey light had gone.

"It is asleep," she said.

"For now."

"How long?"

"A day. A week. I cannot say." He rose, stiffly, and held out his hand to help her up, and then drew it back. "We should go. I will tell you the cost when we are out."

"Is it bad?"

"I would rather not say it here."

They went through the arches and across the courts. The fountain ran upward, smooth and strong. The bench lay in its heap of grit. The three pale sentinels stood by the broken door, with their staves planted, and did not turn their blank faces as the two of them passed.

At the ninth landing, the cold light of the world was falling down the shaft. It was a thin gold shaft, the kind that comes from a low sun. She climbed. The stairs were steeper than she remembered. Her legs shook. At the sixth landing she stopped, with a hand against the wall.

"What day is it?" she said.

He did not answer.

At the top, the stepwell mouth opened to a sky of blazing blue. The sun was high. The air was thick and still and warm. The thorn hedges that had been bare, a day ago, were green again, knotted with hard small fruit. And the road to the west gate was full of dust and the noise of carts.

An old farmer was passing, leading a bullock. He stopped when he saw her. His mouth fell open. He dropped the rope.

"Mistress," he said. "Mistress, forgive me. They said you were taken."

"Taken?"

"The whole city has been looking. There are lamps lit at every crossroads. They say the emperor has not slept." He took off his turban and clutched it to his chest. "It has been eleven nights, mistress. Eleven nights."
