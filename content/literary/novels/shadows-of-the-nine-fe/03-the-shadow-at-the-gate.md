## I

The petitioner had been talking for the better part of an hour, and Vaanathi had stopped listening somewhere around the part where he compared his flooded fields to the suffering of a man buried alive.

She sat where she always sat — to the left of the throne and one careful step below it, close enough to the seat of the Anantha Empire that the court could not forget she was born to it, far enough that no one need mistake her for holding it. Sunlight fell through the high clerestory windows of the hall in long gold bars, catching the dust that never quite settled in a room this old, this used. Her father listened to the petitioner with the stillness he brought to everything — the stillness that made ministers lower their voices without being asked to — and when the man finally wound down into silence, Anantaraja inclined his head a single measured degree.

"Send an official to the eastern embankments before the month turns," he said. "And tell the district revenue office I will want to know why this was reported to me before it was reported to them."

It was, Vaanathi thought, a perfectly good answer. It was also not the answer she had been about to give, which involved diverting a portion of the temple-adjacent grain stores — no, she caught herself, not temple-adjacent, merely the old stores, the ones the city called by their old name out of habit rather than reverence — to the affected families directly, so they would not have to survive on the mercy of an official's schedule. She opened her mouth to say so.

"Your Grace need not trouble the princess with matters of embankments," said Minister Charudatta, smoothly, before she had produced more than a breath of sound, and the court's attention moved past her like water around a stone too small to be worth the current's notice.

Vaanathi closed her mouth. She had learned, over one and twenty years, exactly how much a closed mouth could say, if you knew how to close it correctly. She let this one say a great deal.

Her father did not look at her. He rarely did, during court — it was, she understood without ever having been told so directly, a kindness disguised as a discipline: to look at her would be to invite the hall to look at her too closely, to wonder aloud whether the throne's daughter thought herself fit to advise the throne. Anantaraja protected her by the simple, maddening method of pretending, in public, that she required no protecting. She had decided years ago that she preferred his blindness to Charudatta's attention. She had not yet decided whether preferring the lesser insult made her wise or merely tired.

The next petitioner was announced. He did not come.

What came instead was Ratnan, the under-steward of the royal archive, moving through the side doors at a pace no under-steward was permitted to use in the emperor's hall, his face the colour of old rice. He did not wait for the herald to announce him. He did not, Vaanathi noted with the part of her mind that was always noting things whether or not anyone had asked it to, even remember to bow until he was already three steps past the point where bowing would have mattered.

"Your Grace." His voice cracked on the second word. "Your Grace, the keeper — Smaran — he has not opened the archive since yesterday's bell, and when we broke the door—"

He stopped. The hall had gone the particular kind of quiet that Vaanathi associated with the moment just before a thunderclap, when the light goes strange and everyone's skin remembers, before their minds do, that something has changed in the air.

"He is dead," Ratnan said. "In the reading chamber. Alone."

***

There was a version of the next quarter hour that Vaanathi would, much later, be able to recite without feeling: the dispatch of the court physician, the closing of the great doors, the low tide of murmuring that Anantaraja let run for exactly as long as murmuring was useful and not one heartbeat longer before he silenced it with a raised hand. There was another version that she felt in her body for days afterward, cold along the back of her neck, though she could not have said precisely why. A man she had known her whole life — Smaran, who had shown her, at nine years old, how the old ledgers were bound in cloth soaked in neem oil to keep the silverfish from the pages, who had let her sit cross-legged on the archive floor for entire afternoons reading dynastic records like other children read adventure stories — was dead, and the court's first collective breath, before grief, before even shock, had been something closer to a word.

She heard it pass through the hall in fragments too quiet to trace to a single mouth. *Shadow.* *Ill-timed.* Someone — she thought it might have been the old treasurer, Bhargava, whose fear of the sky had only calcified with age — said, distinctly enough to reach her, "The season turns wrong this year," and did not need to finish the thought for three other ministers to nod as if he had.

Vaanathi did not believe in seasons turning wrong. She had said so, more than once, to her mother, who had smiled the particular smile she used for opinions she found charming and unpersuasive, and to Nila, who agreed with her so readily that Vaanathi sometimes suspected her of agreeing simply to end the conversation faster. But she watched the ripple of unease move through grown men who commanded armies and levied taxes and had, an hour ago, been arguing about embankments, and she understood — not for the first time, but freshly, the way you understand a scar again when the weather turns — that belief was not always the point. Fear did not require belief. Fear only required a story old enough that no one alive remembered inventing it.

"A quiet inquiry," her father said, once the hall had stilled enough to hear him. "The keeper served this house faithfully for thirty years. He will be honoured as such. There will be no talk of omens in my court." A pause, weighted exactly as long as it needed to be to reach every corner of the hall. "Is that understood."

It was not a question. The hall understood it was not a question. The murmuring did not stop — Vaanathi doubted anything short of an actual eclipse could have stopped it entirely — but it dropped below the threshold of what a man could be reprimanded for, which was, she supposed, the most any emperor could truly ask of a rumour.

She was given leave, an hour later, to see the chamber for herself, on the thin official pretext that the keeper's records fell partly under her own study of the border tributaries and someone competent ought to confirm nothing of import had been disturbed. She suspected her father had arranged the pretext himself, in the same silent, sideways manner he arranged everything for her — a door left unlocked rather than a door held open, so that no one could accuse him of choosing to open it.

The reading chamber smelled of neem oil and old palm-leaf and, underneath that, something colder that she made herself not think about too closely. The body had already been taken. What remained was the shape of the room around an absence: a chair pushed back at an angle that suggested haste, not repose; an oil lamp burned down to a hard black wick; bound leaves of preserved palm stacked with the obsessive tidiness that had been Smaran's whole personality compressed into a filing system.

And the door.

Two guards stood by it still, discussing in low voices how strange it was — how the bolt had been thrown from the inside, the old iron bar dropped into its cradle the way it was dropped every night when the keeper wanted no interruption, and yet somehow, in a room with one door and no window wider than a man's forearm, whatever had taken Smaran had left no mark of ever forcing its way to him. Vaanathi listened without appearing to listen, which was a skill Charudatta had inadvertently taught her through years of practice being unheard.

She did not, in that hour, find anything so dramatic as a clue. What she found was smaller and stranger and lodged itself somewhere behind her sternum and stayed there the rest of the day: on the shelf nearest the keeper's writing table, where the great genealogical ledger of the royal line had always sat — she knew this shelf as well as she knew her own room, had spent a hundred idle afternoons tracing the ink of ancestors eight generations gone — there was a gap. Not a missing book. A missing *page*, torn close enough to the spine that only someone who had run a finger down that exact binding a hundred times would notice the binding sat wrong, a hair too thin, where the tear had been smoothed flat and the book reshelved as if nothing were amiss.

Someone had taken something. And whoever it was had wanted very badly for no one to notice they had.

Vaanathi said nothing about it to the guards. She was not yet sure why.

## II

"You have the face," said Nila, from the low couch by the window, "of someone who is about to tell me something they have already half-decided not to tell me."

Vaanathi's chambers looked over the western gardens, where the last of the day's light was turning the tamarind leaves the colour of old brass. She had changed out of her court silks into something looser and was pacing in it, which she was aware made her look exactly as unsettled as she felt, and had decided she did not currently have the discipline to care.

"A page is missing from the royal ledger," she said. "The genealogical volume. Torn out and the binding smoothed to hide it. On the same night the only man in this palace who would have noticed a stray thread on that shelf turned up dead behind a door bolted from the inside."

Nila sat up. "You told your father this."

"I told my father nothing, because my father has already decided this is grief and coincidence and an old man's overworked heart, and because the moment I open my mouth about a missing page, Charudatta will find some elegant reason it doesn't matter, and I will have spent my one useful observation for the week for absolutely nothing." Vaanathi stopped pacing long enough to drop into the chair opposite Nila, less gracefully than a princess of the Anantha line was generally supposed to arrange herself into furniture. "I would like, for once, to know something before I am required to prove I am allowed to know it."

"That," Nila said, "sounds like a fine ambition for a woman with no actual authority to investigate anything."

"I have authority over my own feet. I intend to use them."

Nila had been at her side since they were children — daughter of a minor court astronomer, absorbed into the palace's inner rooms so thoroughly that Vaanathi sometimes forgot there had been a time before her, the way one forgets there was a time before knowing how to walk. She had a gift for saying the unhelpful true thing at precisely the moment Vaanathi least wanted to hear it, which Vaanathi had long ago decided was the entire purpose of a good friend.

"You do understand," Nila said, more gently now, "that half this court would tell you the missing page and the death and the season are the same story. That there's a name for what they'd say did this."

Vaanathi made a small, unimpressed sound in the back of her throat. "Don't."

"I'm not saying I believe it."

"You're saying it like you half do."

"I'm saying," Nila said, with the patience of long practice, "that you grew up hearing that name the way the rest of us grew up hearing about the tiger that took the herdsman's boy near the north road — a story your grandmother's generation still flinches at, even the ones who've never seen a tiger in their lives. You can decide you don't believe it and still feel your skin go cold when someone says it in a dim room. That isn't superstition, Vaanathi. That's just what it is to have been raised by people who were afraid before you were born."

Vaanathi didn't answer that immediately, because it was, infuriatingly, true. She had been perhaps six years old the first time she heard a servant blame a bad harvest on the shadow that walked when the wheel of fortune turned cruel — had watched an old woman spit three times over her shoulder at the mention of a word Vaanathi had since heard invoked over spoiled milk, broken betrothals, a horse gone lame, a king's untimely death two dynasties past. *Ganda — a misfortune arriving at the worst possible hour.* A word that functioned less like a name than like a held breath — the sound a room made right before something in it went wrong. She did not believe a wandering planet-lord walked the earth taking archivists in the night. She was nevertheless aware that some small, old, unreasoned part of her flinched at the syllables anyway, the way a hand flinches from a flame it has never personally been burned by, because enough other hands had told it, over enough generations, that this was what burning felt like.

"There's something else," Nila added, watching her face with the particular care of someone about to hand over information she suspected would not be well received. "A scholar's expected at court within the week. Sent up from the southern collegium, or so the stewards are saying — come to survey the archive's older records, cross-reference something with the border treaties. Apparently the request went out a month ago."

Vaanathi went very still. "A month ago."

"Before Smaran died. I know what you're thinking, and I'd think it too, if I were you, but a month is a long time to plan a murder around a man you'd have no way of knowing would still be alive to murder."

"Or it's a very long time to plan something you needed a dead archivist and a missing page to finish," Vaanathi said, and did not enjoy how steady her own voice sounded saying it. "When does he arrive?"

"Tonight, I'm told. Perhaps he's already here." Nila's mouth curved, not unkindly. "You have that look again."

"What look."

"The one where you've already decided to dislike someone you haven't met yet."

"I haven't decided anything," Vaanathi said, which was, strictly speaking, a lie, and she suspected Nila knew it exactly as well as she did.

## III

He had been at the edge of the eastern courtyard for nearly the length of a full watch before anyone noticed him, which did not surprise him nearly as much as it perhaps should have. People rarely looked at the stillness along the wall when there was movement to watch instead — grooms leading tired horses toward the stables after the day's dispatches, a pair of junior clerks arguing amiably over a ledger neither of them had balanced correctly, the long gold light sliding down toward the rooftops the way it did at this hour in every court he had ever stood in, on every stretch of a world that kept turning whether or not the people on it noticed the turning.

He had chosen the name Sancharan for this errand the way he chose most things — without particular attachment, only the practical calculus of what would let him move unremarked through rooms that were, on the whole, easier to move through unremarked. It suited him well enough. He had been called worse in more languages than this court's scribes had words for.

The crow on his shoulder had opinions, as she generally did, about the wisdom of standing still in plain sight for the length of a full watch.

*You could at least pretend to be tired,* she said, in the particular register only he could hear, half caw and half something closer to a woman's dry amusement filtered through a bird's throat. *Travel-worn scholars do not usually stand like fence posts admiring the architecture.*

He did not dignify this with more than a thought in reply, which she took, correctly, as agreement to consider it, and settled her weight more comfortably against his collar. Iruli had flown ahead of him for three days across country he had already walked once, centuries ago, under a different name, in a season when this same palace had been half its present size and the tamarind trees along that western wall had not yet been planted. He did not mention this to her. She would only have found some way to make it sound like nostalgia, and he was not, strictly, nostalgic. He simply noticed things that stayed and things that didn't, the way anyone notices anything they've had a very long time to watch.

He had already heard, by the time he crossed into the courtyard, that the archivist was dead. Servants talked in doorways the way water finds the lowest ground; it required no particular effort to catch the current. He had heard, too, the word that had followed the death through the halls like a second, quieter death of its own — *shadow*, *ill season*, *ganda*, the old reflexive flinch a palace made whenever misfortune arrived without an obvious author. He was, by considerable practice, unmoved by this. It had happened before. It would happen again, in some other court, under some other roof, for as long as people needed a name to put on the shape of what frightened them and had the sense not to put their own names there instead.

It was still, if he were entirely honest with the part of himself that kept private counsel even from Iruli, a small and specific kind of tiredness, hearing it again.

He was still turning that thought over — mildly, without weight, the way a man turns over a stone he has turned over a thousand times before and does not expect to find anything different beneath it this time either — when the crow left his shoulder without warning, wheeled once through the low light with a cry sharp enough to startle two grooms and a very fine grey mare into a fit of skittish stamping, and landed, with what he privately suspected was deliberate theatre, directly atop the carved stone finial of the courtyard's central archway. Where she sat, wings half-spread, regarding the scrambling grooms with what could only be described as satisfaction.

Heads turned. His, among them, though for a different reason than everyone else's.

*Subtle,* he thought at her.

*You were being ignored,* Iruli returned, entirely unrepentant. *I find that dull.*

It was, in this fashion — a startled mare, a badly behaved crow, and the small commotion of two grooms trying simultaneously to calm a horse and locate whatever madman had loosed a bird into the courtyard — that Sancharan ceased, after a full watch of careful stillness, to be unremarked.

The woman who crossed the courtyard toward the disturbance moved like someone accustomed to being watched and thoroughly tired of it, which he clocked in the space of two strides, the same way he clocked most things about most people before they had said a single word to him: a habit older than caring, and considerably harder to break. She wore no obvious mark of rank — plain court silk, hair bound simply against the evening — and yet every servant in that courtyard adjusted their posture a fraction as she passed, the particular unconscious deference people paid to blood before they'd had time to think about paying it.

"That bird," she said, arriving in front of him with the directness of someone who had decided, somewhere between the archway and this exact spot, that directness would save time, "belongs to you."

"She belongs to no one," Sancharan said. "She merely tolerates my company more than most."

"How fortunate for you both." Her gaze moved over him — quick, assessing, the particular sharpness of someone cataloguing a stranger for later use rather than present pleasure. "You're the scholar. From the southern collegium."

"I am a scholar," he agreed, which was not, strictly, a lie, and had the additional virtue of answering nothing at all.

"Whose request to examine this court's archive arrived a month ago," she said, "and whose arrival happens to fall on the same evening the archivist who guarded that archive was found dead behind a door no one could explain." She said it lightly. It did not land lightly. "Forgive me if I find the timing remarkable."

*Ah,* he thought, with the peculiar weary clarity of a man recognizing a shape he has seen take many different faces across many different centuries. *Here it is, then.*

He had met this exact moment before — the fast, frightened intelligence of someone reaching for suspicion because suspicion, at least, was a story with edges, a thing you could hold onto, when the alternative was admitting that a good man could simply die and the universe would offer no accounting for it at all. He did not, in this particular body, in this particular name, carry the weight that usually accompanied being on the receiving end of that fear. And yet something in him — old, tired, worn smooth by uncountable versions of this same conversation under uncountable other names — braced for it anyway, the way a hand braces for cold water even when told, correctly, that the water this time is warm.

"Forgive me instead," he said, and heard his own voice come out flatter than he'd intended, "if I decline to apologise for a dead man's timing, which was, I imagine, entirely his own affair and none of mine."

It was not, he understood even as he said it, the correct thing to say to a grieving court, to a woman who had — he could see it now, beneath the sharpness, the particular tightness around her eyes that spoke of someone holding a single afternoon's worth of shock very carefully in both hands — plainly cared for whoever had died. It was cruel in the specific, economical way that true things could sometimes be cruel. He watched it land. He watched her decide, in the space of a single indrawn breath, to be angrier rather than hurt, which he recognised, with an old and unwelcome familiarity, as the choice of someone who had learned young that anger kept its shape better than grief did in rooms full of people waiting to see you fail.

*That was unkind,* he told himself, in the same private register he usually reserved for Iruli. *You did not need to say it that way.*

He had not, somewhere beneath the old reflexive armour, meant to be unkind at all. He had meant — he was not entirely certain, turning it over even as her expression closed against him, what exactly he had meant. Only that he was tired of being met with suspicion before he had done anything at all to earn it, and that tiredness, worn long enough, had a way of coming out of a mouth sharper than the heart behind it intended.

"How reassuring," Vaanathi said, each word set down like a stone, "to know the southern collegium teaches its scholars such fine manners alongside their letters." She turned to go, then turned back, because apparently she was not yet finished, and there was something almost admirable, he thought distantly, in how little effort it cost her to keep finding more to say. "You'll forgive me if I have you watched, scholar. This court has had quite enough this week of things arriving in the dark with no explanation for how they got here."

She left before he could answer, which was, he suspected, precisely the point.

He stood a moment in the settling dusk, Iruli drifting back down to his shoulder with the unbearable smugness of a creature who had witnessed the entire exchange and intended to have opinions about it at length.

*Well,* she said. *That went extremely well.*

He did not dignify this either. He was thinking, instead, against every instinct he had spent centuries cultivating not to think it, of the particular colour her eyes had gone in the last of the light — not dark, as he'd expected from across the courtyard, but a brown with something warmer underneath it, like tea held up to a lamp — and of how thoroughly he intended never to mention having noticed this to anyone, ever, least of all the extremely interested crow currently watching him with her head cocked at an angle that suggested she had noticed him noticing.

*Not one word,* he told her.

*I said nothing,* said Iruli, with the particular innocence of someone who had said, in fact, nothing at all, and fully intended to make up for the omission later.

***

Vaanathi did not look back as she crossed the courtyard, which cost her considerably more than she would have liked to admit.

She had meant to be cold. She had not meant — she turned the thought over with something like irritation at herself — to notice, in the middle of being cold, the particular stillness of him, the way he had stood through the whole exchange like a man who had absorbed a great many worse insults than hers and found them all, in the end, unremarkable. It should have made him easier to dismiss. Instead it had done something closer to the opposite, some small unwelcome curiosity lodging itself alongside the anger, the way a splinter lodges alongside a bruise and makes the whole area ache in a way that is difficult to locate precisely.

*He all but admitted he doesn't care that a man died,* she reminded herself, climbing the north stair two steps at a time in a manner entirely unbecoming a princess of the line. *That is not a mystery. That is simply an unpleasant man.*

She almost believed it. She had gotten quite good, over the years, at almost believing the things that made a day easier to finish.

## IV

The stables at that hour were mostly empty, the grooms gone to their supper, the last light draining out of the sky in the slow orange way it did over the western wall this time of year. Sancharan found the quiet of it more restful than he'd admit to wanting, and sat for a while on an overturned feed bucket with his back against sun-warmed stone, letting the day settle.

Iruli preened one wing with theatrical unconcern for a good while before she spoke again.

*You liked her,* she said. *You may deny it. I will simply continue to be correct.*

"I insulted her within the first minute of speaking to her."

*Yes,* Iruli agreed. *Very effective courtship strategy. I look forward to seeing what you attempt next — perhaps you could set something of hers on fire.*

"I did not choose to insult her. It simply happened."

*Things you do not choose to happen, and yet which happen with such remarkable frequency around you.* She hopped closer along the low stable wall, dropping her voice — not that there was anyone to overhear, but old habits held regardless. *For what it's worth, old friend, she is not wrong to be suspicious. Someone in this palace wanted that man dead and wanted it to look like nothing at all. I flew the east wing roofline before you'd even reached the gate. There's a service door behind the archive — bricked mostly shut, the kind no one thinks to watch — and the mortar around it has been disturbed recently. Fresh scrape marks on the stone sill. Someone used it within the last several days.*

Sancharan sat up slightly. "You didn't lead with this."

*I was building to it. You interrupted with your feelings.*

"I have no feelings."

*You have an entire courtyard's worth of feelings, currently walking back toward the west wing with her jaw set very tightly, and you are welcome to keep insisting otherwise to a bird who has known you considerably longer than you have known yourself.* Iruli clicked her beak, which was, in her particular vocabulary, the closest she came to gentleness. *The door, Sancharan. Someone came and went from that archive without ever needing the bolt at all. Whoever locked it from the inside afterward wanted very badly for everyone to believe the room had been sealed. That is not the work of any shadow the court's frightened ministers are muttering about. That is the work of a hand. A careful one.*

He said nothing for a moment, looking out at the last colour draining from the sky, turning it over the way he turned over most things — slowly, without urgency, because urgency had rarely served him and he saw no particular reason it would start now.

"Then someone in this court," he said finally, "is content to let an old fear do the work that a careful hand doesn't want credit for."

*That,* said Iruli, "is usually how it begins."

## V

By the time the sun had fully set, the court's official word had already begun its quiet circulation through the palace's lower halls, the way such words always did — passed from steward to steward, softened at each retelling until it arrived, by morning, sanded down to something almost gentle: the keeper Smaran, beloved of the archive these thirty years, had died of a failing heart, alone, at his desk, as old men sometimes do. A season of ill fortune, some still murmured, over supper, out of the emperor's hearing. Nothing more official than that. Nothing anyone could be reprimanded for believing, so long as they believed it quietly.

Vaanathi heard the official word delivered to her directly, by her mother, with the particular gentleness Manasa reserved for information she suspected her daughter would not accept gracefully. She did not argue. She had learned, this same long week that had taught her the exact weight of a closed mouth, that arguing with an explanation the whole court had already agreed to be relieved by served no purpose beyond exhausting herself for an audience of no one.

She waited instead until the corridors had emptied for the night, until the last of the lamp-boys had made their rounds and gone, and walked back down toward the archive alone, telling no one, carrying nothing but a small oil lamp of her own and the particular stubbornness that Nila liked to claim, not entirely fondly, was the one part of her that would never be talked out of anything.

The reading chamber's door stood closed again, resealed by the palace's own steward in the old formal way — a fresh disc of wax pressed over the join of door and frame, meant to remain unbroken until someone with the standing to authorise it chose to break it. She had watched this done a hundred times in her life, on storerooms and treasuries and, once, on the door of a minister's chambers under a cloud of suspicion that had turned out, disappointingly, to be nothing at all. She knew, without needing to think about it, exactly what the royal seal looked like pressed into wax — had seen it stamped onto a thousand documents since she was old enough to read them.

This was not that.

She held the lamp closer, close enough that the wax caught the light and threw its small shadow up the door's dark wood. The mark pressed into it was careful, precise, clearly made with real craft and no haste at all — and it was not the sunburst-and-crown of the Anantha line. It was a narrow ring, broken at one point along its circumference by a thin dark wedge, like a circle with a single bite taken cleanly out of its edge.

She had never, in all her years growing up inside every room this palace had, seen that mark before in her life.

Vaanathi stood a long moment in the empty corridor, lamp held steady though her hand, she noticed distantly, was not quite as steady as she would have liked, and understood — with the particular cold clarity that arrives only once true fear has finished being loud and settled instead into something quieter and far more permanent — that whatever the court had agreed, this morning, to call an ending, was not one at all.

It was, she thought, closing her fingers around the small clay lamp until its warmth reached her skin, only the first mark of something that had not yet finished being written.
