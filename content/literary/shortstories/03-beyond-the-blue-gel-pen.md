The alarm shrieked at 6:00 AM. Dheeran stared at the ceiling, calculating if he could fake a sudden, mysterious illness to skip the Science mid-term. He was thirteen, in 8th standard, and his life was governed by the vast curriculum of the CBSE board, heavy backpacks, and zero screen time before school.

"Dheeran! Get up! The school bus will already be at the main gate in just 45 minutes!" his mother’s voice echoed down the hallway, carrying the precise authority of a principal.

Forty minutes later, Dheeran was sprinting down the stairs, making a double-tap rhythm with his boots, as he did so. He boarded the yellow bus, which smelled of damp rain, ironed cotton, and the distinct aroma of lemon rice from someone's early breakfast.

He slid into a seat next to his best friend, Rohit, who was frantically staring at a crumpled notebook.

"Bro! My brain is literally cooked," Rohit said, his eyes wide with panic. "Did you memorize the reactivity series? What if they ask which metal reacts with copper? It's giving fail era."

"Bruh! I don't even know the difference between zinc and iron right now," Dheeran muttered, adjusting his heavy bag. "If it comes in the exam, I shall just completely ghost the question."

"Good luck with that!" Rohit sighed.

The school assembly was a blur of sweat, off-key national anthem singing, and the PE teacher screaming, "Straight line! Maintain distance!"

By the time Dheeran sat down in the examination hall, his palms were freezing. The wooden desk was carved with decades of student graffiti - mostly old roll numbers and desperate math formulas. Latha Ma'am paced the aisles, her saree rustling like a warning signal. She dropped the question papers with a sharp thud.

Dheeran flipped the sheet. Question 1: Differentiate between thermoplastics and thermosetting plastics with examples. His brain went completely blank. The definitions he practiced last night had completely evaporated from his memory. He looked to his left. 

Shreya, the class topper, was already drawing lines with a scale at a terrifying speed. She was locked in. He looked to his right. Aakash was staring at his own fingers, completely dissociating.

A wave of intense stress hit Dheeran. "If I mess up this Science exam, my term marks will drop. My parents will look at me with that quiet, devastating disappointment. No way I’m getting the smart watch they promised for my birthday!" He closed his eyes, his heart thumping against his uniform badge. 

Then, he remembered what his older sister told him when she was preparing for her 10th boards: "The question paper wants you to panic. Don't let that panic stop your marks. Just write what you know, not what you think they want to hear."

Dheeran took a deep breath. He didn't remember the exact textbook lines, but he remembered how his plastic spoon bent and melted under hot water while his mom's saucepan handle stayed rigid. He picked up his blue gel pen and started writing in plain, honest words. The lines weren't perfectly straight, but they made sense. He didn't copy from Rohit, who was dropping subtle hints for an answer. He just focused on his own page.

Two hours later, the long bell rang. Trrrreeeeeeenng.

"Pens down! Collect papers from the back!" Latha Ma'am commanded.

The relief in the corridor was instantaneous and chaotic. Boys were tossing their pencil boxes into the air, and girls were arguing over whether Bakelite was a thermosetting plastic.(Turns out it is Thermosetting Plastic!)

"Bruh, that exam was not it," Rohit groaned, slumped against the veranda railing. "I definitely failed."

"Stop the cap, bro... You say that every time and get 90%! Stop actin' as if you're too immatured, Rohit." Dheeran said, twisting open his water bottle. Dheeran sat on the concrete steps by the playground, watching a bunch of 10th graders aggressively argue over a football match. He hadn't written a perfect paper. He probably lost marks on the formal definitions. But looking out at the red soiled school ground, he realized the world hadn't ended. The sky was still blue. The school canteen was still selling samosas. He probably just needed the courage to face his parents and accept what he could do better next time and sincerely feel guilty for his shortcoming this time.

He was only thirteen. This exam was just one small checkpoint on a very very long journey, not the final destination. He felt a quiet sense of pride because he didn't give up when his brain flatlined.

The final bell rang for the ride home. As Dheeran walked towards the bus, his class teacher handed back last week's English essays. At the bottom of Dheeran's page, written in red ink, was a note: 'Very creative expression. Keep it up.'

He smiled, stuffing the paper into his bag. His Science paper might be tough, but his vibe was awesome.
