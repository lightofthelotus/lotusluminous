# Read at 11:47 PM
Meera still had the chat open. Not the conversation, that had gone quiet months ago, just the window itself, pinned to the top of her phone like a tab she kept meaning to close.

She and Diya used to talk about everything, there was nothing they hadn't covered. That was the whole point of it. Whether the office cafeteria was serving the good sambar or the watery one. A stray dog that had started following Priya home from the metro station. Whether kajal or eyeliner, for the wedding they were both dreading.

Then Diya changed jobs. Then Meera's mother had the knee surgery. Then there was a period ,  Meera couldn't even mark the exact week ,  when the messages went from six a day to one, and the one was always something practical. *Sending you the caterer's number.* *Did you get the invite?*

No one had said anything unkind. That was the part Meera kept circling back to, the way you press a bruise to check if it still hurts. If Diya had said something cruel, she'd have known what to do with it. You could be angry at cruelty. You could write a message and delete it four times and finally send a version that was cold but dignified. There were templates for that. Her cousin had one framed, practically, from her own divorce.

But this wasn't that. This was Diya "liking" a photo of Meera's parents' anniversary lunch and not commenting. This was a birthday message that arrived at 11:58 p.m., clearly typed by someone scrolling through a reminder list. This was Meera, at some point, no longer being the first person Diya texted when something good or bad happened,  and Meera, in turn, doing the same, quietly, defensively, like pulling a hand back before it can be the one that's dropped.

She'd tried, once. In March, she'd written: *We should catch up properly, it's been ages.* Diya had replied with three heart emojis and *yes definitely, so busy right now though.* Meera had stared at that for a long time. Not because it was rude. Because it was true, and because "busy" had somehow become the only acceptable language for "I don't think about you enough anymore, and I don't fully know why."

At their batch mate's wedding, they'd stood two feet apart during the photos, and someone had said, "Aren't you two like sisters," and they'd both laughed the exact same laugh, and neither of them had corrected it.

What made it strange was that Meera had a script for other kinds of loss. When her grandmother died, people had known what to bring, what to say, when to leave her alone and when to sit with her. When she'd broken up with Arvind two years ago, her flatmate had bought ice cream and let her play the same three sad songs for a week without judgment, because that was what a breakup called for.

There was no dish for this. No one brought food over because your best friend from college now took four days to reply to a WhatsApp message. No one asked, gently, "How are you holding up," because on paper nothing had happened. Nobody had died. Nobody had left. They just weren't, anymore, whatever they used to be to each other, and there wasn't a word that fully covered it, only the outline of one.

Sometimes Meera thought about texting her. Not to fix anything ,  she wasn't sure, honestly, that there was a version of this that got fixed, the way you can't unshrink a sweater ,  but just to say: *I miss you in a way I don't know how to bring up casually.* She drafted it twice. Both times she looked at it and thought it would land on Priya like an accusation, when it wasn't meant as one. It was closer to grief. Grief doesn't always know how to introduce itself without sounding like blame.

In October, Diya got engaged. Meera found out from Instagram, along with four hundred other people.

Two weeks before the wedding, Meera saw the invite on a story ,  a group photo from Diya's bachelorette, everyone smiling, and Diya had tagged forty people. Meera wasn't tagged, but the invite details were there for anyone scrolling.

She could have ignored it. She could have scrolled past like she'd been doing for months. But somewhere around that point, she stopped and thought: *I'm tired of this*. Not of the loss ,  that wasn't going away ,  but of being passive about it. Of letting things just happen to her.

So she decided to go.

She showed up to the salon where Diya was getting ready, because that's what old friends did, and because sometimes you have to walk into a room knowing it won't feel the way it used to.

Diya's face when she saw her was strange ,  surprise, then something like relief, then a flicker of guilt that she tried to cover. They hugged, and it was stiff, and Meera could feel the distance in it, the way two people hug when they're not sure if they're allowed to anymore.

"I wasn't sure you'd come," Diya said.

Meera braided her hair because that's what Diya always asked her to do, and they didn't talk much, just the practical things. *Can you make this tighter? Is this okay?* And Meera worked through the braid slowly, giving herself time to feel it all ,  the loss, yes, but also something else. Not acceptance, exactly. Just the solid ground of knowing. 

Halfway through, the beautician gently took over, the way you do when it's your job and you're being paid to do it. Meera's hands dropped, and she stepped back, and that small displacement said everything. Even this ,  the one thing that was supposed to be hers to do ,  wasn't anymore. The beautician finished what she'd started, working efficiently, professionally, while Meera stood there knowing she had no place in this scene except as a guest.

Knowing that this was over. Knowing that it had been over for months, and both of them had been pretending otherwise, tiptoeing around the corpse of something that used to matter.

When she finished, Diya turned around and looked at her in the mirror.

"I'm sorry," Diya said. Not for any one thing, just the whole slow betrayal of letting someone slip away because it was easier than asking why.

"Yeah," Meera said. "Me too."

She went to the wedding. She sat in the back. She clapped when she was supposed to, and she didn't stay for the reception. On the drive home, she finally did what she'd been putting off.

She opened the chat and scrolled to the pinned message. It was from their third year of college, 3:14 AM, during exam season. Meera had typed: *i just realized i don't know what i want to do with my life.* And Diya had replied: *good. that means you're not boring. boring people always know.*

Meera had screenshotted it. Pinned it. Kept it there through job changes and breakups and everything else, because it was the most Diya thing Diya had ever said ,  the way she'd made Meera's panic sound like permission.

She hovered over the unpin button and pressed it.

It was such a small gesture, a tap of a screen, but her hands shook when she did it. Like she was making it official. Like she was finally saying: this person was important to me, and now they're not, and that's the truest thing I know right now.

She didn't delete the conversation. That felt too harsh, like erasing proof that it had ever been real. But she didn't pin it either. She let it sink to where other old chats lived, where you could still find them if you looked, but they didn't announce themselves every time you opened your phone.

It was the closest thing to a funeral she could give it.
