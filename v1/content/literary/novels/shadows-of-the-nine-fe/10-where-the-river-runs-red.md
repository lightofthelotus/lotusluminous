## I

"You are going to check on them yourself," Vaanathi said. It was not a question.

They sat together in the walled garden again, three nights after the poisoned letter that almost killed her, three nights in which she had woken twice from dreams she could not entirely describe to him — a shore, a figure, a colour of water that did not belong to any river she had ever seen — and had not yet found the courage to ask whether the dreams were hers or borrowed.

Three nights, too, in which the court had noticed a change in her it could not quite name. She had caught her mother watching her across the supper table with an expression that was not quite worry and not quite satisfaction, the particular careful attention of a woman deciding whether to ask a question she suspected she would not enjoy the answer to. Her father had said nothing at all, which she had come to understand, across a lifetime of learning his silences, was its own kind of question.

"I have put it off as long as I can justify putting it off," Sancharan said. "Iruli is right. More than one door has been disturbed. If Viyamban's family once held guardianship over the whole of this, then whatever unsettled my own anchor may have unsettled others besides, and I would rather learn that from the anchors themselves than from whatever arrives at my own gate next."

"Take me with you."

He looked at her properly then, and she watched him consider refusing — watched the old instinct rise in him, the centuries-deep habit of keeping mortal lives at a careful distance from whatever this was — and watched him set the instinct down again, deliberately, the way a man sets down a weapon he has finally decided he no longer needs in this particular room.

"They will not all be as gracious as I attempt to be," he said. "Some of them do not much care for mortals at all. One or two do not much care for me, which will complicate matters considerably more than any mortal-related caution."

"You said as much before. The one with the temper."

"Lohitan." Something crossed his face at the name — not quite fondness, not quite dread, something that lived in the narrow country between the two. "He guards the anchor nearest here. A day's ride south, then further than riding will carry you. I had intended to go alone."

"You had intended a great many things alone, before this month," Vaanathi said, "and I notice you have stopped managing most of them that way."

That, finally, won her something close to a real smile. "No," he admitted. "I have not."

"Tell me about him. Before we go. I would rather meet a stranger's temper prepared than surprised."

Sancharan considered this a moment, weighing how much of the truth would serve as preparation and how much would simply frighten her uselessly before she had cause to be frightened at all. "He is old," he said finally. "Older than I am, by some measure, though we do not keep our years the way mortals keep theirs, so the comparison means less than it sounds. He governs conflict — the kind between armies, and the smaller, meaner kind between neighbours who have forgotten why they first began quarrelling. He is quick to anger and slow to forgive, and he has never once, in all the centuries I have known him, apologised for either quality."

"He sounds delightful."

"He is, in his fashion. I have simply never found his fashion an easy one to stand close to for very long." Something flickered behind his eyes, brief and old. "We have history, he and I. Not all of it comfortable. I will tell you the whole of it eventually. Today is not the day for it, and he will likely tell you a version of it himself before I have finished deciding how to."

He reached for her hand where it rested on the bench between them — not the careful, deliberate not-quite-touching of the shelf, of the alcove, of a hundred small suspended moments across this strange season, but simply, plainly, taking it, the way a man takes a hand he has decided he is allowed to keep holding. Her fingers closed around his without hesitation, and something in her chest settled at the ordinariness of it — how strange, she thought, that the smallest gesture should feel larger than anything the fortress had shown her.

"Then we go together," he said. "But you should know, before we do, that Lohitan will not receive you gently. He receives no one gently. It is, near as I can tell, the whole of his nature."

"I have survived Charudatta for years," Vaanathi said. "I imagine I will survive one bad-tempered river-lord for an afternoon."

"I admire your confidence," Sancharan said. "I am considerably less certain of it myself."

## II

She found them together in her mother's private sitting room, which was itself unusual enough to make Vaanathi hesitate at the threshold — her parents kept largely separate hours, separate councils, separate quiet corners of the palace to retreat to, and the sight of them sharing a single lamp at this hour told her, before either had said a word, that they had already guessed something of what she had come to say.

"I will be gone a few days," she said, once the courtesies had been exchanged, choosing the plainest words she had and offering them without decoration, the way one offers a wound rather than a story about how it was earned. "I cannot tell you the whole of it. I am asking you to let me go regardless."

Manasa did not answer immediately. She set down the needlework she had not, Vaanathi noticed, actually been working on for some while, and studied her daughter with an attention that held more sorrow in it than anger. "A few days," she repeated. "Vaanathi. You ask this as though it were a small thing, and you know perfectly well it is not."

"I know it is not small. I am asking anyway, because I have run out of smaller things to ask for."

"Your mother is not wrong to be frightened," Anantaraja said, and his voice, for once, did not carry its usual careful distance. It carried something rawer, something that reminded her, uncomfortably, that beneath the crown he wore every day at court there was simply a man who had watched his daughter nearly die in her own chambers three nights past and had spent every hour since pretending, for the sake of a nervous throne, that the matter was settled.

"I am going with Sancharan," Vaanathi said, before either of them could ask, because she had decided on the walk here that she owed her mother at least that much of the truth, whatever the rest had to remain. "Somewhere he cannot yet fully explain to me either. But I am not going blind, Father, and I am not going unprotected. I would ask you to trust that, at least."

She expected argument. What she received instead was a long, considering silence, and something in her father's face that looked less like surprise than like a man watching a door he had long suspected existed finally, quietly, open.

"I know rather more than you have given me credit for knowing, Vaanathi," Anantaraja said at last. "I have known since the night you came home changed and would not say from where. I did not press you, because I judged — correctly, I still believe — that you needed the room to arrive at your own understanding before I complicated it with mine. But I am not so poor a student of this dynasty's own history as you seem to have assumed." He glanced at Manasa, some old, private understanding passing between them, and Vaanathi realised, with a small shock, that her mother had known too — perhaps longer than she had. "Every emperor of this line is told a truth on the night before his coronation that he is permitted to tell no one but his own heir, and only when he judges that heir ready to carry it. My father told me. His father told him. It concerns an old accord, Vaanathi, and nine anchors left on Bhumi's own soil, and the particular debt this very throne owes to a wrong done at its founding that has never once been properly repaid." His voice had gone very quiet. "I did not know your scholar's true name. I confess I still do not, and I will not ask you to be the one who tells me. But I have known, since the week Smaran died, that whatever moved through my court that season was not merely a man, and I have watched you walk toward it anyway with a courage I am not entirely certain I possessed at your age."

Vaanathi stood very still, feeling the whole shape of the season rearrange itself around this new, enormous fact — that her father had not been guessing blindly this whole while, had not been simply an anxious man extending trust he did not understand, but had been *watching*, with the full weight of a secret older than his own crown, and had chosen, every single time, to let her find her own way to it rather than take that finding from her.

"You could have told me," she said, not quite an accusation.

"I could have," Anantaraja agreed. "I chose not to, for the same reason you have chosen not to tell me everything tonight. Some truths are only truly held once a person has walked far enough into them to have earned the holding." He rose, and this time it was toward the old cabinet against the far wall — a piece of furniture Vaanathi had known her entire life and had never once seen him open. He unlocked it with a small brass key he kept, she now realised, on a cord beneath his own robes, close enough to his heart that she had simply never thought to wonder what it opened.

From within, wrapped in cloth gone soft and dark with age, he drew a small bell — no larger than a closed fist, its bronze worn smooth and dull by generations of careful handling, bearing no seal, no mark, nothing to distinguish it from a hundred ordinary temple bells save the particular weight of it in her father's open palm.

"Every heir of this line is given this on the same night the truth is given," he said. "Publicly, it is called a coronation relic, a ceremonial trinket from the empire's founding, and it is displayed once a generation and otherwise locked away and thought of by no one. Privately, my own father told me it is older than the ceremony built around it, and that it does not behave the way an ordinary bell behaves." He turned it once in his hand before offering it to her. "It will not ring for comfort, Vaanathi, however badly you may someday want it to. It will not ring for a clever lie told well, even by someone you love. But when a true thing needs your ears more than your eyes — when the hour comes that you cannot trust what you are seeing, or who you are seeing it from — my father told me it will find a way to be heard. I have never once, in all my years wearing this crown, had cause to test whether that was true. I hope, for your sake, that you never do either. But I would rather you carried it and never needed it, than need it once and not have it."

Vaanathi took the bell in both hands, feeling its unexpected weight, its strange, cool stillness. "Why give it to me now? If you have carried it your whole reign and never once needed it."

"Because I do not believe, Vaanathi, that your road ends at whatever door you walk through this week," her father said quietly. "I believe this is only the first of several. And I would rather you carried something that might matter at the last of them, even if it does nothing at all for you at the first."

"I will," Vaanathi said, and found her voice was not entirely steady.

Her mother crossed the room then and embraced her fiercely, briefly, in the particular wordless way Manasa had always preferred to any speech, and when she stepped back her hands remained on Vaanathi's shoulders, her eyes gone very steady.

"Send word the moment you are able to," Manasa said. "Not details. Not explanations. Only word that you are well. I have buried enough of my own fear this month to have earned that much."

"I will," Vaanathi said again, and this time the words held, steadied by the small unfamiliar weight now settled in her own two hands.

## III

They left before dawn on borrowed horses, Iruli a dark shape wheeling ahead against a sky still holding its last stars, and rode south through country that grew stranger the further they went — the gentle green of Anantapura's own province giving way, by the second hour, to something harder and redder underfoot, laterite stone breaking through the soil in long rust-coloured scars, as though the earth itself had been scraped raw and left to heal badly.

"The soil changes," Vaanathi observed, watching the colour deepen from ordinary brown to something closer to dried blood. "I did not think we had ridden far enough for that."

"You have not," Sancharan said. "This is his doing, or near enough to it. Where his influence runs strongest, the land remembers him in small ways — the colour of the stone, the temper of the weather, the particular restlessness that settles over cattle and men alike on certain nights. It is not magic exactly. It is closer to a long habit the world has fallen into, the way a river cuts the same channel for so many centuries that the water no longer remembers choosing that path."

"And where is he? Beneath a river, you said."

"The Sevvaaru," Sancharan said. "It does not appear on any map your father's surveyors have drawn, though I suspect that is less because it cannot be found and more because it prefers not to be. You will understand what I mean shortly."

They understood within the hour. The river, when they finally reached it, ran through a gorge of the same rust-red stone that had been thickening beneath them all morning, and the water itself carried a faint reddish cast in the low light — not blood, Vaanathi told herself firmly, climbing down from her horse at the gorge's edge, only mineral, only the same iron-rich stone bleeding colour into the current the way it bled colour into everything else here. She told herself this several times before she entirely believed it.

Iruli landed on a sun-warmed boulder and shook out her feathers with visible unease. *I do not care for this place,* she said. *I have never cared for this place. It hums.*

"It has always hummed," Sancharan said. "You have simply never had cause to notice, on the occasions I have come alone."

*I notice everything you refuse to,* Iruli said. *That is, near as I can tell, the whole of my function in this partnership.*

Vaanathi crouched at the water's edge and let it run over her fingers — warm, warmer than a mountain-fed river had any right to be. "Tell me what to expect," she said. "You did not, entirely, this morning."

"Not what you are picturing," Sancharan said, watching her with something careful in his expression. "You have seen what I am, in Anaadi, in your own chambers three nights past — the rings, the vastness none of us can properly hold in words. That is not what walks toward you here. What you meet here will look, near enough, like an ordinary man standing on an ordinary riverbank, the same way I have spent this whole season looking, near enough, like an ordinary scholar." He paused. "None of us ever truly leaves our own sky, Vaanathi. What sits in that far turning dark is the whole of what we are — vast beyond a mortal word for vast, and no more capable of walking this riverbank than your own shadow is capable of walking away from your feet. What you meet is an anchor's echo. A shape we are permitted to wear here, tethered to the stone that keeps it fixed. It is real. It simply is not the whole of him, any more than the man who greeted you across a startled mare was ever the whole of me."

"Then he is here already," Vaanathi said slowly, looking down the gorge with new eyes. "Not something that must be summoned."

"He has likely been here the entire time we have," Sancharan said. "Watching us decide whether we were worth the trouble of being noticed."

***

They found him a little further along the bank, sitting on a flat shelf of the same rust-coloured stone, methodically sharpening a blade that did not look as though it had ever once needed sharpening. He did not look up as they approached, though Vaanathi had the distinct impression he had marked their arrival long before either of them had made a sound.

## IV

He was, as Sancharan had promised, unremarkable to look at in the way only something enormously old ever troubles to be — a broad-shouldered man in plain, travel-worn clothes, skin weathered to the same deep rust as the stone he sat on, hair cropped close and shot through with iron-grey. It was only his eyes that gave him away, restless in a way no ordinary man's ever were, moving constantly across the water, the stone, the two of them, as though stillness itself were a debt he had never quite finished paying off.

"Sancharan." He said the name flatly, without looking up from the blade. "You bring a mortal to my river. You have not done that in longer than I care to remember, and I do not believe I care for the change."

"Lohitan." Sancharan's voice, beside her, had taken on something of the same register she had heard in her own chambers three nights past — older, steadier, carrying weight no ordinary throat should hold. "She is under my protection. I would ask you to extend her the courtesy you extend me, though I am aware that courtesy has historically been in short supply between us."

"Your protection." Lohitan set the blade down at last and looked at Vaanathi properly, and she felt the look land like a physical weight, assessing, unimpressed. "The last time you extended your protection to a mortal, old friend, it did not end in a manner either of us enjoyed discussing afterward. I would have thought you had learned better patience from it."

Something went very still in Sancharan beside her — not the careful stillness she had learned to read as guardedness, but something closer to a wound freshly touched. "That is not why we have come," he said, and his voice had gone tight in a way she had never once heard it go, not in the tower, not in the treasury, not even in the fortress beyond the world. "I have come because your anchor may share the same trouble mine has begun to show. I would know whether you have felt it too."

Lohitan's restless eyes narrowed, considering. "Felt it. Yes. Three moons past, and worse since. Something has been testing the ground near my own stone — careful, patient, the work of a hand that knows precisely what it is disturbing and disturbs it anyway. I assumed, given your own long habit of minding your business and no one else's, that you had not noticed a thing."

"I have noticed a great deal, of late," Sancharan said. "More than I have known what to do with."

"Clearly." Lohitan's gaze flicked back to Vaanathi, weighing her again, and this time something in the weighing shifted — not warmth, not yet, but the particular grudging reappraisal of a man deciding a thing might be more interesting than his first temper had allowed for. "You have a mortal's stubbornness in your spine. I can see it plainly. Most of them flinch the moment I so much as look at them properly. You did not."

"I have had a great deal of practice not flinching," Vaanathi said, "at things considerably closer to home than a river."

Something that might, in a kinder light, have been called a laugh escaped him, short and rough. "I begin to understand the appeal," Lohitan said, to Sancharan rather than to her. "Sharp-tongued and unflinching. Small wonder you have finally remembered how to lose an argument."

"I have not lost anything," Sancharan said, with the particular dignity of a man who both knew and resented that he had.

"You have lost your patience for solitude, which is the argument that matters," Lohitan said. "I will not pretend to be pleased about it. I have watched what solitude spared you, across a great many centuries, and I do not relish watching you set that shield down for a mortal life that will not, however stubborn its spine, outlast a single one of my own restless nights." Something in his tone gentled, fractionally, into concession. "But you did not come for my blessing, and I am not in the habit of giving it regardless. You came for word of my stone. Come. I will show you what has been testing it, and you may draw your own conclusions about whether the same hand has been testing yours."

## V

The stone, when Lohitan led them to it — a slab of the same rust-red rock set into the gorge wall behind a curtain of hanging root, marked, unmistakably, with the same narrow ring and thin dark wedge Vaanathi had traced with her own fingers on a treasury bell, a sky-tower wall, a stepwell's threshold stone — bore a fresh scar along one edge. Not old wear, not centuries of patient weather. A recent scrape, deliberate, careful, the work of something that had wanted to test the stone's give without cracking it outright.

"He is testing all of them," Vaanathi said quietly, tracing the scar without quite touching it, some old caution from the stepwell still governing her hands. "Not searching. Testing. Learning which give and which hold."

"Or learning which are watched, and which have been left to sleep undisturbed long enough that no one would notice a careful hand at work," Sancharan said grimly.

"There is a third possibility neither of you has yet said aloud," Lohitan put in, arms crossed, watching them both with an attention that had sharpened considerably since the stone came into view. "That whoever tests them is not hunting for a weakness to exploit at all. That he is counting them. Making certain, one by one, that all nine still stand where they are meant to stand, before he decides what comes next."

Sancharan went very still. "You think he means to protect them. Not steal them."

"I think," Lohitan said, "that a man erased twice from his own history might have decided the throne's forgetting is not the only danger his family's old charge now faces. I do not excuse what he has done to reach that conclusion. But I have had a great deal of time to consider the shapes fear takes in a patient man, and this has the shape of fear, not greed."

Vaanathi thought of Viyamban's own words, relayed secondhand through everything Sancharan had told her — a lineage of patience, not vengeance — and felt something cold and complicated settle where anger had sat comfortably for weeks. "That does not forgive Smaran. Or what he nearly did to me."

"No," Lohitan agreed, and for the first time his restless eyes went, briefly, entirely still. "It does not. Grief makes poor arithmetic of what a man is owed, and worse arithmetic still of what he is permitted to take in payment. But it may tell you why he has not simply vanished with whatever he has already learned. A man protecting something does not run from it. He stays close, and watches, and grows more dangerous the closer anyone else comes to whatever he has decided he must guard."

The gorge held quiet a long moment, red water murmuring below them, whatever restless pressure had filled the air settling now into something closer to a held breath.

"Thank you," Sancharan said finally. "For the warning, and for the stone."

"Do not thank me yet." Lohitan's gaze moved once more to Vaanathi, and this time, when it landed, something in it had genuinely warmed — not gentleness, precisely, nothing so soft as that, but the particular respect one restless thing extends to another it has decided is kin. "Come back when the stubbornness in your spine has had cause to bend a little further, mortal girl. I find I would not mind the company a second time." He turned back to Sancharan, his voice gentler now than it had arrived. "And you — mind that you tell her the rest of it, before the telling is taken out of your hands the way it so nearly was three nights past. I have watched you lose enough to silence already. I would not watch you lose this too."

He rose, unhurried, and walked back down the bank the way any ordinary man might walk home after a long day's work, until a bend in the gorge took him from view. Nothing about his leaving asked to be noticed. That, Vaanathi thought, watching the empty riverbank where he had sat only a moment before, might be the truest thing she had learned about him all afternoon — that whatever vastness waited for him in some far turning sky, the shape he wore here had learned, across centuries of practice, to leave a room as quietly as it had entered one.

***

They rode back north as the light began its long gold slide toward evening, slower than the ride out had been, neither of them in any particular hurry to close the distance back to a palace that would want explanations neither of them was yet prepared to give.

"He mentioned someone," Vaanathi said, at last, into the quiet. "The last time you extended your protection to a mortal. He said it did not end in a manner either of you enjoyed discussing."

Sancharan did not answer immediately, and when he did, his voice carried a weight she had not heard in it since the fortress. "Not tonight," he said. "I promised you doors, not all of them at once. That one is old, and it does not open easily, even for me."

"I am not asking you to open it tonight," Vaanathi said. "I am only asking you to know that I noticed the shape of it. I do not intend to let it stay a shadow between us forever, Sancharan. But I can be patient about which nights I ask."

Something in him eased at that — not a smile, not quite, but a loosening around the eyes that felt, to her, like the truest thing he had given her all day.

"He liked you," Sancharan said, after a while, with the particular careful wonder of a man reporting something he had not expected to be reporting. "I want that noted. Lohitan does not extend that regard easily, or often, or — in my considerable experience of him — ever, to anyone who was not already old enough to have earned it several times over."

"Perhaps he simply has good taste."

"Perhaps." Something warm moved behind his tired eyes. "Or perhaps he saw, rather more quickly than I managed to, precisely what I was in the process of losing an argument with. I confess I find it easier to forgive him the observation than I would have a week ago."

"You have not lost an argument with me yet, that I recall."

"Have I not?" He glanced at her, something rueful and fond in the look. "I recall arriving in this city with every intention of remaining a careful, distant scholar for the length of a brief errand, and I find myself, three weeks later, riding home from a bad-tempered anchor-keeper's gorge holding a princess's hand and admitting things to her I have not admitted to another living soul in longer than her whole dynasty has existed. I would call that a fairly comprehensive defeat, by any honest accounting."

"I prefer to think of it as a negotiated peace."

"That," Sancharan said, "is a considerably more generous way of putting it than I deserve, and I intend to accept it without argument, before you reconsider."

He reached for her hand again, and this time, when their fingers found each other across the small distance between their horses, neither of them let go for the whole of the long ride home.
