## I

The second bench aged in front of a laundry maid.

She did not scream. To her considerable credit, Sancharan thought afterward, she simply set down her basket. She stared at the stone for the length of three full breaths as it flickered from pale and new to weathered and old, and back again. Then she picked her basket back up and walked very quickly in the opposite direction. It was the way sensible people walk away from things they have decided, for their own peace of mind, not to have seen.

*That is the third one this week,* Iruli said, landing on the courtyard wall with none of her usual theatre. *The bench. The east fountain, which ran backward for the length of a held breath yesterday morning. I watched the water climb rather than fall, and I confess it troubled me considerably more than I let show at the time. And now a witness. A witness who will tell someone, Sancharan, because that is what witnesses do. You cannot charm a laundry maid the way you charm a princess.*

"I am aware."

*Are you? Because you do not look aware. You look like a man calculating how many more days he can go on pretending this is manageable.*

Sancharan did not answer that directly. By now, that was answer enough for both of them.

He found, turning it over in the quiet after she had gone, that he could no longer entirely locate the version of himself who had believed, three weeks ago, that this could be managed at all. That version of himself had believed a scholar's borrowed name and a careful week's patience would be enough to keep the two halves of his life from ever touching. He suspected that version of himself had not accounted for the cost. It had not counted the price of keeping something so large hidden from someone he had come to care for so plainly, so quickly, against every long-practised instinct he owned.

The truth was one he had not yet found the words to give even Iruli in full. The debt was not merely growing. It was widening. What had begun as small, private disturbances tied to his own body, his own tired hands, had begun leaking outward into the world around him. It was the way a held breath eventually has to become a breath taken, whether or not the one holding it has decided they are ready.

*It is not only your debt,* Iruli said, quieter now, watching him with the particular careful attention she reserved for the conversations she suspected he did not want to have. *I flew out past the northern scrubland three nights ago. The stepwell. The ward that took her is not settling back to sleep the way the others have, over the centuries. It is restless. And the tower beyond the wall — I do not like what I felt there either, though I could not have told you precisely why if you asked me to explain it plainly.*

"You think something is waking the anchors."

*I think,* Iruli said, *that more than one door has been disturbed recently. And doors, in my experience, rarely trouble themselves to disturb each other by coincidence.*

Sancharan looked out across the courtyard, toward the wall the laundry maid had vanished behind. He felt, for the first time since he had chosen the name Sancharan and the quiet, ordinary shape of a travelling scholar, a weariness that had nothing to do with centuries. It had everything to do with the particular exhaustion of watching a careful arrangement come apart faster than he could mend it.

"Then I am running out of time to decide how this ends," he said, "on my own terms, rather than someone else's."

*You have been running out of that particular time since the night you carried her through my door,* Iruli said, not unkindly. *I only wonder, old friend, whether you have finally noticed.*

## II

She found him that evening in the small walled garden behind the scribes' hall, where the jasmine had just begun to open against the dark. She settled beside him on the bench without asking permission — something she had stopped doing somewhere in the last fortnight, without either of them remarking on when.

"You are thinking again," Vaanathi said. "Loudly. I could hear it from the gate."

"I did not realise thinking made a sound."

"Yours does. It has a particular quality. I have started to recognise it. It is the one where you are deciding how much of something to give me, and how much to keep for yourself a while longer."

He was quiet a moment. Then, in a voice that had lost some of its usual careful lightness, he said, "There are nine of us."

Vaanathi went very still.

"Not gods," he said, before she could ask, watching the jasmine rather than her, as though the words came easier spoken to a flower than to a face. "Not in the way your court's fear would have it. Not powers to be begged or worshipped. Forces, if you like that word better. Old ones. Each of us was given a measure of this world to guard, long before there was a dynasty here to guard it from. One for the tides. One for the harvests. One for the slow turning of fortune. One for the particular arithmetic mortals came to call karma." He paused. "The mark you have been chasing since Smaran died — it belongs to the anchors we left behind, on Bhumi's own soil, so our influence could be felt without our presence being required. Nine anchors. Nine seals. I do not know how many of the other eight are still where they ought to be."

"Eight," Vaanathi repeated slowly. "Not nine."

"Mine has never left the tower," Sancharan said. "It could not. I am not free to wander so far from the thing I was given to guard, whatever else I may have wandered from in the meantime." Something that was almost a smile crossed his face, tired and rueful. "The others are scattered further. One rests, I believe, beneath a river far south of here, guarded by a lord whose temper runs as hot as the season he governs. I have not always found him easy company, though I will admit the fault was rarely entirely his. Another sits somewhere in the high, cold passes to the north, kept by a lady whose patience makes mine look reckless by comparison. She has not spoken a word to any of us in three centuries, and I do not entirely blame her. A third moves, or so I last heard, rather than staying still at all. It is carried from court to court by a keeper who trusts no single roof to guard it long enough. I have not seen most of them in longer than your empire has existed. We do not, as a rule, gather. It is safer that way. For Bhumi, if not always for us."

"You speak of them as though they were family you have fallen out with."

"That is not far from true," Sancharan admitted. "We are bound by the same accord, the same purpose, and precious little else. Some of us govern with a gentler hand than others. Some have grown careless, over the centuries, in ways that frighten me rather more than I let myself dwell on. I do not think you would enjoy meeting most of them. I confess I am not eager for the introduction to happen any sooner than it must."

Vaanathi absorbed this in the particular still, careful way she absorbed everything that mattered now. She filed it in the locked room of her own mind, alongside the mark and the letters and every other truth this strange season had handed her. "And whoever visited Ratnan — this 'old family friend' none of us can place — you believe he is protecting one of these anchors. Or hunting one."

"I believe," Sancharan said carefully, "that his family was entrusted with a portion of that guardianship, long ago, and that the guardianship was taken from them unjustly. I do not think that makes what he has done forgivable. I think it makes it considerably sadder than a simple villain's cruelty would be. I am not certain, some nights, that I know how to hold both of those truths steadily in the same hand."

"You are defending him."

"I am understanding him," Sancharan said. "It is not the same thing, though I confess the two feel uncomfortably close together some evenings." He looked at her then, properly, and something in his face had gone unusually open, unusually undefended. "I have told you more tonight than I have told any mortal in longer than I can comfortably measure. I do not yet know if that was wise."

"It was honest," Vaanathi said. "I find I have stopped requiring wise, from you. Honest will do."

## III

Viyamban composed the letter with the same patient, meticulous care he brought to everything. He sat alone in his modest quarters by a single lamp, a length of treated seelai smoothed flat before him.

He had chosen cloth over leaf deliberately. A stylus carved into olai in strokes too practised, too personal, to forge convincingly. Every scribe's hand fell into its own particular rhythm of pressure and angle — the kind of thing a careful reader's eye caught, even when they could not have said what exactly looked wrong. Ink on treated cloth was more forgiving. He had spent two evenings teaching his own hand to unlearn the square, economical strokes a lifetime of carving had trained into it. He coaxed his letters into the rounder, looser shapes Ratnan's own ink favoured, until the forgery sat close enough to the boy's true hand that only Ratnan himself would ever have caught the difference. And Ratnan would not be the one reading it.

He wrote it in that borrowed hand, in Ratnan's careful phrasing, learned from the boy's own written record: *I did not tell you everything. There is more I remember about that night. Come to me before you tell your father anything further. I do not trust the walls of my own room to keep this quiet any longer.*

It was, he reflected, turning the cloth once more in the lamplight to be certain the old preparation had dried invisibly into the weave, indistinguishable from the ink around it, a crueler instrument than the one he had used on Smaran. Smaran had died by his own diligence, drawn to a danger he had gone looking for. This one would not require the princess to go looking for anything at all. She would simply do what any loyal, frightened young woman would do for a friend who claimed to be in danger. She would open it alone, and hold it close enough to read by candlelight. The old Siddha preparation worked into the fibre, patient and undetectable to any eye or nose, would do the rest, quietly, the way it always did.

He did not enjoy this part of the work. He had never enjoyed it, not with Smaran, and he did not expect to enjoy it now. But the record existed beyond his reach. The princess was the one person in this palace with both the authority and the recklessness to keep chasing this thread all the way to its end. And Viyamban had spent decades learning that patience, unused past its hour, was only ever a slower way of losing.

*Forgive me,* he thought, sealing the cloth into its plain, unremarkable fold, in a voice he did not allow himself to speak aloud even alone. *I do not do this for cruelty. I do it because I have already buried my name once, and I find, in the end, I am not brave enough to bury it twice.*

## IV

The cloth reached Vaanathi's chambers an hour before midnight, delivered by a page who could not later recall, however carefully he was questioned, who exactly had asked him to carry it.

She read it twice by candlelight, frowning, already composing in her mind the quiet, careful way she would slip out to Ratnan's quarters without waking Nila. She did not notice, in the ordinary business of an ordinary evening's correspondence, the cloth growing warm in her hand — the way fabric left too near a flame grows warm, except that her candle sat a full arm's length away and had no business warming anything at all.

The first symptom was a coldness at the very centre of her chest, small and specific, as though someone had pressed a single fingertip of winter directly against her heart.

She had time to think, with the particular clarity of a mind that has read one mystery too many not to recognise its own shape when it finally arrives, *this is what happened to Smaran*. Then the cold spread outward fast enough that thinking, of any kind, became considerably more difficult to manage. The cloth slipped from her fingers. Her own breath, when she tried to draw it, came shallow and strange, as though the air in the room had thinned all at once. The last coherent thought she managed to hold onto, before the room began to tilt beneath her, was a small, absurd flare of anger at herself for not noticing sooner. She had read a letter twice and never once asked why a boy who wrote in the soft, rounded flow of a lifetime spent with ink had suddenly begun forming his letters in the square, deliberate strokes of a hand more accustomed to carving.

She did not remember calling out. She would learn later that she had. Nila had heard it through the connecting wall and come running. A steward had been sent for the court physician before anyone quite understood there was nothing a court physician could do. What she remembered, in the fragments that stayed with her afterward, was the floor coming up to meet her. And a voice that was not quite Sancharan's voice — older, vaster, carrying a weight no human throat had ever been built to hold — saying her name from a distance that closed impossibly fast.

He was there before she finished falling.

His hands found her, and where they touched her the terrible cold did not retreat so much as it *paused*. It was held at bay the way a held breath holds back a wave. Dimly, from somewhere very far outside her own failing body, Vaanathi became aware that the room had gone strange around her. The candle flame stood motionless, a single unmoving tongue of gold. Dust hung suspended in the air, caught mid-fall, glittering and still. Nila, in the doorway, had gone to stone — one foot lifted, mouth open on the beginning of a word that would not finish arriving for a very long time.

Only Sancharan moved. Only Sancharan, and, distantly, some small failing part of herself he was refusing to let go.

"Stay with me," he said. His voice, close now, had steadied back into something almost like itself, though beneath it she could still hear the older thing — patient and vast, holding the whole frozen room in its hand the way one holds a held breath. "I cannot take you to Anaadi in time. There is no time left for that door. So I am bringing a little of its patience here instead, to you. It will cost what it costs. And I find, entirely to my own surprise, that I no longer care what that cost turns out to be."

Light gathered at his hands. It was not fire, not the ordinary gold of any lamp she had ever known, but something pale and vast and endlessly turning — rings within rings within rings, the same shape she had glimpsed once before in a fortress beyond the world. She had promised herself, in the clear light of the morning after, that she had perhaps imagined it. She had not imagined it.

The rings did not simply hover near his hands this time. They came closer than they had ever come before, closer than they had come even to threaten her in Anaadi's great hall. Where they passed near her skin she felt something that was not quite warmth and not quite pressure — a kind of *recognition*, as though whatever old law lived inside their slow turning had looked at her once, weighed her again, and this time, because he asked it of them, chosen gentleness instead of judgment.

"The poison does not need removing," he said, low, close enough that she felt the words as much as heard them, his voice steadying by degrees even as the vaster thing beneath it continued to hold the room. "It needs *time*. That is all any poison ever needs to finish its work. Enough of it, uninterrupted, to complete what it began. So I am taking the time away from it instead. Every hour it would have spent killing you, I am going to spend somewhere else, somewhere it cannot follow. And I am going to bring you with me while I do it."

She did not understand him, not with the ordinary understanding of a mind still tethered to an ordinary body. But something in her understood him anyway. Some part of her had, perhaps, never entirely left the strange endless dark of Anaadi's sky since the night he first carried her through its door. She understood him the way a held breath understands the wave it is bracing against, long before the wave arrives.

The rings closed the last small distance and settled around her. Not around her wrists, not around her throat, nowhere so crude as a binding, but around the whole shape of her — the way water closes around a stone dropped gently rather than thrown. And where they closed, the world came apart at its seams.

She did not fall. She did not float. She simply *stopped being only where her body was*, and found herself, in the same breath, everywhere the rings had ever turned.

She saw — or felt, or remembered, though none of those words sat quite correctly over what was happening to her — a vastness she had no training to hold steady in her mind. Wheels within wheels, turning at paces too different from one another to belong to the same single moment. And yet somehow all of them were the same moment, folded over each other the way a river folds over its own eddies without ever ceasing to be one river. She understood, in that strange instant, that what mortals called *an hour* was not one thing at all, but a compromise — a single agreed-upon average, smoothed flat across a thousand quicker or slower hours that never quite matched each other. It was the way a court smooths a thousand small disagreements into a single official record. She saw, without quite seeing anything so simple as a picture, the particular hour a mountain keeps, running always a fraction ahead of the hour kept in the valley beneath it. She saw a swifter hour still, thin and bright, belonging to something that moved too fast for any mortal foot to have earned it. And she saw, at the centre of all of it, patient beyond any word she owned for patience, the hour Sancharan himself kept. It was vast and slow, gathering weight the way a held breath gathers stillness — an hour that had been turning since before there were rivers to fold, before there were valleys to lie beneath any mountain at all.

*This is what I am,* his voice said, from everywhere and nowhere, gentler than she had ever heard it. *Not a man who has lived a long time. A weight the world's own hours bend around, the way light bends around anything heavy enough to ask it to. I have been lonely inside that weight for longer than your dynasty has had a name. I did not know, until you, how much I had stopped expecting anyone to be permitted close enough to see it.*

She wanted, even now, even here, to understand it properly. Some stubborn part of her had spent a whole season refusing to accept a mystery without first taking it apart to see how it was made. *How,* she thought back at him, or tried to, the way one tries to shape a word with a mouth one can no longer entirely locate. *How does taking me here save me there?*

*Because there is no "here" and "there,"* he answered. She felt, rather than heard, something almost like patience in the shape of it — the particular patience of someone explaining a truth they had known so long they had forgotten it needed explaining at all. *There is only ever one river, folding over itself. The poison in your blood is a current in that river, moving at the pace poison moves — steady, hungry, certain of where it is going. I have not stopped that current. Nothing stops a current; that is not a thing that can be asked of the world. I have only carried you sideways, out of its particular fold, into a slower one, where a single one of its hungry certain moments takes so long to arrive that your own body has time to close every door it would have opened. When I set you back down into the ordinary fold, the current will still be there. But it will have already spent itself, uselessly, against all the time I gave you to be somewhere it could not follow.*

*That sounds like a very large kindness to spend on one archivist's granddaughter's friend,* she thought. She felt, distinctly, something that might have been laughter move through the turning wheels around her — not mocking, only surprised, as though she had managed to startle something that had long since stopped expecting to be startled.

*It is not a kindness,* he said. *A kindness would cost me nothing. This costs a great deal. And I find, entirely to my own bewilderment, that I would spend a great deal more of it, and gladly, if it kept you exactly this stubborn for exactly as long as the world allows either of us to be anything at all.*

She reached for him. Not with her hands, which she could no longer entirely locate, but with whatever part of her was doing the seeing — the way one reaches instinctively for a hand in the dark without first deciding to. Astonishingly, impossibly, she found him there to be reached for. Not the careful scholar's face, not even the vast patient weight his voice had just described, but something truer than either. It felt, when her searching, formless self finally touched it, less like meeting a stranger and more like remembering a room she had somehow always known the shape of.

It was not a touch of skin. It was nearer than skin had ever let two people be. She felt, in that formless meeting, the particular ache he carried at the centre of all that patience. It was the loneliness of a creature who watched empires rise and fall the way mortals watched seasons turn, who had loved, she understood now, more than once across his impossible span of years, and had buried every one of those loves beneath the same slow, careful weight that was, even now, holding her poisoned heart together thread by thread. She felt him flinch, faintly, at how plainly she saw it. And she felt, just as plainly, his choice not to hide it from her anyway.

*You do not have to carry that alone either,* she thought back at him, with whatever was left of her that could still be called thinking. She felt something in the vast turning wheels around her shift — not slow, exactly, because slowness had stopped meaning anything sensible in this place, but *settle*, the way a held breath finally settles into an ordinary one.

She saw, in the last of it, before the wheels began drawing back toward the shape of an ordinary room, a glimpse — brief and searing and gone before she could hold it properly — of a shore she did not recognise. Waves the wrong colour broke under a sky full of the same patient, knowing lights she had seen scattered through Anaadi's dark. She saw, or thought she saw, a figure standing at that shore's edge, indistinct, turning as if to look back over an ocean too vast for any mortal vessel. Then it was gone, folded back into the turning wheels before she could ask whose memory she had just been permitted to borrow.

Slowly, the vastness narrowed. The wheels drew back into rings. The rings drew back into light. The light gathered once more at his hands and dimmed by careful degrees, the way a fire banked rather than extinguished. Vaanathi found her body again the way one finds a familiar coat after a long day in someone else's clothes — a little strange at first, a little too small for what she had just been, and then, breath by breath, entirely her own again.

The cold in her chest was gone. Not driven out, she understood now, but simply *outrun* — left behind in an hour that had finished happening to a body that was, for the space of that borrowed vastness, no longer quite bound to it.

The room began, by degrees, to move again. The candle flame resumed its small ordinary dance. Dust finished its interrupted fall. Nila's foot came down, her mouth closed on a word she would never quite manage to say. Somewhere beyond the door, faint and ordinary, the sound of the court physician's hurrying footsteps finally arrived — too late to matter, though neither Vaanathi nor Sancharan would ever trouble to tell him so.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Vaanathi understood that something had changed between them that had nothing to do with the poison at all. Whatever she had just touched, in that formless place where his loneliness had let itself be seen, could not be untouched now — any more than a hand could unknow the shape of a room it had learned entirely in the dark. She had spent this whole strange season cataloguing him. A stillness here, a silence there, an evasion filed carefully away for later. She understood now, lying on the floor of her own chambers with her heart beating her own ordinary rhythm again, that cataloguing had been the wrong instrument entirely. You did not catalogue a person you had briefly *been*. You only, ever afterward, recognised them.

She thought of the shore she had glimpsed, the wrong-coloured waves, the indistinct figure turned away from her toward an ocean too large for any word she owned. She did not ask him about it, not yet. Some instinct told her that whatever grief lived in that borrowed memory belonged to an hour he was not yet ready to hand her whole — the way she had once not been ready to hand her own grief for Smaran to a stranger in a courtyard. But she found she wanted it anyway. She wanted all of it, every century of loneliness he had just let her feel the weight of for the length of a single formless breath. It was not out of pity, and not even, entirely, out of the fierce new tenderness she could feel taking root somewhere beneath her still-unsteady ribs. It was because she understood, with a certainty that felt older than the last hour, that she had never in her life wanted to know another person so completely, cost and all.

"You are looking at me differently," Sancharan said quietly, watching her face with a wariness that had nothing to do with the exhaustion still visible in every line of him.

"I am looking at you the way you looked at me," Vaanathi said, "in that place with no proper hours. I did not know it was possible to be *known* like that. I am not certain I have entirely forgiven you for showing me. And I am not certain I want to be forgiven for not minding it nearly as much as I ought to."

Something in his face moved — the last of the old careful armour, worn thin by centuries and worn thinner still by whatever they had just shared in a space too vast for either of their ordinary bodies to properly hold. It did not immediately settle back into its usual guardedness.

Vaanathi opened her eyes fully to find Sancharan kneeling over her, hollowed out with an exhaustion she had never once seen on him. Not in the tower, not in the alcove, not even in Anaadi's own strange endless dark. She understood, with a clarity that arrived far ahead of anything so ordinary as fear, that whatever question she had spent this whole strange season not quite asking him plainly, she was not going to let him deflect it a moment longer.

"Tell me," she said, her voice raw, barely more than breath. "Now. All of it. No more doors, Sancharan. No more locks I have to find on my own."

He looked at her a long moment. Something in his face — the last of the old careful armour, worn thin by centuries and worn thinner still by tonight — finally, visibly, gave way.

"My name is not Sancharan," he said. "It never was. I am the ninth anchor, the one that never left its tower, the one your court has spent generations fearing under a word they were too frightened to say plainly." He drew a breath that did not, she noticed distantly, seem entirely necessary to him — the way a man draws breath before a truth rather than for the air itself. "I am Saturn. Lord of time, and of the slow, unwelcome arithmetic of consequence. I have been feared under that name for longer than your empire has had a throne to fear from. I have watched you, this whole strange season, look at me like I might simply be a man. And I find I have run entirely out of courage to keep letting you believe it."

Vaanathi lay very still on the floor of her own chambers, the last of the cold finally, fully gone from her chest, and looked up at him. At this man. At this vast old patient thing wearing a man's tired face. Beneath the shock, beneath the enormous impossible size of what he had just handed her, she felt something that surprised her considerably more than the revelation itself.

She felt, absurdly, relieved.

"Well," she said, when she had finally found enough breath to say anything at all. "That does explain the bench."
